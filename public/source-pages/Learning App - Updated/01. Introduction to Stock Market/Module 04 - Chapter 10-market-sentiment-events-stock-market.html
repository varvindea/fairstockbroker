The Fear-Greed Cycle — Why Psychology Moves Markets

Markets are not purely rational. They are collections of millions of human decisions — each influenced by fear, greed, hope, regret, and social pressure. Understanding these emotional cycles is as important as understanding earnings or GDP.

Fear-Driven · Indiscriminate · Creates Best Buying Opportunities
Panic Selling — When Fear Overrides Logic
Panic selling occurs when investors, overwhelmed by fear, sell stocks indiscriminately — regardless of the underlying business quality. They're not selling because the company became worse; they're selling because they're terrified of losing more money. This irrational behaviour creates the deepest buying opportunities in markets — COVID crash, 2008 GFC, Kargil war — every panic was followed by the most explosive recoveries. The hard truth: panic selling almost always happens at the worst possible time, locking in losses that patient investors avoid completely.

How a Panic Selling Episode Unfolds
1
⚡ Trigger Event Hits
A shocking news event — pandemic, war, bank failure, political shock. The news is unexpected and the magnitude is unknown. Fear begins to spread. Selling starts in global markets overnight before India opens.
2
📉 Gap Down Opening — Cascading Sell Orders
Nifty opens 3–8% lower. Stop-loss orders trigger automatically — forced selling that has nothing to do with individual stock quality. Mutual funds sell to meet redemptions. Margin calls force leveraged investors to sell. The selling feeds on itself.
3
😱 Maximum Fear — "Never Investing Again" Phase
News headlines scream catastrophe. Social media is full of loss posts and predictions of total collapse. This is statistically close to the market bottom — peak fear and peak selling coincide. Retail investors capitulate and sell — institutions begin quietly buying.
4
✅ Stabilisation — Smart Money Enters Quietly
Selling exhaustion sets in. Institutional investors, FIIs, and long-term investors begin accumulating quality stocks at panic prices. Volume drops. Market stops making new lows even on bad news — a classic technical bottom signal.
5
🎉 V-Shaped Recovery — Panic Sellers Regret
Recovery is often as fast as the crash — or faster. Investors who sold at the bottom now face the painful choice of buying back at higher prices or missing the recovery. COVID example: Nifty at 7,500 (Mar 2020) → 18,000 (Dec 2020) in 9 months. Those who sold at 7,500 needed Nifty at 7,500 to break even — it never returned there.

Fear Of Missing Out · Greed-Driven · Late-Stage Buyers Always Lose
FOMO Buying — Chasing Returns Is the Most Expensive Habit
FOMO (Fear of Missing Out) is the opposite of panic — it's the urge to buy because everyone else seems to be making money and you're missing the party. FOMO investors typically enter at the absolute worst time — near the peak, after the stock has already risen 100–200%, right before the correction that gives back all those gains. The classic FOMO pattern: relative buys stocks after hearing friends made money → stock corrects → they hold hoping for recovery → more correction → panic sell at a loss. FOMO-driven buying creates the same losses as panic selling, just from the other direction.

The FOMO Pattern — Recognise Before You're In It
1
📈 Stock rises 30–50% — News starts
Good fundamentals drive initial move. Only informed, patient investors are in. Media coverage begins. Social media posts appear. Your friend casually mentions a "great stock."
2
🚀 Stock at +80–100% — FOMO begins spreading
This is where most retail investors first hear about the stock. News articles appear. YouTube videos. "Expert" commentary. Social media posts with screenshots of profits. The urge to "not miss out" begins. The stock is already up 100% from value investors' entry.
3
🤑 Stock at +150–200% — Mass FOMO buying
Everyone's talking about it. Mutual fund managers who missed it start buying to "not look silly" (institutional FOMO is real). The stock is now priced for perfection — any disappointment will cause a severe correction. But buying continues because "momentum" seems unstoppable.
4
💥 Correction begins — FOMO buyers panic
A minor negative — slightly lower guidance, a new competitor, or simply the stock being "expensive" — triggers selling. Smart money that bought early takes profits. FOMO buyers are now underwater. They hold hoping for recovery but the stock continues falling.
5
😔 −30–50% correction — FOMO buyers capitulate
Unable to take the pain, FOMO buyers finally sell at a loss — exactly when patient investors are considering buying again. The FOMO cycle creates a double loss: buying near the top AND selling near the next bottom.

How to Avoid FOMO
Rules to Protect Yourself
The 3 FOMO questions:
1. Why do I want to buy this stock today?
2. Would I have bought it 6 months ago at half the price?
3. Is my reason "because others are making money"?

If your answer to #3 is yes — don't buy. You're experiencing FOMO, not making an investment decision. The best antidote is a disciplined SIP that removes emotion from timing entirely.

Speculative Rally — When Story Replaces Substance
A speculative rally occurs when stocks rise not because of actual earnings or growth — but because of an exciting narrative or theme. The narrative could be true (EVs will replace petrol cars) but the stock price gets so far ahead of reality (100× future earnings) that it becomes unsustainable. Speculative rallies in India: the EV theme (2020–21), the defence indigenisation theme (2022–23), the railway/infra theme (2023–24), and small/mid-cap bubbles. The pattern is always the same — a real theme gets extrapolated to impossible valuations, creating a bubble that eventually corrects back to fundamental value.

How to Tell Theme from Speculation
Fundamental Test vs Story Test
Fundamental test: At this price, how many years of perfect 30% earnings growth does the stock require to justify the valuation? If the answer is 15+ years — it's speculation, not investment.

Story test: Can you explain WHY this stock is worth its current price using actual numbers — not "huge opportunity" or "India's infrastructure boom"? If you can only tell a story, not show the numbers, you're speculating.

Unverified · Two-Way Risk · "Buy the Rumour, Sell the News"
Rumours — The Two-Edged Sword of Market Information
Rumours are unverified pieces of information that circulate in markets — about M&A deals, management changes, regulatory decisions, government contracts, or company results. Rumours cause immediate stock price reactions before any official confirmation, because markets price probabilities not certainties. The famous market saying "buy the rumour, sell the news" captures a real pattern: stocks often rise on a rumour and then fall when the news is officially confirmed — because the good news was already priced in. Acting on rumours without verification is also SEBI-prohibited insider trading if you have actual material non-public information.

How Rumours Move Stocks — Both Directions
1
🗣️ Rumour Emerges — Stock Moves Immediately
A WhatsApp forward, Twitter post, or "market source" claims: "Company X is being acquired by Company Y at 40% premium." Stock X immediately rallies 10–15% as traders buy on the possibility. This price movement happens before any official confirmation.
2
✅ Rumour True → "Sell the News"
If the rumour is confirmed, the stock often falls back as traders who bought on the rumour sell into the official announcement — locking in their gains. "Buy the rumour, sell the news" — the confirmation removes the upside optionality, so smart traders exit.
3
❌ Rumour False → Immediate Reversal
If the company officially denies the rumour (required under SEBI listing obligations), the stock falls back — sometimes below its pre-rumour price as disappointed buyers exit. The investor who bought on the rumour suffers a double loss: bought at the elevated rumour price, sold below the pre-rumour price.
4
⚖️ Legal Risk — SEBI's Insider Trading Rules
Buying a stock on actual material non-public information (even if received as a "rumour") is insider trading — a criminal offence under SEBI regulations. "I heard it on WhatsApp" is not a valid legal defence if the information is later proven material and non-public. SEBI investigates unusual trading volumes before major announcements regularly.

How to Handle Rumours Safely
The 5 Rules for Rumour Management
1. Never act on an unverified WhatsApp/Telegram "tip" — this is both risky and potentially illegal
2. Check BSE/NSE exchange filings — all material info must be disclosed here first
3. If you're already holding a stock with a positive rumour, reassess whether your original thesis still holds — not whether the rumour is true
4. Don't chase stocks already risen on rumours
5. Assume you have last information — the person spreading the rumour bought before you

04 — Protect Yourself

6 Rules for Sentiment-Proof Investing

Knowing about sentiment events is only half the battle — the other half is having the systems and rules to prevent your own emotions from making costly decisions. Here are six practical protections:

📋
Rule 1
Write Down Your Investment Thesis Before Buying
Before buying any stock, write down: why you're buying, what price makes it cheap, and what would make you sell. When panic hits, re-read this before acting. If the facts haven't changed — don't sell. If the facts have changed — sell based on logic, not fear. A written thesis is your emotional anchor during volatility.
🔄
Rule 2
SIP Removes the Timing Problem Entirely
A Systematic Investment Plan (SIP) in mutual funds or ETFs invests a fixed amount every month — regardless of market level. It automatically buys more units when prices are low (panic) and fewer when prices are high (FOMO). SIP makes market volatility work for you instead of against you. The emotion is removed by design.
📵
Rule 3
Silence the Noise During Market Panics
During panic events (−5%+ market drop), reduce your news consumption to once daily maximum. Remove stock apps from your home screen temporarily. The urge to check every 5 minutes during a crash is driven by fear — and fear drives bad decisions. The best action during a panic is often no action. Check your portfolio weekly, not hourly.
🧮
Rule 4
Value the Business — Not the Stock Price
When a stock falls 30%, ask: did the company's earnings fall 30%? Did management change? Did the competitive position worsen? If none of these changed — the stock is just cheaper. This is the Warren Buffett approach: stocks are ownership in businesses, not lottery tickets. If the business is fine, the price drop is a sale, not a disaster.
🚫
Rule 5
Never Act on WhatsApp/Telegram Stock Tips
Every stock tip in an unverified group benefits the person who sent it — not you. They bought before telling you. Always check: (1) Is this person SEBI-registered? (2) Do they hold the stock? (3) Is there an exchange filing confirming this? If not — ignore completely. SEBI-registered research analysts must disclose stock holdings and are legally accountable.
📅
Rule 6
Rebalance Annually — Not Based on Sentiment
Set your target allocation (eg. 70% equity, 20% debt, 10% gold) and rebalance once a year — not every time market sentiment changes. Rebalancing forces you to sell what's expensive (euphoria) and buy what's cheap (panic) systematically. This contrarian action — sell high, buy low — is exactly the opposite of what sentiment drives most investors to do.

Speculative rallies are always obvious in hindsight — the hard part is resisting in real-time. When PE ratios hit 80–100× for non-tech companies, the market is pricing in 15–20 years of perfect execution. Any disappointment causes catastrophic corrections. The valuation discipline rule: if you can't justify the price with realistic 5-year earnings projections, don't buy regardless of how exciting the narrative sounds.
📱
Social media has democratised investing — but also democratised misinformation. India's 10Cr+ retail investors are both a strength and a vulnerability for markets. Follow only SEBI-registered research analysts. Any "tip" from an unregistered source benefits them, not you. The Telegram group admin bought before sending you the message. SEBI investigates unusual pre-announcement trading — don't be caught in that net.
🗣️
Rumours create short-term volatility but fundamental value always wins long-term. "Buy the rumour, sell the news" — but only if you caught the rumour early. Late-stage rumour buying is the same as FOMO. For every confirmed rumour that made money, there are 5 false rumours that lost money. Invest in businesses whose earnings growth you can predict — not in news flow you can't control.
