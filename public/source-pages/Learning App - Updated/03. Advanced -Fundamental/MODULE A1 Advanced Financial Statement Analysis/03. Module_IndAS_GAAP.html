01 — Key Learning Outcome

Why Accounting Standards Change Everything You See

📚
Your Core Takeaway
Accounting standards are the rules that determine how economic events — signing a lease, completing a service contract, holding a bond — are translated into the numbers on a financial statement. Different rules produce different numbers from identical business transactions. An analyst who doesn't understand the standard is reading a translation without knowing the language.
Imagine two identical retail stores, side by side on the same street, both paying ₹2 crore per year to lease their space for 10 years. Under old Ind AS (before 2019), both reported zero debt related to the lease — it was "off balance sheet." Under Ind AS 116 (mandatory from April 2019), both must now recognise a "right-of-use asset" (₹16 crore) and a corresponding "lease liability" (₹16 crore) on their balance sheets. The business hasn't changed. The cash flows haven't changed. But suddenly the balance sheet shows ₹16 crore more debt, the EBITDA improves (rent expense is replaced by depreciation + interest, and depreciation is excluded from EBITDA), and the net profit is lower in early years due to front-loaded interest expense.

This is not a trivial change. For IndiGo airlines — which leases its entire fleet — the Ind AS 116 adoption added over ₹30,000 crore in "lease liabilities" to the balance sheet overnight. Analysts comparing IndiGo pre and post-2019 without adjusting for this standard change will draw completely wrong conclusions about debt levels.

Similarly, Ind AS 115 (Revenue Recognition, the Indian version of IFRS 15) changed how IT companies, telecom firms, and real estate developers recognise revenue. Ind AS 109 (Financial Instruments) changed how banks, NBFCs, and companies holding bonds value their financial assets — introducing Expected Credit Loss (ECL) provisioning that substantially increases loan loss provisions. This chapter teaches you what each standard does, why it matters for investors, and how to identify when a company's numbers are materially affected by standard changes rather than underlying business changes.

02 — Three Standards That Reshape Indian Financial Statements

Ind AS 116, 115 & 109 — What Changed and Why It Matters

These three Ind AS standards — all mandatory for Indian listed companies since 2019 — represent the biggest transformation in Indian corporate accounting since the Companies Act 2013. Every analyst must understand them to avoid misreading financial statements.

The same company can show wildly different debt levels, EBITDA, and profit depending on which accounting standard applies. The business is unchanged — only the translation rules differ. The analyst's job is to understand the rules well enough to see through to the economic reality underneath.

🏢
Standard #1 — Mandatory from April 2019
Ind AS 116 — Leases
Brings virtually all lease commitments onto the balance sheet as Right-of-Use (ROU) Assets and Lease Liabilities — transforming how retail, aviation, telecom, and hospitality companies look on paper.
🏢 Sectors most affected: Airlines · Retail · Telecom · Hotels · QSR Chains
Before Ind AS 116: a company leasing 200 store locations for ₹5 crore per year each reported only ₹100 crore of rent expense in the P&L. No debt on the balance sheet. No asset. No liability. The obligation to pay ₹1,000 crore over 10 years was invisible — disclosed only in tiny footnotes as "operating lease commitments."

After Ind AS 116: the company must calculate the present value of all future lease payments, recognise a "Right-of-Use Asset" (roughly ₹800 crore after discounting) and a matching "Lease Liability" (₹800 crore). The rent expense disappears from operating costs and is replaced by depreciation on the ROU asset (₹80 crore/yr) and interest on the lease liability (declining each year). The net effect:

• Debt appears higher — ₹800 Cr added to liabilities overnight
• EBITDA improves — depreciation and interest are excluded from EBITDA; the old rent expense was included
• Early-year PAT falls — interest is front-loaded; total cost is higher in early years
• Later-year PAT improves — as interest component declines over the lease term
• D/E ratios blow up — especially for asset-light businesses that relied on leasing precisely to avoid balance-sheet debt
🚫 Old Ind AS Treatment (Pre-2019)
Operating leases: only rent expense in P&L (₹5 Cr/store/yr)
No asset or liability on balance sheet
Commitments buried in Notes to Accounts — page 180 of annual report
Debt/Equity ratio artificially low for lease-heavy businesses
EBITDA margin understated (rent reduces EBITDA)
✅ Ind AS 116 Treatment (Current)
ROU Asset on balance sheet (PV of all lease payments)
Lease Liability on balance sheet (same amount)
P&L: depreciation (ROU asset) + interest (lease liability) — no rent
EBITDA improved — depreciation excluded from EBITDA calc
Net debt increases; D/E ratios jump; leverage ratios worsened
📊 Impact on Key Financial Ratios — Before vs After Ind AS 116
📈
Debt / Equity Ratio
Jumps significantly. An airline like IndiGo added ₹30,000+ Cr in lease liabilities. Comparisons pre/post-2019 are meaningless without adjustment.
💹
EBITDA & EBITDA Margin
Improves — rent moved off operating costs. Retail companies may show 300–500 bps EBITDA margin improvement purely from standard change, with no business change.
💰
Net Profit (PAT)
Typically lower in early lease years (front-loaded interest). Higher in later years. Long-term cumulative PAT impact is small — same cash flows, just different timing.
✅ How to Adjust for Ind AS 116 on Screener.in — Investor's Guide
1
Find ROU Asset: Balance Sheet → Non-current assets → "Right of Use Assets." If this line is large relative to total assets for a retail or airline company — Ind AS 116 is material. Note the ROU Asset value.
2
Find Lease Liability: Balance Sheet → Current + Non-current liabilities → "Lease Liabilities." For a fair debt comparison pre/post-2019, subtract lease liabilities from total debt. This gives you "adjusted net debt" comparable to pre-2019 figures.
3
Adjust EBITDA: Cash Flow statement → "Lease payments" (principal + interest component). Add this back to EBITDA to get a pre-Ind AS 116 comparable EBITDA — useful for comparing with international peers who may still be on different standards.
4
Check Annual Report Note on Leases: Notes to accounts → "Leases" note. This shows the undiscounted future lease commitments, weighted average discount rate used, and maturity profile. These inputs let you verify the company hasn't used an unrealistically high discount rate to understate the lease liability.

Standard #2 — Mandatory from April 2018
Ind AS 115 — Revenue from Contracts with Customers
Replaces all previous revenue recognition standards with a unified 5-step model. Revenue is recognised only when (or as) performance obligations are satisfied — fundamentally changing timing for IT services, telecom, real estate, and subscription businesses.
📋 Sectors most affected: IT Services · Real Estate · Telecom · Construction · SaaS
The old approach to revenue recognition was fragmented — different rules for different industries, creating inconsistency and manipulation opportunity. Ind AS 115 replaced everything with a single 5-step model applicable to all contracts:

Step 1: Identify the contract with a customer (written, verbal, or implied)
Step 2: Identify the performance obligations (what has been promised)
Step 3: Determine the transaction price (variable consideration must be estimated)
Step 4: Allocate the transaction price to performance obligations
Step 5: Recognise revenue when (or as) each performance obligation is satisfied

The practical impact: a software company that sells a ₹12 crore 3-year enterprise software license + 3 years of maintenance can no longer book all ₹12 crore upfront. It must allocate the price between the license and maintenance elements and recognise maintenance revenue over 3 years. An IT company that receives a ₹50 crore contract for a large system implementation must identify each milestone as a separate performance obligation and recognise revenue only as milestones are completed — not on invoicing.
🚫 Old Revenue Treatment (Pre-2018)
Software license: entire amount booked on delivery (even multi-year)
Long-term contracts: percentage-of-completion or completion method — management discretion
Bundled products/services: difficult to separate; recognised together
Variable consideration (rebates, discounts): often not estimated upfront
Different sectors followed different standards — hard to compare across industries
✅ Ind AS 115 Treatment (Current)
Each performance obligation identified and priced separately — no bundling
Revenue only when obligation is "satisfied" — either at a point in time or over time
Variable consideration (discounts, volume rebates) must be estimated using expected value or most likely amount
Contract assets (recognised but not billed) and liabilities (billed but not earned) disclosed
Single consistent model across all sectors — better comparability
📊 5-Step Model Applied — Real Example: IT Project Contract
📝
Steps 1–2: Identify
₹60 Cr IT contract with 4 milestones: system design (₹10 Cr), build (₹20 Cr), testing (₹15 Cr), go-live (₹15 Cr). Each milestone = separate performance obligation.
💱
Steps 3–4: Price Allocation
Transaction price ₹60 Cr allocated based on standalone selling prices of each milestone. If go-live is more complex, it gets a higher allocation than its stated value.
✅
Step 5: Recognise
Revenue booked only as each milestone is completed and accepted. If client delays sign-off on testing, ₹15 Cr stays in Contract Assets (not yet revenue) even if costs are incurred.
✅ Ind AS 115 Signals to Check on Screener.in + Annual Report
1
Contract Assets vs Contract Liabilities trend: Balance Sheet → current assets: "Contract Assets" (revenue earned but not invoiced yet — could be aggressive timing). Current liabilities: "Contract Liabilities" (invoiced but not yet earned — conservative). A growing Contract Assets balance relative to revenue warrants investigation.
2
Remaining Performance Obligations (RPO): Annual Report Note on Revenue → IND AS 115 requires disclosure of "remaining performance obligations" — revenue from contracts signed but not yet recognised. A declining RPO while revenue is also declining = genuine slowdown. Rising RPO with stable revenue = strong backlog.
3
Revenue recognition policy complexity: Simple businesses (retail, FMCG) have simple 1-paragraph policies. Complex recognition policies (multi-year contracts, variable consideration, bundled performance obligations) warrant closer scrutiny. If the policy has many conditions and estimates — those estimates can be manipulated.

Standard #3 — Mandatory from April 2018
Ind AS 109 — Financial Instruments
Replaces incurred-loss provisioning with Expected Credit Loss (ECL) model — requiring companies to provision for future credit losses before they happen. Fundamentally changes how banks, NBFCs, and companies with large receivables report asset quality and profitability.
💰 Sectors most affected: Banks · NBFCs · Insurers · Companies with large loans/bonds
The old approach to credit losses was reactive: you only provisioned when a loan actually showed signs of default — when the borrower missed payments, asked for restructuring, or the bank classified the loan as NPA. Under Ind AS 109's ECL model, you must provision for expected future losses from the day the loan is made — even if the borrower is currently paying perfectly.

ECL uses a three-stage model: Stage 1 (performing loans) — provision for 12-month expected losses; Stage 2 (significant credit risk increase) — provision for lifetime expected losses; Stage 3 (credit-impaired) — lifetime ECL, similar to old NPA provisioning.

For banks and NBFCs, this means: (1) Provisions are higher and earlier even for good loans; (2) Provisions fluctuate with economic outlook — during recessions, the model's forward-looking assumptions drive provisions up dramatically even before NPAs appear; (3) Management has significant discretion in setting ECL assumptions — probability of default (PD), loss given default (LGD), and exposure at default (EAD) — creating space for both conservatism and manipulation.

For non-financial companies, Ind AS 109 also changed how they classify and measure bonds, equity investments, and derivatives they hold — some must be marked to market through P&L (FVTPL) and others through OCI (FVOCI), creating more volatility in reported numbers.
🚫 Old Incurred-Loss Model (Pre-2018)
Provision only when objective evidence of impairment exists (missed payments, NPA classification)
RBI-mandated NPA provisioning norms — mechanistic, less judgment-based
Provisions could be deferred — making bank balance sheets look better in upcycles
No forward-looking economic assumptions required — backward-looking
Less volatile — provisions didn't swing with economic forecasts
✅ Ind AS 109 ECL Model (Current)
Day-1 provisioning: even a new, performing loan requires a 12-month ECL provision
Probability of Default (PD), Loss Given Default (LGD), Exposure at Default (EAD) — management estimates
Forward-looking macroeconomic scenarios built into assumptions
Provisions rise faster during downturns — before NPAs appear in RBI classification
Higher transparency: requires detailed disclosure of ECL methodology and movements
📊 Classification of Financial Assets Under Ind AS 109
📊
FVTPL — Fair Value Through P&L
Equity trading portfolio, derivatives. Marked to market every quarter — gains and losses hit the P&L directly. Creates P&L volatility. Mutual fund investments held for trading.
🔄
FVOCI — Fair Value Through OCI
Equity investments held strategically (not for trading). Marked to market through Other Comprehensive Income — not P&L. Does not affect reported profit but changes equity.
🏦
Amortised Cost
Loans and bonds held to collect cash flows. No mark-to-market. ECL provision reduces carrying value. Banks' loan books — measured at amortised cost minus ECL provisions.
✅ Ind AS 109 Red Flags for Investors — What to Check
1
ECL provision coverage ratio: Annual Report → Ind AS 109 note. Compare ECL provisions to gross carrying amount by stage. A bank with 95% of loans in Stage 1 but only 0.2% provision coverage may be using optimistic PD assumptions. Compare to peers.
2
Movement in ECL provisions: Notes to accounts → "Movement in loss allowance." If ECL provisions declined significantly in a year without a corresponding improvement in loan quality (Stage 2 and 3 loans also declined) — management may have revised ECL assumptions downward aggressively, inflating reported profit.
3
FVTPL vs FVOCI classification choice: For equity investments, management chooses FVTPL (volatile P&L) or FVOCI (stable P&L). Companies that put large equity portfolios into FVTPL take mark-to-market risk through profit — check if this creates artificial profit in bull markets that reverses in bear markets.

The 8-Minute Ind AS Cheat Sheet

No accounting degree needed. These four fast checks — all doable on Screener.in and BSE Annual Reports in under 8 minutes — tell you whether Ind AS 116, 115, or 109 is materially distorting a company's headline numbers before you spend hours analysing the wrong thing.

🏢
Trick #1 — 90 Seconds · Screener Balance Sheet Tab · Ind AS 116
The "ROU ÷ Total Assets" Materiality Check
90 seconds
Screener.in → Balance Sheet tab → Find "Right of Use Assets" (non-current assets section) → Divide by Total Assets → That percentage tells you instantly whether Ind AS 116 is distorting the balance sheet.

Under 5%: Ind AS 116 is immaterial — ignore it and move on. 5–20%: Moderate impact — adjust D/E and EBITDA. Above 20%: Ind AS 116 is dominating the balance sheet — all leverage ratios are misleading without lease adjustment.
✅ Safe — Ignore 116

1Note Lease Liability (current + non-current) from Balance Sheet

Trick #2 — 60 Seconds · Balance Sheet Current Assets · Ind AS 115
The "Contract Assets vs Contract Liabilities" Ratio Trick
60 seconds
Screener.in → Balance Sheet → Current Assets section → Find "Contract Assets" → Current Liabilities section → Find "Contract Liabilities".

These two numbers, compared to annual revenue, instantly reveal Ind AS 115's effect on the company's revenue timing. Think of it as a "revenue pressure gauge" — high Contract Assets = aggressive recognition, high Contract Liabilities = conservative (deferred) recognition.
✅ Healthy Pattern
Contract Liabilities > Contract Assets
Customer paid in advance but work not done yet → revenue is conservative. Company is spending customer money, not booking it prematurely.

Examples: Airlines (advance bookings), Maruti (dealer deposits), subscription SaaS businesses
🚨 Investigate This Pattern
Contract Assets >10–15% of Revenue
Revenue recognised but not yet invoiced — work completed but billing lagging. If growing each year, company may be accelerating recognition.

Red flag for: Software product companies · Long-term project companies
The one-line rule: For IT services and project companies, Contract Assets ÷ Annual Revenue should be stable or declining as the company grows. A ratio rising from 5% to 12% to 18% over 3 years = revenue recognition is running ahead of billing and potentially ahead of actual work done. Check Annual Report → Revenue Note for explanation.
🏦
Trick #3 — 2 Minutes · Annual Report Notes · Ind AS 109
The "ECL Coverage ÷ Peer Benchmark" Shortcut
2 minutes
For banks and NBFCs, Ind AS 109's ECL model gives management substantial discretion. The fastest way to spot conservative vs aggressive provisioning is a single ratio comparison across peers.

Annual Report → Notes to Financial Statements → Note on "Financial Instruments" or "Credit Risk" → Find: Total ECL provisions ÷ Gross loan book = Coverage Ratio. Compare this to 2–3 peer banks/NBFCs. Significantly below peers = optimistic ECL assumptions = provisions may be understated.
📊 Three ECL Numbers to Find in Annual Report Notes
1
Stage 2 Loans % of total book — loans where credit risk has "significantly increased." Should be disclosed separately. Below 3% = low watchlist. Above 8% = stress building ahead of NPA classification.
2
ECL Coverage on Stage 3 (NPAs) — what % of the NPA value is covered by provisions. Below 50% = under-provisioned vs peers. HDFC Bank typically maintains 75%+.
3
Macroeconomic scenarios used — look for "Base," "Upside," and "Downside" scenarios with weightings. If only one scenario used, or downside weight is below 10%, ECL is probably too optimistic.
Quick peer comparison: If a bank's ECL coverage ratio is more than 30% below its closest peer with a similar loan book — ECL assumptions are likely optimistic. Before investing in any bank or NBFC, spend 2 minutes finding the coverage ratio and comparing it to HDFC Bank (the sector benchmark for conservative provisioning).

Trick #5 — The One Universal Question · Before Any Ratio Analysis
Did the Standard Change — or Did the Business Change?
Universal rule
Every time you see a dramatic change in any financial metric — debt, EBITDA margin, revenue, provisions — ask this one question before drawing any conclusion. If the answer is "the standard changed," the business may be entirely unchanged. If the answer is "the business changed," dig deeper.
🚨
Dangerous Analyst Mistake — Comparing Pre/Post-2019 Without Adjustment
An analyst looking at a retail chain's D/E ratio in FY2018 (0.4×) vs FY2020 (3.2×) and concluding "the company has massively leveraged up" — when the entire change is Ind AS 116 adding lease liabilities. The business didn't change. The standard did. This mistake leads to completely wrong investment conclusions.
✅
The Right Approach — Always Check Annual Report for Standard Transition Notes
Annual report → Notes to Accounts → "Effect of adoption of new accounting standards." Any year when Ind AS 116, 115, or 109 was adopted will have a quantified reconciliation showing the before/after impact. Read this note first. If the standard change explains the metric movement — the business story is unchanged and your analysis can proceed on adjusted numbers.

The One-Line Ind AS Rule
Check ROU ÷ Total Assets First. If >5%, All Your Debt and EBITDA Numbers Are Wrong Until Adjusted.
This single ratio — ROU Asset divided by Total Assets — instantly flags whether Ind AS 116 is material. For aviation, the ratio is 40–70%. For retailers, 10–30%. For manufacturers, under 5%. Knowing this before looking at any other ratio prevents the most common Ind AS analysis mistake: treating lease liabilities as financial debt and lease-adjusted EBITDA as comparable to old EBITDA.

The equivalent US GAAP rule (ASC 842) produces very similar results — making Indian lease-heavy companies broadly comparable to US counterparts when both use the standard. The gap that remains is in credit loss accounting: Ind AS 109 uses 12-month ECL for Stage 1 loans while US GAAP's CECL model requires lifetime ECL from day 1 — making US banks provision more aggressively from origination. When comparing Indian banks to US-listed peers, Indian banks' earlier-stage provisions will look lower by design — not because Indian loan quality is better.

✅ Ind AS 109 benchmark quality: HDFC Bank's ECL disclosures are the reference standard for Indian banking. Stage-wise breakdown, macroeconomic assumptions, PD/LGD methodology, coverage ratios — all disclosed. The conservative provisioning approach means ECL provisions are sufficient to absorb realistic stress scenarios, not just the optimistic base case. This transparency is why HDFC Bank commands premium valuations — investors can see through the accounting to the real asset quality.

🚨
Revenue cliff during transition: Under the old Percentage-of-Completion (POC) method, a developer building a 500-unit project would recognise revenue as construction progressed — 20% construction = 20% revenue. Under Ind AS 115, revenue can only be recognised when the customer has "control" of the flat — typically at possession/handover. This created an immediate revenue cliff: all the POC-recognised revenue had to be reversed, and re-booked only as flats were handed over. Several real estate companies reported dramatic revenue drops in FY2019 purely from the standard change — not business deterioration.
🚨
Contract Liabilities ballooned overnight: All customer advances received for under-construction flats (which had previously been partially recognised as revenue under POC) were reclassified as "Contract Liabilities" — deferred revenue that will be recognised at possession. Some developers showed Contract Liabilities equal to 2–3 years of revenue. This confused investors who didn't understand the standard change — declining revenue + surging Contract Liabilities looked like distress when it was actually just an accounting standard change.
⚠️
Disclosure quality varied enormously: Some developers (like DLF) provided clear reconciliation of the transition impact. Others provided minimal explanation, leaving investors to guess whether the revenue decline was business deterioration or the standard change. The disclosure quality differential created significant information asymmetry — analysts who understood the standard had an enormous edge over those who didn't.
🚨 The real estate Ind AS 115 lesson: A developer's "revenue" under Ind AS 115 is dramatically different from old POC revenue and from real cash inflows. For real estate analysis, track three metrics separately: (1) Bookings (new sales contracted — not revenue), (2) Collections (cash received — closer to economic reality), (3) Ind AS 115 revenue (possession-based — lags business by 2–4 years). None of these equals the others. Comparing revenue across real estate companies requires first confirming they're using the same recognition trigger.

Ind AS 116 EBITDA inflation masks operating weakness: Airlines lease their entire fleet. If this exceeds EBITDA, the company is cash-flow negative on an operating basis despite positive reported EBITDA.
⚠️ Ind AS 116 creates a systematic distortion for airlines: This isn't fraud — IndiGo is correctly applying the standard. The problem is that Ind AS 116 makes airlines look better on EBITDA and worse on D/E than the economic reality. Investors who don't make lease adjustments will systematically overestimate operating profitability and overestimate debt burden simultaneously — both errors at once. Always use EBITDAR and lease-adjusted debt for aviation analysis.

Using optimistic macro scenarios to understate ECL: Ind AS 109 requires companies to incorporate forward-looking macroeconomic scenarios in ECL calculations. During COVID-19 (FY2020-21), some NBFCs used exclusively optimistic GDP recovery scenarios (V-shaped recovery) rather than the more realistic base + downside scenarios that HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank employed. Some NBFCs disclosed Stage 2 only in aggregate, without vintage analysis or sector breakdown, making it impossible for investors to assess adequacy.
⚠️
How to spot ECL manipulation: Compare a company's ECL coverage ratio (total provisions ÷ gross loans) with sector peers. If significantly below peers with similar loan books, either asset quality is genuinely superior (verify with gross NPA, restructured book) or ECL assumptions are optimistic. Also check if macroeconomic scenarios are disclosed — companies using only base-case scenarios are applying the standard more aggressively than those incorporating downside scenarios.
⚠️ The Ind AS 109 investor rule for NBFCs: Never compare NBFC profitability across companies without normalising for ECL provisioning assumptions. Two identical loan books can show dramatically different PAT depending on ECL conservatism. Check: (1) Coverage ratio vs peers, (2) Stage 2 % disclosed, (3) Macro scenarios used. If any of these are opaque or optimistic vs peers — apply a provisioning discount to PAT before comparison.

Upfront license recognition on bundled deals: A software company sells a ₹6 crore deal: ₹4 crore perpetual license + ₹2 crore annual maintenance for 3 years. 🚨 Software Ind AS 115 checklist: For any software product company, check: (1) What % of revenue is license vs maintenance? (2) Is maintenance recognised upfront or ratably? (3) How large are Contract Assets relative to revenue? (4) Has the standalone selling price methodology for performance obligation allocation changed year-over-year? Any change in allocation methodology without a business reason is a potential manipulation signal.

Ind AS 115 used to inflate inter-company revenue: IL&FS used its 347 subsidiaries to create circular revenue — one entity billed another for "project management fees" or "advisory services" that were recognised as Ind AS 115 revenue. From the perspective of any individual entity, the revenue met the technical criteria — there was a contract, a performance obligation, and "consideration." Under Ind AS 109, these loans required ECL provisioning. But because the entities lending were IL&FS subsidiaries and the entities borrowing were also IL&FS subsidiaries, the ECL assumptions used reflected "group support" assumptions that proved completely wrong when the group collapsed. The circular nature meant ECL models couldn't properly assess the true default probability.
🚨
The complexity defense: A 347-entity corporate structure ensures that no single financial statement reveals the truth. Each entity's Ind AS financials look superficially compliant — contracts exist, ECL models exist, revenue is allocated. The manipulation happens at the gaps between standards: consolidated revenue eliminates intercompany but individual entity financials show it; ECL provisions are technically reasonable for individual loans but the system-wide risk is not captured.
🚨 The IL&FS multi-standard failure: IL&FS demonstrates the most dangerous form of accounting manipulation: using legitimate standard compliance at each individual step to produce an illegitimate overall picture. Each Ind AS 115 contract was technically valid. Each Ind AS 109 provision had a methodology. But the interactions between standards, the circular structure, and the complexity of 347 entities prevented any single vantage point from revealing the truth. For investors: extreme complexity in corporate structure is itself a red flag — not because standards can't handle it, but because complexity makes verification impossible.

The Master Ind AS Rule
Always Ask: Is This Change in Numbers from the Business or from the Accounting Standard?
Every time you see a dramatic change in a company's debt levels, EBITDA margin, or revenue — your first question must be: did the underlying business change, or did an accounting standard change? For any Indian listed company analysed with data from FY2019 or FY2020 onwards, you must check whether Ind AS 116 (leases), Ind AS 115 (revenue), or Ind AS 109 (financial instruments) is materially affecting the numbers.

A three-minute materiality check on Screener.in: (1) Go to Balance Sheet → Is "Right of Use Assets" greater than 5% of total assets? If yes, Ind AS 116 is material — adjust D/E and EBITDA accordingly. (2) Go to Balance Sheet → Is "Contract Assets" greater than 10% of annual revenue? If yes, investigate Ind AS 115 recognition timing. (3) For banks/NBFCs: Is the ECL coverage ratio disclosed with stage breakdown? If not, make peer comparisons with caution.

The US GAAP equivalent (ASC 842 for leases, ASC 606 for revenue, CECL for credit losses) is broadly similar in intent to Ind AS 116, 115, and 109 — making Indian accounting broadly comparable to US-listed peers on these dimensions. The key differences: CECL uses lifetime expected losses from day-1 (more aggressive than Ind AS 109's 12-month Stage 1 ECL), and US GAAP allows more operating lease classification flexibility for short-term leases.
