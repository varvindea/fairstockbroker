01 — Key Learning Outcome

Why Consolidated Numbers Can Hide Everything Important

🔍
Your Core Takeaway
Segment reporting breaks a company's total numbers into the underlying businesses — showing you which segments are growing, which are profitable, which are shrinking, and which are quietly destroying value. Without this breakdown, you are flying blind.
Imagine a conglomerate reports ₹5,000 crore revenue and ₹600 crore profit. That sounds decent. But when you look at segments, you discover: the consumer products division generates ₹3,000 crore revenue with a 25% profit margin (₹750 crore profit), the chemicals division generates ₹1,500 crore revenue with 5% margin (₹75 crore profit), and the infrastructure division generates ₹500 crore revenue but loses ₹225 crore. The consolidated profit of ₹600 crore hides the fact that the best segment is carrying two underperformers — and one is destroying substantial value every year.

This matters for five critical investment decisions: (1) Valuing each segment separately at appropriate multiples; (2) Identifying which growth investments are actually generating returns; (3) Spotting management's strategy of using profitable segments to subsidise loss-makers indefinitely; (4) Understanding how exposed the company is to a particular geography or currency; (5) Catching early when a flagship segment is deteriorating — before it shows up in consolidated results.

Under Ind AS 108 (the Indian equivalent of IFRS 8), listed companies must report financial information about operating segments — the same way management internally reviews the business. This "management approach" to segment definition means segment data is as close to how the CEO sees the business as you will ever get from a public document. Every investor should read segment notes before reading consolidated financials — not after.

02 — What Segment Reporting Reveals

Inside the Black Box — What Segments Show That Consolidated Numbers Hide

Segment reporting is like opening the hood of a car — you stop looking at whether it moves and start understanding which engine parts are working, which are struggling, and which need replacing. Every investor should spend more time on segment notes than on the consolidated P&L.

Segment reporting breaks apart the consolidated black box. A consolidated profit of ₹600 Cr hides a star (Consumer: +₹750 Cr), a mediocre (Chemicals: +₹75 Cr), and a destroyer (Infrastructure: −₹225 Cr). The analyst with segment data makes different decisions than the one reading only the headline. Geographic data shows 91% revenue concentration in India + North America — a currency and geopolitical risk that never appears in the P&L.

Four Types of Segment Information to Look For
🏭
Business / Product Segments
Most Common
Revenue, profit, assets, and liabilities broken down by product line or business type. Example: Reliance Industries shows separate segments for Petrochemicals, Oil & Gas, Retail, and Telecom. Each segment has its own revenue, EBIT, and assets — allowing you to value each separately. The petrochemical EBIT multiple is very different from the telecom EBIT multiple.
🌍
Geographic Segments
Currency + Risk
Revenue split by country or region — crucial for understanding currency risk, regulatory risk, and growth concentration. TCS's geographic breakdown (North America 55%, Europe 30%, India 5%, others 10%) shows that a US recession is an existential risk, not a marginal one. Companies that merge all "International" into one line are hiding this analysis.
📉
Profit Margins by Segment
Value Destroyer
EBIT or EBITDA margin for each segment reveals which business is genuinely profitable. A company with 15% consolidated margins may have one segment at 35% subsidising another at −5%. The cross-subsidy keeps the loss-maker alive indefinitely, destroying capital that could be returned to shareholders or deployed in the high-margin business.
💰
Capital Allocation by Segment
ROCE Focus
Segment assets reveal where management is deploying capital. Segment ROCE (EBIT ÷ Segment Assets) shows whether each segment is creating or destroying value relative to its cost of capital. A segment growing assets rapidly with declining ROCE is a capital destruction warning — money is being invested but not generating adequate returns.

6 Tests to Run on Any Company's Segment Disclosures
📊
Test 1: Segment Margin Divergence
Annual Report → Segment Note
Calculate EBIT margin for each segment. Any segment with margin more than 15 percentage points below the group average is a drag candidate. Any segment with margin 15+ percentage points above = the hidden star. Most investors only look at consolidated margins and miss both.
Segment EBIT Margin = Segment EBIT ÷ Segment Revenue
💸
Test 2: Segment ROCE
Segment Assets
Divide segment EBIT by segment assets for every segment. Compare to cost of capital (~12–15% for most Indian companies). Any segment with ROCE below cost of capital is destroying shareholder value — regardless of whether it reports a positive EBIT. A segment earning 4% ROCE when capital costs 14% loses 10 paise per rupee deployed every year.
Segment ROCE = Segment EBIT ÷ Segment Assets
📈
Test 3: Segment Revenue Growth Rate
2-Year Comparison
Compare segment revenue growth to group revenue growth. A segment growing at 5% when the group reports 20% is shrinking in importance — or masking a problem. A segment growing at 60% that management hasn't highlighted deserves investigation as a potential re-rating catalyst when noticed by the broader market.
Segment Growth = (Segment Rev_t − Rev_t-1) ÷ Rev_t-1
🌍
Test 4: Geographic Concentration
Currency Risk
Calculate the % of revenue from any single country or region. Above 40% = concentration risk. Above 60% = very high concentration. For IT companies, check North America %. For pharma, check US generics %. For auto ancillary, check key OEM customer country. Concentration above 50% in any single market = the company's fate is linked to that market's cycle.
Geo Concentration = Region Revenue ÷ Total Revenue
🔄
Test 5: Inter-Segment Revenue
Inflation Warning
Look for "inter-segment eliminations" in the segment note. Large inter-segment revenue suggests one division is selling to another at potentially inflated prices — boosting the selling division's reported revenue and margin artificially. Inter-segment transactions above 15% of gross segment revenue warrant investigation of the pricing methodology.
Inter-Segment % = Eliminations ÷ Gross Segment Revenue
⚠️
Test 6: Segment Disclosure Change
Management Signal
When management changes how segments are reported year-over-year — merging two previously separate segments, changing the geographic breakdown, or reclassifying businesses — this is almost always to obscure deterioration in a specific area. Under Ind AS 108, changes must be disclosed but aren't always highlighted. Always read the "changes in segment" note carefully.

Sum-of-the-Parts (SOTP) valuation made possible: Reliance's five-segment disclosure allows analysts to value Jio at telecom multiples (EBITDA 12–15×), Retail at retail multiples (EBITDA 30–40×), and Petrochemicals at commodity multiples (EBITDA 6–8×) — and add them up. "Others" larger than 10% = something is being hidden: Ind AS 108 requires disclosure of segments that are individually significant (typically >10% of total revenue, profit, or assets). The "Others" category should capture genuinely small, miscellaneous activities. When "Others" represents 20–30% of group revenue — it means management is deliberately grouping businesses to avoid disclosing one or more of them separately. The most common reason: loss-making ventures that would embarrass management if shown individually.
🚨
Segments merged when losses emerge: Multiple Indian conglomerates have been observed merging previously separate segments (that were showing deteriorating trends) into a combined category when results worsen. Under Ind AS 108, this requires disclosure of the change and its rationale — but the disclosure is often buried and incomplete. The merger itself is the signal: why would management voluntarily reduce transparency unless the component data was damaging?
🚨
How to detect: Compare current year segment structure to prior year. If segments have been merged or a new large "Others" category has appeared, find the reason. Check the management commentary — if they've stopped discussing a previously highlighted business unit, suspect it's now inside "Others" performing poorly.
🚨 The "Others" rule: If "Others" segment exceeds 10% of group revenue and no EBIT breakdown is provided — dock the company's disclosure quality score significantly. This is the single most common form of segment opacity in Indian annual reports. Investors should demand management explain every business inside "Others" and why it doesn't qualify for separate disclosure.

Geographic revenue fabricated across all segments: Satyam's fraud involved inflating revenue across multiple geographies — US, Europe, and India — through fake invoices to fictitious clients. The forensic test: does each geographic segment's revenue convert to cash from that geography? For IT companies, check "unbilled revenue" and "accrued income" by geography. When geographic segment revenue grows while geographic-origin cash flows don't — the geographic breakdown is unverified and potentially fabricated.

Three segments for 347 subsidiaries = structured opacity: IL&FS reported three broad segments: Infrastructure Services, Financial Services, and Social and Commercial Infrastructure. But within these three categories, 347 subsidiaries were aggregated. Each SPV had separate debt, separate cash flows, and separate project-level economics. When a company's segment disclosure is too broad relative to its actual business complexity — that disparity is itself a red flag. Three segments for 347 entities is not compliance; it's concealment.

⚡ Quick Tricks — Find Hidden Segments Fast
⚡ Quick Tricks

The 8-Minute Segment Analysis Cheat Sheet

You don't need to read a 300-page annual report to spot the hidden profit star or the drag segment. These five fast checks — all using free tools — give you the essential segment picture in under 8 minutes.

🔍
Trick #1 — 60 Seconds · Annual Report PDF (Ctrl+F)
The "Ctrl+F Segment" Jump — Find the Note Instantly
60 seconds
Download the Annual Report PDF from Screener.in → Documents tab → Press Ctrl+F (or Cmd+F on Mac) → Type "segment information". Jump directly to the note. On mobile: tap the PDF viewer search icon → type "segment".

This single note contains all segment revenue, EBIT, assets, and geographic breakdown. You don't need to read any other note first. Every Indian listed company's Ind AS 108 segment note is in here — typically called "Note 30–38: Segment Information." Find it → everything else follows.
✅ What to Read First
Segment name → Revenue → EBIT (or "Segment result") → Segment assets. In that order. Everything else is secondary.
🚨 Red Flag on First Read
Is "Others" segment larger than any named segment? If yes — stop and investigate what's inside it before going further.
📊
Trick #2 — 2 Minutes · Calculator or Phone
The "Margin by Segment" Calculation — Find the Star and the Drag
2 minutes
For each segment: divide Segment EBIT ÷ Segment Revenue. Write down the margin for each segment. Compare to group margin. Any segment with margin 15+ percentage points above group average = star worth digging into. Any segment with margin 15+ percentage points below, or negative margin = drag candidate.
1
From the segment note: copy Segment Revenue and Segment EBIT (or "Segment Result" — it's EBIT in Ind AS 108 language) for each segment
2
Divide: EBIT ÷ Revenue = EBIT Margin %. Do this in your phone calculator — takes 30 seconds per segment
3
Compare all segment margins to each other and to the consolidated margin. The spread tells you everything about cross-subsidisation.
4
If a segment has negative EBIT margin — calculate how much profit the group would have made if this segment didn't exist. That's the value the drag is destroying.
Star margin (25%) − Consolidated margin (12%) = 13% premium → this segment is worth a higher valuation multiple
Drag margin (−45%) → remove drag → consolidated margin would be 17.5% → group is worth more "broken up"
📈
Trick #3 — 90 Seconds · Two Annual Reports
The "Segment Revenue Growth" Check — Which Part Is Actually Growing
90 seconds
Open current year and prior year annual reports side by side. Find segment revenue for each segment in both years. Calculate growth rate for each segment: (Current Year − Prior Year) ÷ Prior Year.

Compare each segment's growth to the group's reported revenue growth. The segments with growth above group rate are gaining importance. Segments below group rate are losing relevance — or their problems are being masked by a faster-growing segment.
✅ Good Signal
High-margin segment growing faster than group = quality improvement in consolidated mix. The group is becoming more valuable over time.
🚨 Bad Signal
Low-margin or loss-making segment growing faster than group = mix is deteriorating. Consolidated margins will compress even if revenue grows.
🌍
Trick #4 — 60 Seconds · Geographic Note
The "Single Country >40%" Concentration Alert
60 seconds
In the segment note, find the geographic revenue breakdown. Calculate each country/region as a % of total revenue. If any single country or region exceeds 40%, this company's fate is linked to that geography.

One-line rule: above 40% = concentration risk. Above 60% = high concentration. Above 80% = single-market dependence.
1
IT companies: check North America % → above 50% = US tech spending cycle directly drives earnings. FAANG budget cuts hit immediately.
2
Pharma companies: check US % → above 40% = USFDA actions can cut revenue 15–20% overnight. Is US breaking out or buried in "International"?
3
Auto ancillary: check key OEM customer country → if 60% revenue from one OEM in one country — that OEM's production cycle is your production cycle.
4
FMCG/Consumer: India typically 90%+ → check whether any meaningful international revenue exists and whether it's growing faster or slower than domestic.
🏆
Trick #5 — 3 Minutes · The Universal Segment Question
If You Could Buy Only One Segment, Which Would You Buy?
3 minutes
After running Tricks 1–4, ask yourself: if the company were broken up and you could invest in just one segment at the consolidated valuation, which segment would you buy? And which one would you never invest in as a standalone?

This question crystallises everything: the star segment (high margin, growing, high ROCE) is what you're really buying. The drag segment is what you're paying a tax to hold. The market's consolidated valuation may dramatically underprice the star or overvalue the consolidated entity because of the drag.
⭐
If Star Segment Is Undervalued in Consolidated Price
Apply the star segment's appropriate industry multiple to its EBIT. If the result exceeds the company's current EV — the stock is cheap even before you get any value from other segments. This is the SOTP opportunity — common in Indian conglomerates where one division is a jewel.
🚨
If Drag Segment Makes the Whole Unattractive
Calculate consolidated profit if drag segment were shut down or divested. If the hypothetical "ex-drag" margins and ROCE make the stock look cheap — lobby for (or wait for) management to act on the drag. Activist investors and PE firms often target exactly this structure.
⚡
The One-Line Segment Rule
Read the Segment Note Before the Consolidated P&L. Every Time. Without Exception.
The consolidated P&L is a blended average of all the underlying businesses. Reading it first is like looking at the average of a class test score before knowing whether the class had one brilliant student and ten poor ones, or ten mediocre students. The average tells you nothing about the distribution — and the distribution is what matters for investment decisions.

The segment note — typically two to four pages in any annual report — is the most information-dense section in the entire document. Segment EBIT margins, segment ROCE, geographic concentration, and year-over-year segment growth trends tell you more about the quality of the business than any ratio calculated from the consolidated financial statements.

Under Ind AS 108, every listed Indian company must disclose segment information consistent with how its chief operating decision maker reviews the business. This "management approach" means you are reading the same framework the CEO uses — making segment data as close to management thinking as any public document can be. The question is not whether this information exists — it does, for every listed company. The question is whether you are reading it.
⚡ Quick Tricks

Find Hidden Segments in Under 8 Minutes — No Accounting Degree Needed

These five fast, practical tricks work on any Indian listed company using only free tools — Screener.in, BSE filing PDFs, and a phone calculator. Run them in order. Stop when you find your answer.

🧮
Trick A — 3 Minutes · Annual Report Segment Note + Phone Calculator
The "Napkin SOTP" — Is the Star Segment Worth More Than the Whole Company?
3 minutes
This is the most powerful segment trick in investing. From the segment note, take the highest-margin segment's EBIT and multiply it by the typical industry multiple for that type of business. If the result is close to or exceeds the company's current EV (Enterprise Value) — you are getting all other segments for free.

This napkin SOTP has identified some of the best Indian investment opportunities of the past decade — including Reliance before Jio was valued, Hindustan Unilever before FMCG multiples re-rated, and L&T before the engineering-to-services transition was priced in.
📱 Step-by-Step Napkin SOTP (takes 3 minutes)
1Segment note → note down EBIT for each segment (called "Segment Result" in Ind AS 108 language)
2Apply a rough industry multiple to each segment's EBIT — Consumer/FMCG: 25–35×, IT/Services: 20–30×, Industrials: 12–18×, Commodity/Chemicals: 6–10×, Financial Services: 15–25×
3Sum all segment values → add cash (from balance sheet) → subtract debt → compare to current market cap from Screener.in overview page
4If Segment A's value alone ≥ current market cap — you are buying Segments B, C, D for zero. That is the SOTP opportunity. Investigate why the market hasn't priced it.
✅ Classic SOTP Opportunity Example
Reliance Industries (2019): Jio EBITDA × telecom multiple alone exceeded market cap. Investors who ran the SOTP got retail + petrochemicals + O&G essentially free. The market took 2 more years to price it in.
🚨 When SOTP Shows a Trap
If the drag segment's losses are large enough that even the star's SOTP value barely exceeds market cap — the drag is destroying more value than the star creates. Don't buy hoping management exits the drag.
🔎
Trick B — 90 Seconds · Annual Report vs Investor Deck
The "Missing Segment" Trick — What Management Stopped Talking About
90 seconds
Compare this year's investor presentation (available on Screener → Documents) to last year's. Which business unit, product line, or geography that management featured last year is absent or downplayed this year?

Management investor presentations are marketing documents — they highlight what is going well. When a business unit that was given a dedicated slide last year has no slide this year, it's almost certainly deteriorating. The segment note in the annual report will confirm or deny this — because Ind AS 108 requires disclosure regardless of management's preference.
🚨 Red Flag Pattern
Investor deck no longer breaks out a previously highlighted division → Check segment note: is that business now inside "Others"? Have margins declined? Is revenue stagnant while the group is growing?
✅ Green Flag Pattern
A segment that appears in the annual report note but management hasn't featured in investor presentations — and it has strong margins. Market may not have priced it. Investigate why it's not being highlighted.
Where to get documents: Screener.in → search company → Documents tab → "Annual Report" + "Investor Presentation." Both should be there. If the investor presentation is more than 6 months old — management has stopped communicating. That itself is a signal.
📅
Trick C — 2 Minutes · Quarterly Results + Concall
The "Segment Mentioned Only in Q4" Warning
2 minutes
Search Screener.in → Documents → Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4 Concall Transcripts (or investor releases). Count how many times a specific segment, business unit, or geographic region is mentioned across the four quarterly calls.

A segment mentioned prominently in Q1–Q3 but barely referenced in Q4 is almost certainly the one that disappointed for the year — and management is hoping investors forget about it before the annual report is filed. The annual report segment note will show the actual numbers — but the concall pattern gives you 3 months advance warning.
🔍 The Ctrl+F Concall Method
1Download Q4 concall transcript from Screener Documents tab → Ctrl+F the segment name (e.g. "chemicals", "infrastructure", "US market")
2Note how many times it appears. Then open Q2 transcript and Ctrl+F the same term → compare count
3Fallen dramatically? That segment is the problem. Read the Q4 segment note immediately and check its EBIT vs the prior year.
Real-world example: When IL&FS infrastructure began deteriorating in 2017–18, concall mentions of specific project milestones and "collection timelines" dropped sharply across Q2 and Q3 of FY18. Analysts who tracked this language shift had an early warning — the segment note in the annual report confirmed the collapse months later.
🌍
Trick D — 60 Seconds · Segment Note Geographic Table
The "International = One Line" Red Flag Test
60 seconds
In the segment note, look at the geographic revenue table. Count the number of geographic lines. The number of geography lines directly indicates disclosure quality. Two lines (India + International) = minimum compliance, maximum opacity. Five or more lines = high quality disclosure you can actually analyse.

Apply this sector-specific rule immediately:
Sector Minimum Acceptable Breakdown Red Flag Situation
IT Services India, North America, Europe, APAC, Rest of World "Domestic + International" only 🚨
Pharmaceuticals India, USA, Europe, Emerging Markets "India + Exports" without US separate 🚨
Auto Ancillary India OEM, India Aftermarket, Export by region "Domestic + Exports" with no OEM breakdown 🚨
Textiles / Gems India, USA, EU, Middle East, Others "Domestic + Export" with related party exports 🚨
FMCG India (majority), International by major market India-only is fine — low export exposure ✅
The one-line test: If a company has significant international revenue but reports it as a single "International" line — deduct one quality point from the segment disclosure score and assume you cannot properly analyse geographic risk. Use management commentary, investor presentations, and proxy data (US plant approvals, export invoices in concall Q&A) to reconstruct the geographic breakdown yourself.
🏆
Trick E — The 5-Minute Master Check · Combines All Above
The "Segment ROCE Ranking" — Rank Every Segment, Find the Winner and Loser Instantly
5 minutes
The single most powerful segment metric is Segment ROCE = Segment EBIT ÷ Segment Assets. Rank every segment by ROCE from highest to lowest. This ranking tells you — in one number per segment — which parts of the business are creating value above cost of capital and which are destroying it. Do this every year for 3 years. The trend in each segment's ROCE is more important than any single year's value.
⭐
ROCE >25% — Capital Compounder
This segment is generating outstanding returns. Management should be allocating maximum capital here. If capex is instead going to a 5% ROCE segment — ask why in the AGM or concall Q&A. The capital allocation mismatch is a red flag for management quality.
⚠️
ROCE 10–25% — Acceptable Zone
Earning above cost of capital but not exceptional. The question is trajectory — is ROCE rising (business getting better) or falling (business deteriorating with capital trapped)? Falling ROCE on a growing asset base = value destruction in slow motion.
🚨
ROCE <10% (or Negative) — Capital Destroyer
Below cost of capital = destroying shareholder wealth every year. How large is this segment's asset base? Multiply (cost of capital minus actual ROCE) × segment assets = annual value destruction in rupees. This number often shocks investors who only looked at consolidated results.
🏆 Complete 5-Minute Segment Checklist
60s: Ctrl+F "segment" → open segment note → scan for segment names and EBIT per segment.
2min: EBIT ÷ Revenue for each → identify star (highest) and drag (lowest or negative).
1min: EBIT ÷ Segment Assets for each → rank by ROCE → note any below 10%.
60s: Geographic note → count geography lines → flag any >40% single-country concentration.
30s: Does any segment have negative EBIT? → calculate what consolidated margins would be without it.
Result: You now know which segment is the real investment thesis and which is the tax you pay to hold it.
⚡
The Segment Analyst's One-Line Rule
If a Company Has More Than Two Business Lines — Always Read the Segment Note. Consolidated Numbers Tell You the Average; Segment Notes Tell You the Truth.
Every single one of the "bad examples" in this chapter — Satyam's fabricated geographic revenues, IL&FS's 347-subsidiary opacity, Vodafone Idea's post-merger circle-level blackout, conglomerates' "Others" dustbin segments — would have been detected faster, or avoided entirely, by an investor who habitually opened the segment note before the consolidated P&L.

The five tricks above take eight minutes total. They work on any company, any sector, any size — using only free tools. The segment note is there in every annual report, mandated by Ind AS 108, typically pages 180–190 of the PDF. Most investors skip it entirely and read only the headline revenue and profit. The investors who read the segment note are reading a different document — one that shows the actual business rather than the consolidated average.

The next time you see a company trading at a discount to peers despite "similar revenue growth" — check the segment note first. The answer is almost always there: a drag segment is destroying the margin. And the solution is almost always either management exits the drag, or the market eventually stops giving it full credit in the consolidated valuation.
