01 — Key Learning Outcome

Why Operating Cash Flow Can Be Manipulated Just Like Profit

💰
Your Core Takeaway
Most investors believe that "cash flow doesn't lie" — that unlike profit (which uses accounting estimates), cash is real. This is partly true. But operating cash flow (CFO) can be artificially inflated through working capital manipulation, reclassification of cash flows, and non-operating sources — making a deteriorating business look healthy long enough to fool investors.
Imagine a manufacturing company that is losing market share and whose customers are taking longer to pay. Its profit looks fine because revenue is still being booked. But CFO should be declining — as receivables pile up, the gap between profit and cash should widen.

Now imagine the management team knows this and wants to maintain the illusion of a healthy business. They have several tools: they can stretch payables (delay paying suppliers — which releases cash temporarily), reduce inventory aggressively (sell off stock built over prior years — releasing cash that won't be replaced), factor receivables (sell receivables to a bank at a discount — cash comes in now but future receivables are already "spent"), or reclassify interest payments from CFO to CFF (financing activities — improving apparent CFO).

Each of these techniques produces cash in the current quarter — allowing CFO to look strong even as the business deteriorates. But each leaves a forensic fingerprint: payables days rising abnormally, inventory days falling suspiciously, receivables factored appearing in Notes, or interest reclassified in the cash flow reconciliation. This chapter teaches you to find all four fingerprints — and spot the difference between genuine cash generation and manufactured CFO.
🚨 Payables stretch = fake CFO boost ⚠️ Inventory dump = unsustainable 📋 Receivables factoring = hidden cash cost ✅ CFO/PAT >0.9× = quality signal

The Mechanics — How Working Capital Manipulation Actually Works
Operating Cash Flow = PAT + Non-Cash Items − Working Capital Increase
The formula shows exactly where manipulation lives. Non-cash items (depreciation, provisions) are difficult to manipulate directly. But working capital — receivables, inventory, payables — is entirely under management's influence. Squeeze it from all three sides simultaneously and CFO looks stellar even as the business burns.
The Cash Flow from Operations (CFO) statement is calculated indirectly from PAT using adjustments. The three working capital components that management can manipulate are:

Receivables: Collect faster (good) or factor receivables (appears good but costs money) → Receivables declining = CFO boost
Inventory: Reduce inventory (may be genuine efficiency or unsustainable liquidation) → Inventory declining = CFO boost
Payables: Pay suppliers slower (payables increasing = CFO boost, but strains relationships and may indicate cash stress)

The key insight: if receivables decline, inventory declines, AND payables increase all at the same time — three working capital components all moving in the "favorable" direction simultaneously — the probability that this is organic improvement vs manufactured CFO is very low. Genuine business improvement rarely produces all three simultaneously.
✅ Genuine CFO Quality Signals
CFO/PAT consistently above 0.9× for 5+ years — profit is converting to cash every year
Working capital improvement accompanied by revenue growth — faster collections on more sales
Payables stable or declining — company strong enough to NOT stretch suppliers
Inventory days stable or gently declining — genuine supply chain efficiency, not dumping
CFO exceeds capex (positive FCF) for multiple years — business self-funds its growth
🚨 Working Capital Manipulation Warning Signs
Payables days rising 20+ days in a single year while revenue is flat or falling
Inventory days falling 30%+ while revenue isn't growing — inventory being dumped
Receivables factoring disclosed in Notes — CFO inflated by "selling" future cash
All three: receivables down + inventory down + payables up in the same year
CFO strong but FCF (CFO minus capex) negative — cash generated but immediately spent
7 Cash Flow Red Flags — Check These on Every Company
📈
Red Flag 1: CFO/PAT Below 0.7×
Screener → Cash Flow Tab
The single most important cash quality ratio. Divide Operating Cash Flow by Net Profit. Below 0.7× for 2+ years = profit is not converting to cash. Either working capital is consuming cash (receivables/inventory building) or earnings are partially fictitious. Calculate for 5 years — the trend matters more than any single year.
CFO/PAT = Cash from Operations ÷ Net Profit after Tax
⏰
Red Flag 2: Payables Days Rising Fast
Screener → Ratios Tab
Creditor Days (payables days) rising 20+ days in a year while revenue is flat = management is delaying supplier payments to conserve cash. This inflates CFO temporarily. But it strains supplier relationships, may attract interest penalties, and must eventually reverse — at which point CFO will crater. Compare to sector peers.
Payables Days = (Payables ÷ COGS) × 365
📦
Red Flag 3: Inventory Days Sudden Collapse
Screener → Ratios Tab
Inventory Days falling 30%+ in one year without a corresponding revenue surge = inventory is being liquidated, not sold through normal operations. Cash from inventory liquidation inflates CFO — but next year there is no inventory to liquidate. A one-time boost masquerading as operational efficiency. Check if gross margin also declined (sold at discounts).
Inventory Days = (Inventory ÷ COGS) × 365
📋
Red Flag 4: Receivables Factoring in Notes
Annual Report → CFS Note
Companies can "factor" (sell) their receivables to banks, receiving cash immediately and removing receivables from the balance sheet. This inflates current CFO but is effectively borrowing against future receivables. Ind AS requires disclosure in Notes to Cash Flow Statement. Ctrl+F "factored" or "discounted bills" in the annual report PDF. Growing factoring = growing disguised debt.
Annual Report → Ctrl+F "factored" / "discounted"
🔀
Red Flag 5: CapEx Classified as OpEx — or Vice Versa
Balance Sheet vs P&L
If a company capitalises operating costs as assets (e.g., software development costs, brand-building expenses recognised as intangibles), these costs are removed from the P&L (improving PAT) and appear as CapEx in the investing section (worsening CFI but not CFO). Result: CFO looks better than underlying operations. Signal: rapidly rising intangible assets or CWIP without matching revenue growth.
Check: Intangible Assets growth vs Revenue growth
📉
Red Flag 6: FCF ≪ CFO (CapEx Absorbing All Cash)
Screener → Cash Flow
Free Cash Flow = CFO minus Maintenance CapEx. If CFO is consistently strong but FCF is near-zero or negative — every rupee of operating cash is being immediately reinvested in fixed assets. This may be legitimate growth investment — or may indicate the business requires constant asset replacement (asset-heavy industries) or is sustaining operations through continuous capex that should be maintenance, not growth.
FCF = CFO − Capital Expenditure (Investing Activities)
💱
Red Flag 7: Interest Reclassified from CFO to CFF
Cash Flow Statement
Under Ind AS, companies can choose to classify interest paid either in CFO or CFF (financing activities). A company that switches from CFO classification to CFF classification for interest payments immediately improves reported CFO — without any real change in the business. Compare year-over-year classification. Any reclassification of interest payments should be disclosed and should trigger a recalculation of comparable CFO.
Check: Interest paid in CFO vs CFF — any reclassification?

The forensic check: compare intangible asset growth to revenue growth. If intangibles are growing 40% per year while revenue grows 8% — the company is likely capitalising costs that should be expensed. Screener Balance Sheet → Intangible Assets trend.
🚨
CFO/PAT declining for 3 years while management claimed business was improving
CG Power's CFO/PAT ratio declined consistently for 3 years before the board's forensic audit revealed ₹2,800+ crore of unauthorised transactions. Declining CFO/PAT while management narrative remains positive is a specific red flag because it suggests cash is leaving the business through channels not reflected in the reported P&L. In CG Power's case, advances to related parties through subsidiary routes were consuming cash — showing up as declining CFO quality before the transactions were discovered. The Screener Cash Flow tab would have shown this deterioration clearly.
⚠️
Growing "Other Current Assets" — advances to related parties hiding in plain sight
Screener Balance Sheet → "Other Current Assets" grew significantly faster than revenue for multiple years before the CG Power fraud was disclosed. This line item often contains advances to subsidiaries, loans to related parties, and inter-company receivables — all the instruments used to extract cash through subsidiary routes. When Other Current Assets grows faster than revenue for 2+ consecutive years, open the Annual Report and check what's inside it. In CG Power's case, it contained advances to entities connected to the promoter group.
⚠️ The combined CG Power cash quality signal: Three simultaneous signals — (1) Intangibles growing faster than revenue (CapEx reclassification), (2) CFO/PAT declining while management narrative was positive (cash leaving through non-P&L channels), (3) Other Current Assets growing faster than revenue (advances to related parties). Any single signal is a yellow flag. All three together is a definitive red flag. The Screener Balance Sheet and Cash Flow tabs would have revealed all three in under 5 minutes, years before the forensic audit.

⚡ Quick Tricks

The 8-Minute Cash Flow Quality Cheat Sheet

Five fast checks — all free on Screener.in and annual report PDFs — that give you the complete cash flow quality picture in under 8 minutes. Run them before analysing any company's profitability.

💰
Trick #1 — 60 Seconds · Screener Cash Flow Tab
The "Two Number" CFO/PAT Test — The Most Important Cash Quality Check
60 seconds
Screener.in → Cash Flow tab → Find "Cash from Operations" (CFO) → Screener Financials tab → Find "Net Profit" (PAT). Divide CFO ÷ PAT. Do this for 5 years. Draw the trend in your head. This single ratio tells you whether profit is real or manufactured.
✅ Elite
>1.0×
Cash exceeds profit. Nestle 1.11×, TCS 1.05×
⚠️ Watch
0.7–0.9×
Borderline. Check WC days trend.
🚨 Investigate
<0.7×
For 2+ years: Manpasand 0.28×, exit.
📊
Trick #2 — 90 Seconds · Screener Ratios Tab
The "Three WC Days Straight Lines" — Find Manipulation in the Trend
90 seconds
Screener.in → Ratios tab → Find three rows: Debtor Days, Inventory Days, Creditor Days. Look at 5 years. Draw a mental straight line through each.

The manipulation signal: All three moving in the CFO-favorable direction simultaneously — Debtor Days down + Inventory Days down + Creditor Days up — all in the same year. Legitimate business improvement rarely moves all three at once. This triple movement is the working capital manipulation fingerprint.
✅ Organic Pattern
Debtor Days: stable or gently improving
Inventory Days: stable or gently declining
Creditor Days: stable or slightly improving
Each metric moves gradually in one direction over 3–5 years. Business improvement happens slowly.
🚨 Manipulation Pattern
Debtor Days: ↓ suddenly 25%
Inventory Days: ↓ suddenly 30%
Creditor Days: ↑ suddenly 40 days
All three in one year. Too convenient. Manufactured CFO.
🔎
Trick #3 — 2 Minutes · Annual Report PDF Ctrl+F
The "Ctrl+F Factoring" — Find Hidden Debt Disguised as Operating Cash
2 minutes
Download Annual Report PDF from Screener Documents tab → press Ctrl+F → search these exact terms. Ind AS requires disclosure of receivables factoring and bill discounting in the Notes to the Cash Flow Statement — it's mandatory, but buried.
"factored"
Receivables sold to bank. CFO inflated by proceeds. Growing factoring = growing disguised debt.
"discounted bills"
Bills of exchange discounted with bank. Same economic effect as factoring — selling future receivables now.
"securitised"
Receivables pooled and sold as securities. Common in auto finance companies — check if volume rising.
"recourse"
Factored with recourse = if customer doesn't pay, company pays bank back. Hidden contingent liability.
"interest paid in CFO"
Check if interest is in CFO or CFF. Any reclassification from CFO to CFF artificially improves CFO.
Zero hits = best outcome
No factoring, no bill discounting, no reclassifications = CFO is what it appears to be. Clean.
📈
Trick #4 — 60 Seconds · Screener Balance Sheet
The "Intangibles vs Revenue" Growth Ratio — CapEx Reclassification Signal
60 seconds
Screener → Balance Sheet → Find "Intangible Assets" and "Capital Work in Progress (CWIP)". Compare their 3-year growth rate to Revenue's 3-year growth rate.

Rule: If Intangibles or CWIP grow 3× faster than revenue over 3 years — costs are being capitalised to inflate CFO. When operating expenses are capitalised as assets, they leave the P&L (improving PAT) and become CapEx (leaving CFO intact, hitting CFI). Both P&L and CFO look better — but balance sheet quality deteriorates.
The cross-check: If Intangibles are growing fast, check whether the company is disclosing them as "software development costs," "product development costs," or "brand development costs" in the Notes. These may be legitimate R&D capitalisation — but they require the costs to meet strict recognition criteria (probable future economic benefits, technical feasibility). If the intangibles are not generating revenue growth — the capitalisation criteria may not be met. Rising intangibles + flat revenue = red flag.
🏆
Trick #5 — 3 Minutes · The Complete Cash Flow Quality Verdict
The "Cash Corroboration" Master Check — Follow the Cash to Its Destination
3 minutes
Real CFO must go somewhere verifiable. Follow the cash from its source (CFO) to its destination (dividends, buybacks, debt repayment, capex, or bank balance growth). If CFO is strong but none of the destinations reflect it — the CFO isn't real.
1️⃣
Cash from Operations (Screener Cash Flow tab)
Note the CFO for the last 3 years. This is the starting point. Sum = total operating cash generated over 3 years.
2️⃣
Trace to Destinations (Cash Flow Investing + Financing sections)
Capex paid (CFI) + Dividends paid (CFF) + Debt repaid (CFF) + Buybacks (CFF) + Cash balance increase (Balance Sheet) = should approximately equal CFO over the same 3 years.
3️⃣
If Destinations Don't Match CFO — Find the Leak
If CFO of ₹300 Cr over 3 years but capex + dividends + debt repayment + cash increase = only ₹120 Cr — where did ₹180 Cr go? This is the "unaccounted cash" signal. Check: Other Current Assets, Advances to subsidiaries, related party transactions. The cash went somewhere — find it.

The One-Line Cash Flow Rule
Cash Flow Doesn't Lie — But Manufactured CFO Deceives Just as Effectively as Fake Profits. Know the Difference.
The belief that "cash doesn't lie" is dangerous if taken too literally. Cash flow from operations is an accounting construct — not a bank statement. It uses the same indirect method (PAT + adjustments) as earnings, and the adjustments are as manipulable as any P&L line. Payables stretching, inventory dumping, receivables factoring, and capex reclassification are all legal, all common, and all distort the picture of business health that CFO is supposed to provide.

The five checks above take 8 minutes and are completely free. CFO/PAT ratio tells you whether profit is converting to cash. Working capital days (all three, for 5 years) tell you whether that conversion is structural or manufactured. Ctrl+F for factoring tells you whether receivables have been sold. Intangibles growth tells you whether costs are being capitalised. And cash corroboration tells you whether the CFO is going anywhere real.

Every major Indian cash flow manipulation — Manpasand (0.28× CFO/PAT), IL&FS (payables 180+ days), Jet Airways (triple WC manipulation), CG Power (intangibles rising + CFO declining), Satyam (fake cash — cross-check interest income) — left exactly these fingerprints. The data was free. The checks were simple. The failures were entirely preventable with 8 minutes of analysis.
