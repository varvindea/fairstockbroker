01 — Key Learning Outcome

Why Revenue Can Lie — Even in Audited Accounts

🔍
Your Core Takeaway
Revenue is the most manipulated line in any income statement because it is the first line that analysts, media, and investors look at — and because accounting standards allow revenue to be "recognised" (recorded) before cash actually arrives, before customers confirm they want the goods, and sometimes before goods even leave the factory.
Imagine you run a water bottle factory. Your sales quota for December is ₹10 crore. It's December 28th and you've only sold ₹6 crore. So you call your biggest distributor and say: "Take 200,000 extra bottles today. We'll give you special payment terms — pay us in April. And if they don't sell, we'll take them back quietly." The distributor agrees. You ship the bottles on December 30th. Your P&L for the year shows ₹10 crore in revenue. The auditors tick it off — goods left the factory, an invoice was raised, the distributor signed the purchase order.

But did you actually "earn" ₹4 crore? No. The distributor didn't want those bottles. They'll return 80% of them in January. Your next quarter's revenue will mysteriously fall. Your receivables will blow up because the distributor isn't paying. This is channel stuffing — and it happens in pharmaceuticals, FMCG, auto ancillaries, and technology companies across India and globally.

Bill and Hold is even simpler: you raise an invoice for goods that are still sitting in your own warehouse. The customer "agreed" to buy them but hasn't arranged storage yet. Revenue is recognised because there's a signed contract — but the goods never moved. These two techniques represent the most common forms of revenue manipulation in public markets, and both leave specific, detectable fingerprints in financial data available free on Screener.in.

02 — The Two Revenue Inflation Techniques Explained

How Management Inflates the Top Line

Both techniques exploit a gap in accounting rules: revenue can be recognised when "risks and rewards" transfer — but management has enormous discretion over when that moment is deemed to have occurred. Understanding the mechanics reveals why the fingerprints are always predictable.

Real revenue: a customer places an order, goods are delivered, the customer pays. Three signals confirm quality — receivables stable, CFO tracks PAT, and quarterly revenue is smooth. Inflated revenue: goods are forced into the distribution channel (channel stuffing) or invoiced without delivery (bill and hold). Three fingerprints always appear — receivables spike, CFO falls below PAT, and Q1 revenue mysteriously disappoints after a strong Q4.

🚛
Technique #1 — Most Common in FMCG, Pharma, Auto
Channel Stuffing
Forcing products into the distribution channel — retailers, distributors, dealers — beyond their actual sales capacity, to inflate quarterly revenue. The goods sit in the channel unsold, creating returns, receivables, and next-quarter revenue collapse.
🚨 High Frequency · Quarter-End Pressure · Leaves Receivable Fingerprint
Picture a pharmaceutical company with 45 regional distributors. The company's quarterly revenue quota is ₹180 crore. By March 27th, actual sell-through (products actually purchased by patients at pharmacies) is only ₹130 crore. So the company calls its distributors: "Take ₹50 crore of additional inventory today. We'll extend your payment terms to 90 days (instead of the usual 30), and give you a 5% year-end discount. If it doesn't move, we'll adjust next quarter."

The distributors comply because they depend on the company for their business. The company ships ₹50 crore of goods, raises invoices, and books ₹180 crore in quarterly revenue. The auditor sees shipped goods, signed invoices, and purchase orders from distributors. Revenue is recognised under IND-AS 115. But the goods pile up in distributor warehouses. In Q1 of next year, distributors don't place new orders because their shelves are still full. Q1 revenue "disappoints." Meanwhile, receivables are ₹50 crore higher because distributors haven't paid for goods they don't really want.

This cycle was repeated by Enron's broadband division, various pharma companies, and several Indian FMCG brands. The pattern is always identical — Q4 revenue spike, Q1 revenue miss, rising receivables, declining CFO/PAT ratio.

Technique #2 — Common in Industrial, Construction, IT Equipment
Bill and Hold
Raising invoices for goods that are still sitting in the seller's own warehouse or factory — because the buyer claims they don't have space yet, or "delivery arrangements" haven't been finalised. Revenue is recognised before the customer physically receives anything.
⚠️ Hard to Detect · Requires Annual Report Reading · IND-AS 115 Addresses It
A capital goods company makes industrial boilers. It gets an order in Q3 for delivery in Q1 next year. The CFO wants to book the revenue in Q3. So the company arranges a "bill and hold" arrangement: the customer signs a purchase order acknowledging they've bought the equipment. The company raises an invoice. Revenue is recognised. But the boiler sits in the factory. Delivery happens next quarter.

Under IND-AS 115, bill and hold is only permitted if four conditions are met: (1) the reason for the arrangement must be substantive — the customer actually requested it, not the seller; (2) the goods must be separately identified as belonging to the customer; (3) the goods must be ready for delivery; (4) the entity cannot use the goods or redirect them. In practice, management has enormous discretion in judging whether conditions (1) and (2) are met.

Bill and hold is harder to detect than channel stuffing because it appears in the notes to accounts rather than in headline financials. But the fingerprints are similar: inventory doesn't decline (goods haven't moved), receivables increase without a proportionate cash receipt, and the notes to accounts explicitly describe "bill and hold" arrangements that can be read by any investor willing to look at page 140 of the annual report.
🔴 How Bill & Hold Looks in the Data
📦
Revenue Rising But Inventory Not Falling
If revenue is genuine, goods leave the warehouse and inventory declines. If inventory stays high while revenue rises, goods haven't moved. Screener Balance Sheet: track Inventory Days alongside Revenue growth.
📋
Notes to Accounts Disclosure
IND-AS 115 requires disclosure of bill and hold arrangements in the annual report. Annual report (BSE filing) → Notes to Accounts → Revenue Recognition Policy. If company discloses bill and hold — read the exact conditions and verify they're met.
💸
Revenue Backloaded to Quarter End
A company doing legitimate bill and hold has contracts to show. If revenue is unusually concentrated on the last 3–5 days of a quarter (visible from delivery/despatch records in annual reports), it suggests transactions were structured specifically to make the quarter.
✅ How to Detect Bill & Hold — 3-Step Annual Report Check
1
Revenue recognition policy: Annual report (BSE/NSE filing via Screener Documents tab) → Notes to Financial Statements → Note 1 or Note 2: Significant Accounting Policies → Revenue Recognition. Does the company mention bill and hold? If yes, read the exact conditions they claim to meet.
2
Inventory days vs revenue growth: Screener Ratios tab → Inventory Turnover Days. For a company reporting 20% revenue growth, inventory days should decline or stay stable (more sales with same stock = better turnover). Rising inventory days alongside rising revenue = goods aren't moving despite invoices being raised.
3
Contract asset/liability balance: Balance Sheet → look for "Contract Assets" (revenue recognised but not billed) and "Contract Liabilities" (cash received but revenue not yet recognised). A rapidly growing Contract Assets balance — especially near year end — suggests aggressive revenue pull-forward that may reverse next period.

Technique #3 — Software, Services, Subscription Businesses
Aggressive Revenue Recognition
Recognising multi-year contracts as current-year revenue, booking milestone payments before milestones are achieved, or recognising subscription revenue upfront instead of rateably over the contract period. Creates revenue today that will need to reverse tomorrow.
🔍 Screener: Deferred Revenue + Contract Liabilities Trend
A software company signs a 3-year contract worth ₹90 crore with a large enterprise client. Under proper revenue recognition (IND-AS 115), the company should recognise ₹30 crore per year. But the CFO argues that the performance obligation was "substantially fulfilled" upfront (setting up the software), so they recognise ₹70 crore in year 1 and only ₹10 crore each in years 2 and 3.

The result: Year 1 revenue looks spectacular. Year 2 and Year 3 are weak "for reasons we're working through." The management's discretion in determining when a "performance obligation is satisfied" creates enormous room for revenue timing manipulation. The tell-tale sign: a rapidly declining deferred revenue balance (or a Contract Liabilities balance that shrinks while new contracts are being signed) suggests revenue is being pulled forward aggressively.
📉
Falling Deferred Revenue
Screener Balance Sheet: if Deferred Revenue (advance payments from customers not yet earned) is declining while total revenue is growing, revenue recognition is accelerating — sometimes aggressively so.
📈
Contract Assets Growing
Contract Assets = revenue recognised but customer hasn't been billed yet. Growing Contract Assets means revenue is being recognised before invoices are raised — aggressive timing. Balance Sheet → Other Current Assets → Contract Assets.
🔄
Backlog / Remaining Performance Obligations
IND-AS 115 Note disclosure: "remaining performance obligations" shows revenue contracted but not yet recognised. If this declines faster than new bookings arrive, the company is burning through its revenue backlog — not growing.
✅ Key Ratios to Track for All Three Techniques
1
DSO (Debtor Days): Receivables ÷ (Revenue/365). Rising DSO = customers taking longer to pay = either credit extension (channel stuffing) or bill and hold unpaid. Screener Ratios tab → Debtor Days — compare 5-year trend.
2
CFO/PAT ratio: Operating Cash Flow ÷ Net Profit. Should be above 0.85× for most businesses. Below 0.7× for 2+ years = revenue recognition outrunning cash reality. Cash Flow tab on Screener.
3
Revenue per day in Q4 vs rest of year: Quarterly Results tab → (Q4 revenue ÷ 90 days) vs (Q1+Q2+Q3 revenue ÷ 275 days). If Q4 daily revenue is 40%+ above the rest-of-year average for 2+ consecutive years, investigate channel stuffing or end-of-year revenue pushing.

Test 1: DSO Trend (5-Year)
Screener → Ratios

Screener → Cash Flow
The gold standard. Real revenue generates real cash. CFO/PAT below 0.7× for 2+ years = revenue is not converting to cash. Calculate: Operating CFO ÷ Net Profit after tax. Target: above 0.9× for quality companies.

Test 3: Q4/Q1 Revenue Ratio
Screener → Quarterly
Q4 revenue ÷ Q1 revenue of same fiscal year. Above 1.5× consistently for 3+ years without seasonal explanation = channel stuffing signal. Compare with peers in same sector — if only one company shows this pattern, it's suspicious.
Q4/Q1 ratio = Q4 Revenue ÷ Q1 Revenue

Test 4: Inventory Turnover Days
Screener → Ratios
If revenue grows 20% but inventory days rise from 45 to 70 — goods are not moving despite invoices being raised. This is the bill and hold fingerprint. Inventory Days rising while revenue also rising = investigate immediately.

Test 5: Revenue vs Receivables Growth
Screener → Balance Sheet
Revenue and receivables should grow at approximately the same rate for a stable business. If receivables grow 40% while revenue grows 15%, credit is being extended — either to enable channel stuffing or because customers can't pay. Simple 5-year trend check.

Annual Report → Notes
Read the Note on Revenue Recognition in the Annual Report (BSE filing via Screener Documents). Any mention of "bill and hold," "extended credit terms," "right of return provisions," or "variable consideration" requires deeper investigation of the underlying transactions.

Test 7: Bad Debt / Provision Trend
P&L + Balance Sheet
Channel stuffing always eventually produces bad debts — distributors who can't pay for goods they didn't want. A rising "provision for doubtful debts" in the P&L, especially appearing 1–2 years after a revenue spike, is the retrospective confirmation of earlier channel stuffing.

⚡ Quick Tricks

The 10-Minute Revenue Quality Cheat Sheet

No spreadsheets. No financial models. These are fast, visual, one-glance tricks that any investor can do in under 10 minutes on Screener.in and the BSE website — spotting channel stuffing and bill & hold before reading a single word of the annual report.

⚡
Trick #1 — 60 Seconds · Screener.in Ratios Tab
The "DSO Straight Line" Trick
Takes 60 sec
Go to Screener.in → Search any company → Click Ratios tab → Find "Debtor Days" row → Look at 5 years of numbers from left to right.

Draw an imaginary straight line through those 5 numbers in your head. If the line goes upward (DSO rising year on year while revenue also rises) — that's the channel stuffing fingerprint. If the line is flat or falling — revenue quality is clean for this test.

The ideal: a company growing revenue 15% per year whose DSO stays flat or falls. That means customers are paying faster (or the same) even as the company sells more. This is only possible if the revenue is real and customers actually want the goods.
✅ Safe Pattern
FY20: 38 · FY21: 36 · FY22: 40 · FY23: 37 · FY24: 35
Flat / slightly falling DSO despite revenue growing = customers paying normally. No credit being extended to push inventory.
🚨 Warning Pattern
FY20: 42 · FY21: 58 · FY22: 79 · FY23: 112 · FY24: 143
Rising DSO every year = distributor channel filling up with goods that aren't moving. The receivables are piling up because nobody's paying. Run the next tricks immediately.

Trick #2 — 90 Seconds · Cash Flow Tab
The "Two Number" CFO/PAT Trick
Takes 90 sec
Screener.in → Cash Flow tab → Find two numbers: "Net Profit" and "Cash from Operations" for the same year → Divide Cash from Operations by Net Profit → That's your CFO/PAT ratio. Do this for 3 years.

Rule: above 0.85× = safe. Below 0.7× for 2+ years = investigate immediately.

Why it works: real revenue always converts to real cash within 30–90 days. If profit is ₹100 Cr but cash generated is only ₹30 Cr — those other ₹70 Cr of "profit" are sitting as unpaid receivables (channel stuffing) or undelivered invoices (bill and hold). You don't need to know anything else — just these two numbers divided.

Trick #3 — 2 Minutes · Quarterly Tab
The "Q4 Spike / Q1 Crash" Chart Trick
Takes 2 min
Screener.in → Quarterly tab → Look at the bar chart of revenue (or just the numbers) → Compare Q3 and Q4 to Q1 of the next year. Channel stuffing always shows the same visual pattern: a jump in Q4 followed by a dip in Q1. It looks like a wave — up, then crash, then recover.

The key question: does the peer group show the same pattern? If all paint companies show higher Q4 revenue (due to pre-monsoon season), that's legitimate seasonality. If only one company shows a 45% Q4 spike while peers are flat — that one company is the stuffing suspect.

One-sentence rule: if Q4 revenue is 35%+ above the Q1–Q3 quarterly average for 3+ years running, and the sector doesn't show the same pattern — channel stuffing is happening.

Trick #4 — 3 Minutes · Annual Report PDF (BSE Filing)
The "Ctrl+F Bill and Hold" Trick
Takes 3 min
Screener.in → Documents tab → Download the latest Annual Report PDF → Press Ctrl+F (or Cmd+F on Mac / search icon on mobile) → Search these exact phrases one by one. If any appear — read that section immediately:

This trick works because IND-AS 115 requires companies to disclose their revenue recognition judgements. Any company using bill and hold, right of return, or extended payment must describe it. Most investors never read Notes to Accounts — which is exactly why finding these terms gives you an information edge.
"bill and hold"
If found → company has undelivered goods billed as revenue. Read the specific note to see if the 4 IND-AS 115 conditions are met.
"right of return"
If found → distributors can return goods. Revenue should be estimated net of expected returns. Are returns being understated?
"extended credit"
If found → company gave customers more time to pay. Why? Legitimate business reason, or to enable channel stuffing in the current quarter?
"variable consideration"
Revenue where the amount isn't fixed yet (rebates, price adjustments). Companies can underestimate constraints and recognise more revenue now.
"contract assets"
Revenue recognised before the invoice is raised. Growing Contract Assets = aggressive recognition. Check Balance Sheet for this line item.
"significant financing"
Revenue from contracts with payment terms >12 months must be adjusted for financing. A tell-tale sign of bill and hold with very long payment terms.
Zero hits = best outcome. A Note on Revenue Recognition that is 2–3 paragraphs long and mentions only "point-in-time recognition on delivery" = clean, simple, low-risk revenue model. A note that is 8 paragraphs long with multiple conditions, estimates, and judgements = higher complexity = higher manipulation surface area.

The Revenue Quality Master Rule
Revenue That Doesn't Convert to Cash Within 90–120 Days Is Risk, Not Profit
Every example in the "bad" section had the same underlying characteristic: revenue was recognised on the income statement long before cash arrived — and in many cases, the cash never arrived at all. The accounting rules allow this gap. Revenue quality analysis exists to close it.

The practical rule: for any company you own or are considering, check CFO/PAT on Screener.in for the last 5 years. If it is consistently above 0.85×, the revenue quality is likely genuine. If it has been below 0.7× for 2+ years, investigate the specific cause — is it a commodity working capital cycle (like Polycab's copper year), a legitimate deferred revenue model, or are receivables building because customers aren't paying?

The second check is DSO trend. A company with genuinely increasing customer demand does not need to extend credit terms to make sales. Stable or declining DSO alongside growing revenue is the revenue quality hallmark. Rising DSO alongside rising revenue is the channel stuffing fingerprint that preceded every major FMCG, pharma, and industrial revenue fraud in the last 20 years — from Manpasand to Enron's bandwidth trading desk.

Run these two checks — CFO/PAT and DSO trend — on every holding in your portfolio every quarter. Both numbers are free on Screener.in. It takes 10 minutes per stock. It's the difference between owning businesses and owning accounting entries.
