01 — Key Learning Outcome

Why Two Financial Statements Tell Two Very Different Stories

🔍
Your Core Takeaway
Every listed Indian company files two sets of financial statements — Standalone (the parent company alone) and Consolidated (the parent plus all its subsidiaries, associates, and joint ventures). The gap between these two statements is where some of the most important — and dangerous — information in investing lives.
Imagine a parent company — call it ABC Ltd. Its standalone financial statements show ₹800 crore profit, zero debt, strong cash flows. Management is rewarded, the stock rises. But ABC Ltd has 12 subsidiaries. Three of them are losing money — ₹200 crore per year in aggregate. Five have taken bank loans that don't appear on ABC's standalone balance sheet. The consolidated financial statements show all of this — if you know where to look. But many investors read only the standalone numbers from the press release and miss the full picture.

The relationship between parent and subsidiary is governed by Ind AS 110 (Consolidated Financial Statements), Ind AS 111 (Joint Arrangements), and Ind AS 28 (Investments in Associates). These standards determine which entities get fully consolidated (subsidiaries where parent has control), which get equity-accounted (associates with significant influence), and which remain at cost (minority holdings).

Three critical concepts to master: (1) Minority Interest / Non-Controlling Interest (NCI) — when the parent owns less than 100% of a subsidiary, the minority shareholders' share of profits and net assets shows up separately in consolidated financials; (2) Standalone vs Consolidated divergence — large gaps between the two reveal hidden subsidiary problems; (3) Guarantee exposure — a parent may guarantee a subsidiary's loans, creating contingent liabilities that don't appear on the balance sheet until the subsidiary defaults.
📊 Ind AS 110: Consolidated accounts 🏢 Standalone: Parent company only ⚠️ Minority interest = NCI impact 💰 Contingent liability = loan guarantees
02 — The Corporate Structure Explained Simply

Parent, Subsidiaries & What Each Statement Shows

Think of a parent company as a family home and each subsidiary as a separate house the family owns. The standalone accounts show only the family home's finances. The consolidated accounts show the finances of every house the family owns — including the ones that are run-down, mortgaged, or rented to questionable tenants.

The parent ABC Ltd looks profitable in standalone (₹800 Cr PAT, zero debt). But the consolidated statement reveals Sub C is carrying ₹1,500 Cr of debt and losing ₹220 Cr per year — invisible in standalone. Sub B's losses are partially absorbed by minority shareholders (NCI). Consolidated PAT of ₹480 Cr vs standalone PAT of ₹800 Cr — a ₹320 Cr gap that only appears when you compare both statements.

⚠️ Minority Interest / Non-Controlling Interest (NCI) — Explained in Plain Language
When ABC Ltd owns 65% of Sub B, the other 35% belongs to "minority shareholders" — people who own the sub directly but not through ABC Ltd. When ABC consolidates Sub B into its accounts, it must include 100% of Sub B's assets, liabilities, and revenue (because ABC controls Sub B). But in the P&L, it must separately show the 35% of profit (or loss) that belongs to minority shareholders.

This 35% is called Minority Interest (old term) or Non-Controlling Interest/NCI (Ind AS terminology). It shows up in two places:
• In the Consolidated P&L: "Profit attributable to non-controlling interests" — subtracted to get profit "attributable to owners of the parent"
• In the Consolidated Balance Sheet: As a separate line in equity — "Non-controlling interests" — representing the minority's share of the subsidiary's net assets
NCI in P&L
Sub B loses ₹80 Cr. 35% = ₹28 Cr absorbed by NCI. ABC's share = ₹52 Cr loss. Consolidated PAT reduced by ₹52 Cr (not full ₹80 Cr).
NCI in Balance Sheet
Sub B has net assets ₹500 Cr. NCI = 35% × ₹500 Cr = ₹175 Cr shown in consolidated equity. This is "minority's skin in the game."
Why NCI Matters
Rising NCI as % of consolidated equity = parent ownership diluting in subs. Shrinking NCI = parent buying out minorities (or sub losing value).
Four Subsidiary Risk Structures Every Investor Must Recognise
🚨
Subsidiary Debt Trap
Biggest Risk
The parent borrows nothing — but its subsidiary has ₹5,000 crore of loans. These don't appear on the standalone balance sheet. When the subsidiary struggles to repay, the parent often provides "letters of comfort," upstream loans, or eventually guarantees — transforming a subsidiary problem into a parent crisis. Screener: compare standalone debt to consolidated debt. Any large gap = subsidiary debt exposure.
⚠️
Minority Interest Erosion
NCI Warning
When a subsidiary with significant minority shareholders continuously loses money, the minority interest (NCI) on the consolidated balance sheet shrinks — because losses are being absorbed by NCI. Eventually NCI hits zero; after that, all further losses fall entirely on the parent. Check: is NCI declining each year relative to the subsidiary's revenue? Declining NCI = minority shareholders being eroded by losses.
🔄
Related Party Circular Revenue
Revenue Inflation
Parent company "buys" services from a subsidiary at inflated prices — or sells goods to a subsidiary at below-market prices. These transactions inflate the parent's standalone revenue or the subsidiary's reported revenue, but at consolidation, inter-company transactions are eliminated. Large related-party revenues that disappear in consolidation reveal circular revenue structures. Check Notes → Related Party Transactions annually.
💎
Hidden Gem Subsidiary
Positive Signal
Sometimes a subsidiary that is growing rapidly and highly profitable is being consolidated but not separately highlighted by management — because they don't want competitors or potential acquirers to notice it. Investors who read the segment note or subsidiary schedule (Schedule of Subsidiaries in the consolidated accounts) can identify these hidden gems before they are widely recognised. This is the positive side of subsidiary analysis.
Six Specific Warning Signs in Subsidiary Analysis
📉
Consolidated PAT < Standalone PAT
Screener: Compare Toggle
When consolidated profit is consistently lower than standalone profit — subsidiaries are losing money. Calculate the gap: Standalone PAT minus Consolidated PAT = subsidiary losses absorbed by parent. If this gap exceeds 15% of standalone PAT for 2+ years, the subsidiary drag is material. Screener.in makes this comparison trivial — toggle between consolidated and standalone views.
💳
Consolidated Debt >> Standalone Debt
Balance Sheet Gap
If consolidated borrowings are 3× or more than standalone borrowings — subsidiaries are the primary borrowers. This debt is real and covenant-attached even though it doesn't appear on the parent's standalone sheet. Check if parent has guaranteed any of these loans (Notes → Contingent Liabilities → Corporate Guarantees). If yes, parent is on the hook if subsidiary defaults.
🔀
Subsidiaries Added or Removed
Schedule of Subsidiaries
Compare the "Schedule of Subsidiaries" (listed in the consolidated annual report) year over year. New subsidiaries added in Q4 that inflated year-end consolidation revenue is a red flag. Subsidiaries quietly removed — especially those with losses — suggest the company is deconsolidating problems to clean up the consolidated number. Any movement requires explanation.
🔃
Upstream Dividend from Loss-Making Sub
Income Inflation
A subsidiary paying dividends to the parent while itself reporting losses is using past retained earnings or debt to fund the dividend. This inflates the parent's standalone income (dividend income is income in standalone) while the subsidiary deteriorates. Check: standalone "Other Income" → dividend from subsidiaries. Compare to subsidiary's own PAT. If subsidiary PAT is negative but dividend is flowing up — investigate.
💱
Goodwill Growth on Consolidation
Acquisition Premium Risk
When a parent acquires a subsidiary at a premium to book value, the excess is recognised as "Goodwill" on the consolidated balance sheet. Rising goodwill with stagnant or declining subsidiary profitability → impairment risk. Under Ind AS 36, goodwill must be tested annually for impairment. Companies that consistently avoid impairment despite clearly struggling acquisitions are over-stating consolidated assets.
📋
Step Acquisition / Deconsolidation Gains
One-Time Profit
When a parent increases its stake in a subsidiary from below 50% (equity method) to above 50% (full consolidation), accounting rules require recognising a "deemed disposal gain" — a one-time profit. Similarly, losing control of a subsidiary creates a large gain or loss. These are non-cash accounting entries that inflate consolidated PAT without any cash being generated. Look for "exceptional items" in consolidated P&L.

Each SPV borrowed independently — parent guaranteed silently
IL&FS structured its infrastructure projects as separate Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs). Each SPV borrowed from banks independently for its project. Consolidated-to-standalone debt ratio was the signal
Had investors consistently compared consolidated debt to standalone debt for IL&FS Financial Services (the listed entity), the massive gap would have been visible. The listed entity's standalone debt was manageable; the consolidated group debt was catastrophic. Every year this gap widened, the risk grew. The signal was in the comparison — investors who read only the standalone missed the iceberg.
🚨
NCI structure hid cross-default risk
Many of IL&FS's subsidiaries had external investors (through NCI structures) who were promised specific returns. When projects failed to generate adequate cash flows, IL&FS parent had to upstream funds to subsidiaries to meet these commitments — creating a cashflow drain at parent level that didn't appear as "expense" in any normal accounting line.
🚨 The IL&FS subsidiary lesson: 347 entities with aggregate debt 30× the parent's standalone debt = the parent's standalone balance sheet was completely misleading. The forensic check: consolidated debt ÷ standalone debt. For IL&FS, this ratio was dramatically above 10× for years. A ratio above 3× warrants investigation; above 5× for a non-manufacturing company is a serious red flag. The tool to catch it: Screener's consolidated vs standalone toggle — takes 30 seconds.

Offshore subsidiaries as "customers" for circular revenue
Gitanjali Gems had offshore subsidiary and affiliated entities that appeared in its books as export customers. The parent would "export" jewellery to these affiliated offshore entities, raise invoices, recognise revenue, and use fraudulent PNB Letters of Undertaking (LOUs) to finance the "receivables" from these entities. The offshore entities then recycled the money back. The subsidiaries were not genuine customers — they were conduits for a circular financing scheme.
🚨
Related Party Transaction note was the available signal
In Gitanjali's annual report Notes → Related Party Transactions: the notes disclosed large "export sales" to affiliated entities. These transactions were large in scale, showed very long DSO (180+ days — export customers that aren't paying), and the affiliated entities had the same promoter group. Investors who read the Related Party Note could see that "export revenue" to related offshore entities was a very large portion of total revenue — this is the circular revenue signal.
🚨
Consolidated accounts that should have eliminated the circularity didn't
If the offshore "customers" were true subsidiaries of Gitanjali, the inter-company sales should have been eliminated on consolidation. But they were structured as "associates" or third-party affiliates — close enough to control that the revenue was real from the promoter's perspective, but far enough to avoid consolidation elimination. This is the grey zone of subsidiary accounting that enables fraud.
🚨 The circular subsidiary revenue forensic check: For any export-heavy company — check (1) Related Party Transactions Note: are a significant % of exports to related/affiliated entities? (2) DSO of those related-party receivables: are they aging beyond 90 days? (3) Are the offshore "customers" associates or JVs that should be eliminated on consolidation but aren't? Three positive answers = circular revenue risk. This check takes 5 minutes in the annual report.

Subsidiary used to route loans to promoter-related entities
CG Power's forensic audit (conducted after the board identified suspicious transactions) revealed that subsidiary companies had been used to disburse loans to entities connected to the promoter group. These loans appeared in the subsidiary books as "advances" or "receivables" — normal-looking accounting entries. At consolidation, they appeared as inter-company receivables that should have been eliminated — but since the recipient entities were not always subsidiaries, the transactions stayed in the consolidated accounts as third-party exposures.
🚨
The advances-to-related-parties forensic check
CG Power's annual reports had a growing "Advances" line in the consolidated balance sheet — money given out but not yet received back. The nature of these advances (to whom? for what?) was disclosed in the Related Party Note but not prominently highlighted. Investors who tracked the "Advances to Related Parties" line over 3–4 years would have seen it growing faster than the business — a forensic signal that money was leaving the group through the subsidiary structure.
🚨
Promoter pledge + related party advances = double red flag
CG Power had significant promoter pledge (Avantha Group promoters had pledged shares) simultaneous with growing related-party advances in consolidated accounts. High promoter pledge + growing advances to related parties = money is possibly being extracted from the company through the subsidiary route to fund the promoter's personal debt. This combination — observable in BSE shareholding data (pledge) + Notes to accounts (advances) — is one of the clearest forensic patterns in Indian corporate governance failures.
🚨 The CG Power subsidiary forensic check: The combination of (1) growing "advances to related parties" in consolidated balance sheet, (2) significant promoter pledging visible in quarterly shareholding reports, and (3) CFO/PAT consistently below 0.7× was visible 2–3 years before the forensic audit discovery. Each signal individually is a yellow flag; all three together is a red flag that demands immediate investigation of the subsidiary structure.

⚡ Quick Tricks

The 8-Minute Subsidiary Risk Cheat Sheet

Five fast checks — using only Screener.in, BSE shareholding data, and annual report PDF search. Run them in order. Any single trigger warrants investigation; multiple triggers together demand it.

🔄
Trick #1 — 60 Seconds · Screener.in Toggle
The "Consolidated vs Standalone Toggle" — Find the Gap Instantly
60 seconds
Screener.in → Search company → Financials tab → Top of the page there is a toggle: "Consolidated / Standalone". Switch between them. Write down two numbers for the same year: PAT (Consolidated) and PAT (Standalone). Then do the same for Total Borrowings.

Calculate:
PAT Gap = Standalone PAT − Consolidated PAT → If positive and large, subsidiaries are losing money.
Debt Gap = Consolidated Borrowings − Standalone Borrowings → If large, subsidiaries are the borrowers.
🚨 Warning Thresholds
PAT gap >20% of standalone PAT = subsidiary losses are material
Debt gap >3× standalone debt = subsidiaries are the primary borrowers
Both triggers simultaneously = deep investigate required
✅ Clean Signal
Consolidated PAT > Standalone PAT = subsidiaries are adding value (good)
Debt gap <2× standalone = subsidiary debt manageable
Both clean = move on, no concern
📊
Trick #2 — 90 Seconds · Consolidated Balance Sheet
The "NCI Trend" Check — Is Minority Interest Growing or Eroding?
90 seconds
Screener.in → Balance Sheet tab (consolidated) → Find "Non-Controlling Interests" or "Minority Interest" in the Equity section. Track this number for 3 years.

Growing NCI = subsidiaries with minority shareholders are profitable — minority equity growing with retained earnings = healthy.
Declining or negative NCI = subsidiaries losing money year after year — minority equity being eroded by losses. When NCI hits zero, all future losses fall entirely on the parent.
1
Open consolidated Balance Sheet on Screener → Equity section → Find "Non-Controlling Interests" (sometimes called "Minority Interest")
2
Compare 3 years: FY2022 → FY2023 → FY2024. Is the trend up, flat, or down?
3
If declining: cross-check consolidated P&L → "Profit attributable to Non-Controlling Interests" → is it negative (NCI absorbing losses)? If yes — subsidiaries with minority partners are losing money.
4
Calculate: NCI ÷ Total Equity. If declining as a ratio over 3 years AND the company hasn't bought out minority shareholders → subsidiary losses are the explanation.
🔎
Trick #3 — 2 Minutes · Annual Report PDF Ctrl+F
The "Advances to Related Parties" Trap — Find Growing Leakage
2 minutes
Download Annual Report PDF from Screener Documents → Press Ctrl+F → Search "loans to related" or "advances to subsidiaries" or "amounts due from".

Find the Related Party Transactions table (mandatory under Ind AS 24). Look for loans or advances from the parent to subsidiaries or related entities. Track this number over 3 years. Rising advances = money leaving the group through the subsidiary structure. Always ask: what is the business purpose, and is it being repaid?
🚨 Red Flag Patterns
Advances growing faster than revenue = money leaving the group
Advances to entities with same promoter = potential extraction
Advances aging >1 year without repayment = unlikely to return
✅ Clean Patterns
Advances declining = being repaid — healthy
Advances for specific capex projects with defined return = legitimate
Inter-company advances eliminated in consolidation = no net group exposure
📋
Trick #4 — 90 Seconds · BSE Shareholding + Annual Report
The "Promoter Pledge + Subsidiary Advances" Double Red Flag
90 seconds
BSE/NSE → Shareholding Pattern (Regulation 31, filed quarterly) → Check "Promoter Pledged Shares %". Cross-reference with Annual Report → Related Party Transactions → Advances to related entities.

When a promoter has pledged significant shares AND their company is simultaneously advancing money to related entities through subsidiaries — the probability of extraction/tunneling is high. The promoter is under financial pressure (hence the pledge) and may be using the listed company's subsidiaries to access liquidity.
📊 Combined Risk Scoring
A
Promoter pledge >20% = financial distress signal (BSE quarterly)
B
Related party advances growing = money leaving via subsidiaries (Annual Report)
C
CFO/PAT below 0.7× = cash not tracking profit (Screener Cash Flow)
If A + B + C all present: Extremely high probability of value extraction via subsidiary structure. Exit or short position. CG Power, DHFL, Gitanjali all showed this pattern before collapse.

Trick #5 — The 8-Minute Master Subsidiary Checklist
Consolidated vs Standalone — Complete Risk Assessment in 8 Minutes
8 minutes
Run these five checks in sequence for any company with multiple subsidiaries. A single green doesn't mean safe; a single red doesn't always mean fraud. But multiple reds together demand you stop and investigate before investing.
1️⃣
Screener Toggle Test (60 sec)
PAT gap >20%? Debt gap >3×? → Flag for investigation
2️⃣
NCI Trend Check (90 sec)
Consolidated balance sheet → NCI 3-year trend → Declining? Sub losses are real.
3️⃣
Related Party Advances Ctrl+F (2 min)
Annual report PDF → "loans to related" → Growing over 3 years? Extraction risk.
4️⃣
Promoter Pledge Check (90 sec)
BSE shareholding → Promoter pledged % → >20% = distress signal → combine with Trick 3.
5️⃣
Schedule of Subsidiaries Check (2 min)
Annual report → "Subsidiaries" schedule → Compare current to prior year. New entities added in Q4? Entities quietly removed? Either requires explanation.
🏆 All 5 checks in 8 minutes = full subsidiary risk picture
Zero red flags across all 5 checks = subsidiary structure is likely clean. Move on to business analysis. One or two flags = note and monitor quarterly. Three or more flags = do not invest until the subsidiary structure is understood in full detail, including reading the Related Party Transactions note and the complete Schedule of Subsidiaries in the annual report.
⚡
The One-Line Consolidated vs Standalone Rule
Always Read Consolidated. The Gap Between Consolidated and Standalone Is the Risk. Large Gap = Large Investigation.
Every press release, most analyst summaries, and many investor presentations highlight standalone numbers — because they often look better. Subsidiary debt doesn't appear. Subsidiary losses don't appear. Related-party loans to affiliated entities don't appear. The parent looks clean, profitable, and debt-free.

The consolidated financial statement is the economic truth. It includes every subsidiary's assets, liabilities, revenues, and losses. If a company has subsidiaries — which every listed Indian company of any size does — the consolidated statement is the relevant document for investment analysis. Standalone analysis is useful only for specific purposes (dividend coverage, parent entity asset quality, or when subsidiaries are genuinely immaterial).

The Screener.in toggle between consolidated and standalone takes 10 seconds. The gap it reveals — in PAT, in debt, in ROCE — is the starting point for every subsidiary risk analysis. IL&FS, Jet Airways, Gitanjali Gems, CG Power, and Vodafone Idea all had telling gaps between their consolidated and standalone statements — visible, free, and available to any investor willing to look. The information was never hidden; it was simply not being read by most.
⚡ Quick Tricks

The 8-Minute Subsidiary Risk Cheat Sheet

Five fast, practical checks — using only free tools (Screener.in, BSE website, annual report PDF) — that give you a complete subsidiary risk picture in under 8 minutes. Run them before analysing any company with multiple subsidiaries.

🔄
Trick #1 — 60 Seconds · Screener.in Toggle
The "PAT Gap + Debt Gap" — Two Numbers That Reveal Everything
60 seconds
Screener.in → Search any company → Financials tab → At the top you will see "Consolidated / Standalone" toggle. Click each and write down two numbers:

1. Net Profit (PAT) — both consolidated and standalone, same year
2. Total Borrowings — both consolidated and standalone, same year

Then divide each: Consolidated ÷ Standalone. These two ratios tell you immediately whether subsidiaries are a risk source or a value creator — before reading a single word of the annual report.
✅ Healthy Ratios
Consol PAT ÷ Standalone PAT ≥ 1.0
Subsidiaries are adding profit — consolidated is better than standalone. Clean structure.
Consol Debt ÷ Standalone Debt < 2×
Subsidiary borrowings manageable relative to parent. No hidden debt iceberg.
🚨 Warning Ratios
Consol PAT ÷ Standalone PAT < 0.8
Subsidiaries losing 20%+ of parent's profit. Gap = subsidiary losses absorbed by group every year.
Consol Debt ÷ Standalone Debt > 3×
Subsidiaries are the primary borrowers. Standalone balance sheet is misleading.
Historical examples: IL&FS Consolidated Debt ÷ Standalone Debt exceeded 15× for years. Motherson Consolidated Revenue = 11.4× standalone. Any ratio above 5× demands reading the subsidiary schedule in the annual report to understand what entities are driving the gap.
📊
Trick #2 — 90 Seconds · Screener Consolidated Balance Sheet
The "NCI Straight Line" — Track Minority Interest Over 3 Years
90 seconds
Screener.in → Balance Sheet (Consolidated) → Equity section → Find "Non-Controlling Interests" or "Minority Interest". Write down the value for FY2022, FY2023, FY2024. Draw an imaginary straight line through the three numbers.

NCI behaves like a thermometer for subsidiary health — it goes up when subsidiaries earn and down when they lose. You don't need to read a single accounting note to interpret it.
📈
NCI Growing Year-on-Year
Subsidiaries with minority partners are earning money. Minority equity increases through retained profits. Example: Bajaj Finance NCI growing as the company compounds profitably. Green flag — subsidiaries are healthy.
➡️
NCI Flat (No Change)
Check if the company has bought out minority shareholders (check Notes for "acquisition of NCI"). If no buyout — subsidiaries with minority partners are breaking even or not growing. Watch closely next year.
📉
NCI Declining or Approaching Zero
Subsidiaries with minority shareholders are losing money. NCI equity is being eroded by losses absorbed by minority. When NCI hits zero — all future losses fall 100% on the parent. Vodafone Idea showed this pattern for 3 consecutive years before its equity crisis. Red flag — act now.
Quick math: If NCI declined by ₹X from FY2023 to FY2024 without a buyout — then ₹X ÷ (1 − parent's ownership %) is approximately the subsidiary's total loss for the year attributable to the NCI portion. Scale this up to find the total subsidiary loss impact on consolidated P&L.
🔎
Trick #3 — 2 Minutes · Annual Report PDF Ctrl+F
The "Ctrl+F Related Party" — Find Money Flowing Through Subsidiary Routes
2 minutes
Download Annual Report PDF from Screener → Documents tab → Press Ctrl+F (or ⌘F on Mac, search icon on mobile) and search these exact phrases one by one. The Related Party Transactions Note (mandated under Ind AS 24) is where subsidiary-related money flows are disclosed.
"loans to subsidiaries"
Money lent from parent to subs. If growing year on year and not being repaid — potential extraction or sub funding losses.
"corporate guarantees"
Parent guaranteeing sub's bank loans. If sub defaults, parent pays. Check total guarantee amount vs parent net worth.
"advances to related"
Advances to promoter-affiliated entities via subsidiary routes. Compare to prior year — growing = red flag.
"dividend from subsidiary"
Upstream dividend. If sub is loss-making but still paying dividend — it's using past reserves or debt to fund the parent. Investigate.
"amount due from"
Track outstanding amounts between parent and subs. Stable or declining = normal. Growing = investigate what's outstanding and why.
"management fees"
Parent charging subs for services. If excessive vs sub's own revenue — potential value extraction from minority shareholders of sub.
Zero hits on the red terms = best outcome. A Related Party note where transactions between parent and subsidiaries are only routine (management services, common employee costs, treasury operations) is clean. When you find loans, guarantees, and advances all growing simultaneously — that combination demands deep investigation before investing.
📋
Trick #4 — 90 Seconds · BSE Website + Screener
The "Promoter Pledge + CFO/PAT" Triple Alarm System
90 seconds
Three signals — each visible in 30 seconds each — that together form the most reliable early warning system for subsidiary-driven financial distress. When all three are present simultaneously, the probability of hidden subsidiary problems is very high.
A
Promoter Pledge >20% (BSE: Shareholding → Reg 31)
BSE → Company → Shareholding Pattern → Latest filing → "Shares pledged or otherwise encumbered." Above 20% = promoter under financial pressure. Above 50% = extreme distress. Free, public, updated quarterly.
B
Consolidated CFO/PAT <0.7× (Screener: Cash Flow)
Screener → Cash Flow tab (Consolidated) → Cash from Operations ÷ Net Profit. Below 0.7× = profit not converting to cash. Subsidiary losses or related-party cash extraction often show up here before they appear anywhere else.
C
Growing "Other Current Assets" in Consolidated (Screener: Balance Sheet)
Screener → Balance Sheet (Consolidated) → "Other Current Assets." This line often hides advances to subsidiaries, loans to related parties, and inter-company receivables. Growing faster than revenue = money accumulating outside the normal business cycle.
A + B + C all present = triple alarm. CG Power had all three in FY2018–19. DHFL had all three. Gitanjali had all three. Any single signal = yellow flag, monitor quarterly. All three together = stop, investigate, and do not invest until the subsidiary structure is fully understood through the annual report.
🏆
Trick #5 — The Master Check · Annual Report Schedule of Subsidiaries
The "Subsidiary Count + Goodwill" — How Many Entities and What Did You Pay For Them?
2 minutes
Annual Report → Ctrl+F "Schedule of Subsidiaries" or "Statement pursuant to Section 129". This schedule lists every subsidiary, associate, and joint venture — with ownership %, capital, reserves, revenue, PAT, and "proposed dividend." It takes 2 minutes to read and tells you the health of every entity in the group at a glance.

Simultaneously, check the Consolidated Balance Sheet for "Goodwill on Consolidation" — the premium paid above book value when subsidiaries were acquired. Large goodwill that isn't being tested for impairment despite deteriorating subsidiary performance = overstated assets.
📋 What to Check in Sub Schedule
1Which subs have negative reserves? (Accumulated losses)
2Which subs show zero or negative PAT for 2+ years?
3New subsidiaries added in Q4 that weren't there last year?
4Subsidiaries removed vs prior year? (Deconsolidation of problems?)
📊 Goodwill Warning Signs
AGoodwill >15% of Total Assets = large acquisition premium sitting on balance sheet
BGoodwill unchanged for 3 years despite acquired sub losing money = impairment avoided
CGoodwill impaired by large amount = management finally admits overpaid for acquisition

60s: Screener toggle → PAT gap and Debt gap ratios → flag if PAT <0.8× or Debt >3×
90s: NCI 3-year trend on consolidated balance sheet → declining = sub losses
2min: Ctrl+F annual report → "loans to subsidiaries" + "corporate guarantees" + "advances to related"
90s: BSE pledge data + consolidated CFO/PAT + "Other Current Assets" growth → triple alarm if all present
2min: Schedule of Subsidiaries → loss-making subs + goodwill check
Result: You now know whether the subsidiary structure is a value creator or a risk vehicle — before reading a single word of management commentary.

The One-Line Subsidiary Risk Rule
If Consolidated Debt is 3× Standalone Debt — Your Standalone Analysis Is Wrong. Full Stop.
The standalone balance sheet of any company with significant subsidiaries is an incomplete picture of that company's true financial position. Subsidiary debt is real debt — it comes with interest obligations, covenants, and maturity risk. Subsidiary losses are real value destruction — they reduce the group's equity year after year. Related-party advances through subsidiaries are real cash outflows — regardless of how they're classified.

The single most important financial habit for any serious investor in Indian equities: toggle to consolidated on Screener.in before reading any financial number. Calculate the PAT ratio (consolidated ÷ standalone) and the debt ratio (consolidated ÷ standalone). If both are close to 1.0 — the subsidiary structure is simple and clean. If either diverges significantly — the subsidiary structure is telling you something important that the standalone statement is hiding.

IL&FS had a debt ratio above 15×. The information was in the consolidated accounts. It was free. It was publicly filed. Nobody was reading it — or rather, nobody was comparing it to the standalone. The eight minutes these tricks take would have prevented enormous capital loss. The question is never whether the information exists. It always does. The question is whether you are looking.
