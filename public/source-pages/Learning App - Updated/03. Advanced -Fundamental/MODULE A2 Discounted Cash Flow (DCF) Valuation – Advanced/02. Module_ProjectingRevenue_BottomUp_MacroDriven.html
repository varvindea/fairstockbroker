01 — Key Learning Outcome

Build Granular Revenue Models by Segment and Product

By the end of this module you will be able to open a company’s P&L on Screener.in, identify its revenue segments, choose the right projection approach for each segment, and build a model that captures the true drivers of that business — not just a generic growth rate.

📈
Your Core Takeaway — Revenue is Not Just One Number
A company’s total revenue is the sum of many different businesses. Modelling it as one blended growth rate hides the real story — and makes the model dangerously inaccurate when one segment accelerates or collapses.
Imagine projecting ITC’s revenue as a single 10% growth rate. But ITC has cigarettes (stable, high-margin, slow-growth), FMCG (growing fast, low-margin), hotels (highly cyclical), agri-business (weather-dependent), and paperboards (industrial, capex-driven). Each segment has a completely different growth driver, margin profile, and risk. Blending them into one number produces a projection that is right for none of them. The Bottom-Up approach models each segment using its own specific driver — volume, price, capacity utilisation, market share — and then sums them up. The Macro-Driven approach starts with the total market size (GDP, sector growth) and applies a company-specific market share assumption. Both approaches are more accurate than a simple historical CAGR applied blindly.
📊 Bottom-Up: Volume × Price per segment 🌎 Macro: GDP × Penetration × Market Share 📋 Hybrid: Bottom-Up for known + Macro for new
02 — Two Approaches, One Goal

Bottom-Up vs Macro-Driven — How Each Works

Both approaches aim to project future revenue accurately, but they start from different places and suit different types of businesses. Understanding when to use each — and when to combine both — is the mark of a professional analyst.

🏭
Approach 1 — Granular & Precise
Bottom-Up Revenue Model
The Bottom-Up approach builds total revenue from the smallest unit of the business — a single product, service tier, store, customer, or geographic region. You model each segment individually using its own specific drivers (volume, price, capacity, subscribers, stores) and then sum them to get total revenue. This approach is more work but far more accurate because it forces you to understand the real mechanics of each part of the business.
Step 1: Identify all revenue segments — from Screener.in Annual Report or segment disclosures (product, geography, customer type).
Step 2: Find the unit driver for each segment — volume (units, subscribers, stores, beds) × price (ASP, ARPU, rate per unit).
Step 3: Project volume and price separately — volume from capacity/market share, price from inflation + mix + pricing power.
Step 4: Sum segments — total revenue = sum of all segment revenues. Check against historical Screener.in P&L data for sanity.
Step 5: Sensitivity test — which segment drives 80% of total revenue? That is where assumption accuracy matters most.
Best for: Consumer, FMCG, Manufacturing, SaaS Also: Hospitals, Hotels, Retail chains Output: Segment revenue × number of segments
💡 Real analogy: Think of it like planning a restaurant’s monthly revenue: 200 lunch covers × ₹400 average bill + 150 dinner covers × ₹700 average bill + 50 catering orders × ₹2,000 = ₹2,78,000/month. You built the total from the parts — that is Bottom-Up. If you just said “restaurant revenue grows 15% p.a.” you would miss that dinner is growing 30% while lunch is flat.
🌎
Approach 2 — Market-Anchored
Macro-Driven Revenue Model
The Macro-Driven approach starts with the total addressable market (TAM) and works down: TAM × company’s market share = company revenue. The TAM itself is projected using macro drivers: GDP growth, sector-specific growth rates, population trends, regulatory tailwinds, or penetration rates. This approach is faster to build and more useful for emerging markets or businesses where individual product-level data is unavailable or unreliable.
Step 1: Define the total addressable market (TAM) — e.g., India’s mutual fund AUM, total credit card spend, total telecom subscribers.
Step 2: Project TAM growth — use GDP growth × sector multiplier, or historical sector CAGR from AMFI, SEBI, RBI, TRAI data.
Step 3: Project market share — company’s current share + historical trend + management guidance on share gain or loss.
Step 4: Revenue = TAM × Market Share — simple multiplication gives projected revenue for each year.
Step 5: Cross-check with bottom-up — does the Macro result match what a Bottom-Up approach would give? Large gaps = investigation needed.
Best for: Banks, NBFCs, Telecom, Insurance Also: New sectors, Fintech, EV ecosystem Output: TAM × Market Share = Revenue
💡 Real analogy: India’s mutual fund industry AUM is ₹60 lakh crore and growing at 18% p.a. HDFC AMC has 11% market share, stable to rising. So projected HDFC AMC AUM in 3 years = ₹60L Cr × (1.18)^3 × 11% = ₹9.7 lakh crore. Revenue = AUM × Expense Ratio (0.38%). This Macro-Driven model is far more useful than trying to bottom-up model each fund’s individual inflows.
The 3-Segment Rule — 80% of Revenue Explained by 20% of Drivers
Every Company Has a Primary Driver, a Growth Segment, and a Tail.
In any revenue model, one segment typically drives 60–70% of total revenue and deserves 70% of your modelling effort. A second segment is the “growth engine” that will change the revenue mix over time. The rest are tail segments that can be modelled with simpler assumptions. Identify which is which before building the model.
Core Segment
60–70% of Revenue
Stable, mature, predictable. Model with historical CAGR + price inflation. E.g., ITC Cigarettes, Nestle Maggi
Growth Engine
20–30% of Revenue
Fast-growing, changing mix, needs Bottom-Up model. E.g., ITC FMCG, Polycab FMEG, TCS new tech
Tail Segments
5–15% of Revenue
Small, volatile, or new. Use Macro-Driven or simple flat growth. Too small to justify deep modelling

Step 1: Screener.in → P&L tab shows total consolidated revenue only. Step 2: Click the Annual Report link on Screener.in → open the PDF → search “Segment Revenue” or “Segment Information” (Note 35–45 in most reports). Step 3: Copy the segment-wise revenue table for 3–5 years. Build a separate tab in your model for each segment. Step 4: Sum segments to match total P&L revenue as a cross-check. If they match, your segment model is consistent with the reported consolidated figure.

📈 The Segment Driver Formula

Every revenue segment has a natural unit driver. Consumer: Volume (units/kg) × Realisation (₹/unit). B2B: Capacity (tonnes/MW) × Utilisation% × Price/unit. Subscription: Subscribers × ARPU. Retail: Stores × SSSG (Same-Store Sales Growth). Financial Services: AUM/Loan Book × Fee Rate/NIM. Hotel: Rooms × ARR × Occupancy. Find the natural unit for your segment, project volume and price separately, and multiply. This gives a more defensible projection than a single growth rate.

⏱ How to Project Volume vs Price Separately

Volume drivers: market growth rate (industry CAGR), capacity additions (management guidance), distribution expansion, penetration rate increase, competitive share gain/loss. Price drivers: historical price hike CAGR (5–10 year average), commodity cost pass-through, product mix upgrading (premiumisation), regulatory price controls. Projecting both separately lets you model scenarios: “Bear: volume flat, price +3%” vs “Bull: volume +8%, price +5%” — much more nuanced than “revenue +8% or +13%.”

⚠ The Revenue Model Red Flags

1. Using only total revenue CAGR: Hides segment-level divergence. One segment may be declining while another masks it. 2. No cross-check with capacity: If revenue growth implies capacity utilisation >100%, the model is wrong. 3. Ignoring mix shift: Revenue grows but margins compress = lower-margin segments growing faster. 4. Using competitor’s growth rate: #2 in a market may have different share dynamics than #1. 5. Projecting volume and price together: Locks you into one scenario and hides the real drivers.

🔍
The Revenue Model Sanity Check
Three Cross-Checks Every Revenue Model Must Pass Before You Use It
1. Capacity check: Does projected revenue require capacity utilisation above 90%? If yes, model a capacity expansion (Capex) or reduce the growth assumption. 2. Market share check: Does projected market share exceed a plausible level for the company’s competitive position? No company sustains >50% market share growth for 5 consecutive years. 3. Price vs inflation check: Are you assuming price hikes above inflation for 5 years? That requires demonstrable pricing power (brand, switching costs, regulation). If the company doesn’t have it, use inflation-level price growth.

The Golden Rule of Revenue Modelling
Never Apply a Single Growth Rate to a Multi-Segment Business. Always Model Volume and Price Separately. Always Check the Capacity Math.
These three rules eliminate 80% of revenue model errors. Segment-level modelling catches mix shifts. Volume × Price separation enables better scenario analysis. Capacity math catches physically impossible projections. All three require 20–30 extra minutes of work per company — but produce projections that are 5× more reliable. 05 — Tricks to Project Revenue Fast

5 Screener.in Shortcuts to Build Revenue Models Faster

These are field-tested tricks to build accurate segment revenue models in under 30 minutes — all using Screener.in and freely available data.

1
Screener.in — P&L Tab
The “Revenue per Employee” Cross-Check
For service companies (IT, consulting, staffing), revenue is largely headcount-driven. Revenue per Employee = Total Revenue ÷ Total Headcount. If your Bottom-Up model shows revenue growing 20% while headcount grows only 5%, you are implicitly assuming revenue/employee productivity improves 14% p.a. Is that realistic? For IT companies, productivity improvements of 8–12% are normal (automation + AI). Above 15% is aggressive. Use this cross-check to validate any service company’s revenue model. Screener.in P&L tab shows revenue; employee count is in the Annual Report or BSE filings.
→ P&L tab → Total Revenue ÷ employee count = Revenue per Head
→ Compare 5-year historical trend: rising = productivity gain
→ Your projected Revenue/Head growth = model’s implicit productivity assumption
✔ <10% productivity improvement = realistic ⚠ 10–15% = ambitious but possible ✗ >15% = investigate what drives this
2
Annual Report — Segment Notes
The “Segment Revenue to Total Revenue” Mix Check
Before projecting, calculate each segment’s % of total revenue for the last 5 years and check whether it is stable, rising, or falling. A segment’s share rising = it is growing faster than the total. A segment’s share falling = it is growing slower (or declining). Your projected segment growth rates must be consistent with projected mix shifts. If you project all segments at the same growth rate, the mix is stable — is that realistic? If you project the low-margin segment faster, margins will compress. Force yourself to justify each segment’s mix trajectory before finalising revenue assumptions.
→ Annual Report → Segment Note → copy segment revenue 5 years
→ Calculate each segment % of total for FY21, FY22, FY23, FY24, FY25
→ Is the mix trending? Your projected mix in Year 5 must be defensible
✔ Mix stable = same growth rates acceptable ⚠ Mix shifting = different growth rates needed per segment ✗ Mix diverging rapidly = your margin assumption will be wrong
3
Screener.in Ratios + Annual Report
The “Asset Turnover as Revenue Ceiling” Trick
For asset-heavy businesses (manufacturing, infrastructure), revenue is ultimately constrained by asset base. Asset Turnover = Revenue ÷ Net Fixed Assets. If historical asset turnover is 1.8× and you project revenue growing 20% without any capex increase, you are implicitly assuming asset turnover rises to 2.2× in 3 years. Is that feasible? Check the historical range. For most capital-intensive businesses, asset turnover has a natural ceiling (maximum capacity utilisation). If your revenue projection breaches the ceiling, you must either add capex or reduce revenue growth. Screener.in Ratios tab shows asset turnover directly.
→ Ratios tab → Asset Turnover Ratio → note 5-year range
→ Revenue projection ÷ Net Block = Implied asset turnover in Year 3
→ Implied turnover > historical max → revenue projection too aggressive
✔ Implied turnover within historical range = feasible ⚠ Slightly above historical max = aggressive but arguable ✗ Far above historical max = impossible without new assets
4
Quarterly Results + Industry Data
The “Volume vs Price Decomposition” Trick
Every quarterly result for a consumer/industrial company contains management commentary on volume growth vs price growth. Note both separately for the last 8 quarters. Calculate volume CAGR and price CAGR independently. For your model: project volume using capacity/market-share logic and project price using inflation + pricing power assessment. Then revenue = volume × price. This produces two independent levers for scenario analysis. Bear case: volume flat, price +2%. Base: volume +7%, price +4%. Bull: volume +10%, price +6%. Each scenario has a specific economic narrative rather than “revenue +6/11/16%.”
→ Quarterly results PDF → find volume growth and value growth commentary
→ Implied price growth = Value growth − Volume growth (approximately)
→ Project volume and price separately → multiply for revenue
✔ Volume+Price separately = nuanced scenario analysis ⚠ Only total revenue CAGR = less precise ✗ Projecting both together = hides what is really driving growth

Screener.in + AMFI / TRAI / RBI / SEBI Data — Macro Cross-Check
The “Market Share Sanity Test” — Most Important Revenue Model Check
For Macro-Driven models, the single most important check is: Is the projected market share plausible given competitive dynamics? After building your model, extract the implied market share in Year 5. Then ask: who is losing share as your company gains? Is that plausible? Strong incumbents rarely lose more than 1–2% share per year to a new entrant. Gaining from 8% to 22% in 3 years requires the market leader to simultaneously drop from 55% to 41% — for which there must be a specific, demonstrable reason.
→ Find industry TAM data: AMFI (MF), TRAI (Telecom), SEBI (Broking), RBI (Banking)
→ Calculate company’s current market share: Revenue ÷ TAM
→ Your projected revenue ÷ projected TAM = implied Year 5 market share
→ Is the implied share gain realistic? Who loses? Why?
→ ✔ Share gain: +1–2% p.a. = realistic if strong product/service advantage
→ ⚠ Share gain: +3–5% p.a. = aggressive, needs specific justification
→ ✗ Share gain: >5% p.a. = exceptional, only during disruption events
→ Historical share trend from sector data = best anchor for assumption
✔ Share gain consistent with historical trend = defensible ⚠ Share gain above trend = needs specific competitive catalyst ✗ Share gain far above trend & no catalyst = unfounded model

Consumer / FMCG
Bottom-Up (Vol × Price)
Volume = distribution reach × offtake rate • Price = inflation + premium mix • Check: ASP trend from quarterly results • Examples: Nestle, Pidilite, Asian Paints
Manufacturing / Industrial
Bottom-Up (Capacity × Price)
Volume = Capacity × Utilisation% • Price = commodity price + conversion premium • Check: Does volume stay <95% utilisation? • Examples: Polycab, Tata Steel, Ultratech
Financial Services
Macro (AUM/Loan × Rate)
AUM/Loan Book = TAM × Market Share • Revenue = AUM/Loan × Yield/Expense Ratio • Data: AMFI, RBI, SEBI • Examples: HDFC AMC, Bajaj Finance, ICICI Bank
Technology / SaaS
Bottom-Up (Subs × ARPU)
Subscribers = Total users × Conversion% • ARPU = monthly fee × annual plan mix • Check: Churn rate × subscriber base • Examples: TCS, Infosys (by service line)
Hotels / Retail
Bottom-Up (Unit × Rate × Fill)
Hotels: Rooms × ARR × Occupancy% = RevPAR • Retail: Stores × SSSG + new store revenue • Check: Occupancy ceiling, store maturity curve • Examples: IHCL, Westlife, Trent

The One-Line Revenue Rule
If You Can’t Explain the Revenue Assumption as “X Units × Y Price” or “Z% of TAM at W% Share,” You Don’t Have a Revenue Model — You Have a Guess.
Every defensible revenue projection reduces to one of two formulas: Volume × Price (Bottom-Up) or TAM × Market Share (Macro-Driven). If your model cannot be expressed as either, you are probably using a historical CAGR applied blindly — which is fine as a starting point but dangerous as a final assumption. The data to express it properly is in Screener.in P&L, the Annual Report segment notes, and freely available sector regulatory data (AMFI, TRAI, RBI, SEBI). Professional equity analysts can defend every revenue assumption with a specific number from a specific source. That is the standard to aim for.
