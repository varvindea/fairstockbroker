01 — Key Learning Outcome

Evaluate Terminal Value Sensitivity

By the end of this module you will compute Terminal Value using both the Gordon Growth Model and the Exit Multiple Method, understand why TV dominates the DCF, and build a sensitivity table that shows how valuation changes when key assumptions shift.

📈
Your Core Takeaway — The Tail Wags the Dog
In most DCF models, Terminal Value represents 60–80% of the total intrinsic value. This means the business you can actually forecast (Years 1–5) contributes only 20–40% of the answer. The rest comes from a single formula built on two assumptions: a perpetual growth rate and a discount rate.
Imagine you are trying to value a mango tree. You can estimate the fruit it will produce over the next 5 years reasonably well. But what is the tree worth beyond Year 5 — for the next 50–100 years? That is the Terminal Value. In a DCF, the Terminal Value captures “all future cash flows beyond the explicit forecast period, discounted back to today.” Because it represents such a large proportion of the total DCF value, small changes in the growth rate assumption (g) or the discount rate (WACC) produce dramatic swings in the final answer. A company worth ₹10,000 Cr under one TV assumption might be worth ₹15,000 Cr under another — using completely reasonable inputs in both cases. This is why Terminal Value sensitivity analysis is not optional — it is essential.
🍓 GGM: FCF ÷ (WACC − g) 📈 Exit Multiple: EV/EBITDA × Terminal EBITDA 📋 Sensitivity: WACC vs g table ⚠ TV = 60–80% of total DCF value
02 — Two Methods Explained

Gordon Growth Model vs Exit Multiple — How Each Works

Both methods estimate the same thing — what is the business worth beyond Year 5 — but from different angles. The GGM uses a perpetuity formula; the Exit Multiple uses a comparable-company transaction price. Using both and comparing the results is the professional standard.

🍓
Method 1 — Perpetuity Formula
Gordon Growth Model (GGM)
The GGM treats the business as a perpetuity — an asset that produces cash flows forever, growing at a constant rate. The Terminal Value is the present value of all FCFs from Year 6 to infinity, compounded at the growth rate and discounted at WACC. This is the most theoretically correct method and the one used by most investment banks in DCF footnotes.
TV = FCFₙ × (1 + g) ÷ (WACC − g)
FCFₙ = final year of explicit forecast (Year 5 FCF) • g = long-term perpetual growth rate (use 4–6% for India; must be < nominal GDP growth = ~10–11%) • WACC − g must be positive (always) • TV is then discounted: PV(TV) = TV ÷ (1+WACC)^5
Step 1: Get Year 5 FCF from your 3-statement model. This is OCF − Capex.
Step 2: Choose g (perpetual growth rate). For Indian companies: 4% (conservative) / 5% (base) / 6% (optimistic). Never above 7% for an Indian company — implies growing faster than the entire economy forever.
Step 3: TV = FCF5 × (1+g) ÷ (WACC − g). Example: FCF5=₹827 Cr, g=5%, WACC=11.1% → TV = 827×1.05 ÷ (0.111−0.05) = ₹14,240 Cr.
Step 4: Discount TV back: PV(TV) = 14,240 ÷ (1.111)^5 = ₹8,450 Cr today.
Step 5: Add PV of explicit FCFs (Years 1–5) to PV(TV) = Total Enterprise Value.
💡 Plain English: If a mango tree gives ₹100 fruit every year and you expect it to give 5% more fruit each year forever, and your cost of capital is 11.1%, then the tree is worth ₹100×1.05 ÷ (11.1%−5%) = ₹1,721 today. That is the GGM in one sentence. The entire value of the tree comes from expected future fruit, not today’s fruit.
📈
Method 2 — Comparable Transactions
Exit Multiple Method
The Exit Multiple Method assumes the business is sold at the end of Year 5 at a multiple that peer companies currently trade at. TV = EV/EBITDA multiple × Year 5 projected EBITDA. This method is market-anchored — it ties the terminal value to what similar businesses actually trade at today — rather than a theoretical perpetuity formula. It is widely used in private equity and M&A contexts.
TV = Exit Multiple × Terminal EBITDA (or FCF)
Exit Multiple = current sector EV/EBITDA multiple (from Screener.in peer comparison or Bloomberg) • Terminal EBITDA = projected EBITDA in Year 5 • Can also use EV/FCF or P/E multiple • PV(TV) = TV ÷ (1+WACC)^5
Step 1: Find the sector EV/EBITDA multiple on Screener.in. Compare tab → sort by EV/EBITDA for peer group. Use the median, not the mean (less affected by outliers).
Step 2: Get Year 5 projected EBITDA from your 3-statement model (P&L projection).
Step 3: TV = Multiple × Year 5 EBITDA. Example: CDSL FY28E EBITDA ₹1,217 Cr × sector median 30× = ₹36,510 Cr.
Step 4: Discount back: PV(TV) = 36,510 ÷ (1.111)^5 = ₹21,660 Cr.
Step 5: Cross-check vs GGM result. Large gap = investigate which assumption is wrong.
💡 Plain English: If similar mango trees sell for 20× their annual fruit production in the local market, and your tree produces ₹100 of fruit in Year 5, then the tree is worth ₹2,000 at the end of Year 5. Discount that back to today and you have the Exit Multiple TV. The key assumption is: will similar trees still sell for 20× fruit in 5 years? If the sector derate or re-rate risk exists, the Exit Multiple method is less reliable.

📊 How to Choose Between GGM and Exit Multiple

Use GGM when: the company is mature, stable, and will operate indefinitely (FMCG, utilities, established IT). The perpetuity assumption is reasonable. Use Exit Multiple when: you have good peer comparables (M&A context, private equity, early-stage), sector multiples are available, or the company’s long-term growth is highly uncertain. Best practice: Compute both, compare, and use the range as your valuation range. If GGM and Exit Multiple are within 20% of each other → high confidence. If they differ by 50%+ → one input needs investigation.

☀️ The Perpetual Growth Rate (g) — The Most Dangerous Assumption

g must be less than WACC (mathematically required — otherwise TV is negative or infinite). g must be less than nominal GDP growth — no company can grow faster than the economy forever. India’s nominal GDP growth is ~10–11%; a realistic perpetual g for Indian companies is 4–6%. Using g=8% for any company implies it will eventually be larger than the Indian economy — mathematically impossible. Always use g = inflation rate (5%) as your base case and run sensitivity at g−1% (bear) and g+1% (bull).

📋 Finding Exit Multiples on Screener.in

Step 1: Screener.in → company page → Compare tab → this shows EV/EBITDA for all sector peers. Step 2: Note the median EV/EBITDA of 5–10 comparable companies (not the outliers at the top or bottom). Step 3: Apply a discount of 10–20% to the current multiple for the Exit Multiple assumption — because in Year 5, the company may have a lower multiple if it is larger and more mature. Step 4: Cross-check: does your implied Exit Multiple produce a GGM-equivalent g? If Exit Multiple implies g=15%, it is too aggressive.

🚨 TV as % of Total DCF — The Warning Sign

If TV >85% of total DCF value — your explicit forecast period is too short or the growth rate in the explicit period is too low. The model is almost entirely dependent on the perpetuity assumption, which is the least reliable part. Fix: extend the explicit forecast to 7–10 years, or run a two-stage model (high growth for 5 years, then transition to mature growth). If TV <50% of total DCF — the explicit growth assumptions are very aggressive relative to the terminal assumptions. Check if the 5-year growth rate is realistic and whether the company naturally transitions to a lower growth profile after Year 5.

🔍
The Golden Rule of Terminal Value
Always Build a Sensitivity Table. Never Rely on a Single Terminal Value Number. Report a Valuation Range, Not a Single Price Target.
A 2% change in g (from 5% to 7%) can change CDSL’s Terminal Value by 59%. A 2% change in WACC has a similar effect. A professional DCF always presents a 5×5 or 4×5 sensitivity table (WACC vs g) and reports valuation as a range — e.g., “intrinsic value ₹1,200–1,800 per share” not “₹1,500 per share.” Any analyst who gives a single exact price target from a DCF without showing sensitivity is either hiding uncertainty or does not understand the model’s limitations.

The Golden Rule of Terminal Value
g Must Be < WACC (Always). g Must Be < Nominal GDP Growth (Always). TV Must Have a Sensitivity Table (Always). Never Report a Single TV Number.
Three absolute rules that eliminate the most dangerous Terminal Value errors. Every professional DCF model has a sensitivity table showing how intrinsic value changes across a range of WACC and g assumptions. The base case value is just the midpoint of a range — not the truth. An analyst who cannot articulate the bear and bull case TV assumptions for their model does not truly understand their own DCF. Always use these: g = 4% (bear), 5% (base), 6% (bull) for Indian companies. Run the WACC similarly: WACC − 1% (optimistic), base, WACC +1% (conservative). The resulting 3×3 table tells you everything about how confident you should be in your valuation.
05 — Tricks to Get Terminal Value Right

5 Shortcuts to Reliable Terminal Value Fast

These field-tested tricks help you compute reliable Terminal Value, cross-check between methods, and build the sensitivity table in under 20 minutes.

1
GGM Quick Sanity Check
The “TV as % of DCF” Health Check
After computing TV, immediately calculate: PV(TV) ÷ Total Enterprise Value × 100. Healthy range: 60–80%. Below 60%: your explicit period may be over-optimistic. Above 80%: extend your explicit forecast or acknowledge the model is perpetuity-dependent. Above 90%: the company is likely too early-stage for reliable DCF — consider P/E or EV/EBITDA multiples instead. This single check tells you how reliable the overall model is before you present it to anyone.
→ Compute Total EV = PV(FCFs Y1-5) + PV(TV)
→ TV% = PV(TV) ÷ Total EV × 100
→ 60–80% = healthy. >80% = sensitivity essential. >90% = use multiples
✔ 60–75%: healthy model balance ⚠ 75–85%: run sensitivity table ✗ >90%: reconsider DCF approach
2
Screener.in Compare Tab
The “Peer Multiple in 60 Seconds” Trick
For the Exit Multiple method, you need a peer EV/EBITDA multiple. Screener.in makes this instant: company page → Compare tab → sort by EV/EBITDA. Note the median of 5–8 direct peers (exclude obvious outliers). Apply a 10–20% discount to the current median for your Year 5 Exit Multiple assumption — because companies often derate as they mature and grow larger. Cross-check: convert your Exit Multiple to an implied GGM g using the formula: Implied g = WACC − (FCF/TV). If implied g is >7%, the Exit Multiple is too high.
→ Screener.in → Compare tab → EV/EBITDA column → find peer median
→ Apply 10–20% discount: Year 5 Exit Multiple = Peer Median × 0.80–0.90
→ Cross-check: Implied g = WACC − (FCF ÷ TV). If >7%: reduce multiple
✔ Implied g 4–6%: Exit Multiple is well-calibrated ⚠ Implied g 6–8%: aggressive but arguable ✗ Implied g >8%: Exit Multiple too high
3
The Reverse DCF Trick
The “What Does the Market Price Imply?” Trick
Instead of computing intrinsic value forward, back-solve: at the current market price, what perpetual growth rate is implied? Rearrange GGM: Implied g = WACC − (Normalised FCF ÷ Market Cap + Net Debt). If implied g is 4–6% → market is pricing reasonable assumptions → fairly valued. If implied g is 9%+ → market is pricing very aggressive assumptions → potentially overvalued. If implied g is 2–3% → market is pricing very pessimistic assumptions → potentially undervalued. This Reverse DCF is one of the most powerful and fast valuation reality-checks in equity research.
→ Market Cap + Net Debt = Current Enterprise Value (from Screener.in)
→ Normalised FCF = 5-year average FCF (CF tab on Screener.in)
→ Implied g = WACC − (Normalised FCF ÷ EV). Compare to 4–6% realistic range
✔ Implied g 4–6%: fair value zone ⚠ Implied g 2–4%: potentially undervalued ✗ Implied g >7%: potentially overvalued
4
Two-Stage GGM for High-Growth Companies
The “Two-Stage DCF” Trick for Fast-Growing Companies
Single-stage GGM assumes growth is already at terminal rate from Day 1. For a company still growing at 20%+ in Year 5, this understates value. Two-stage model: Stage 1 = explicit high-growth period (Years 1–5 at 18–22% FCF growth, fully modelled). Stage 2 = terminal GGM at mature rate (5%). Terminal Value is computed on Stage 1 Year 5 FCF, discounted back from Year 5. This approach is appropriate for CDSL (currently 25% revenue CAGR fading to 5% terminal) and any Nifty 500 company still in high-growth mode where a single-stage g=5% assumption understates near-term momentum.
→ Build explicit 5-year FCF model (Module 09 approach) → this is Stage 1
→ TV (Stage 2) = Year 5 FCF × (1 + g_terminal) ÷ (WACC − g_terminal)
→ Total value = Sum of PV(FCFs Y1-5) + PV(TV). Run sensitivity on g_terminal
✔ Two-stage: better for high-growth companies ⚠ Single-stage: acceptable for mature companies ✗ Single-stage on high-growth: understates value significantly
5
Build the Sensitivity Table — Most Important TV Output
The Complete “WACC vs g Sensitivity Table” in 10 Minutes
Every professional DCF report contains a sensitivity table showing how intrinsic value changes as WACC and g vary. Build one in your spreadsheet in 10 minutes and it becomes the most informative output of your entire analysis.
1. Set up a 5×5 table: WACC rows (base −2%, −1%, base, +1%, +2%) vs g columns (3%, 4%, 5%, 6%, 7%)
2. Each cell formula: PV(FCFs) + [FCF5×(1+g)÷(WACC−g)] × discount factor
3. Colour code: Green = Bull (>current price), Yellow = Near current, Red = Bear (<current)
4. Add per-share row: Total Enterprise Value + Net Cash ÷ Share Count
5. Note: which cells show buy? Which show sell? Does majority of table show value?
6. Cross-check: does the Exit Multiple method’s value fall in the same colour zone?
7. Report the range (e.g., ₹1,400–2,200/share) not a single number
8. If >70% of table cells show buy → high conviction. If only 2–3 cells show buy → marginal
✔ >70% table cells show buy = high conviction ⚠ 40–70% cells show buy = moderate conviction ✗ <40% show buy = wait for better entry or re-examine thesis

🎯
The One-Line Terminal Value Rule
If You Can’t Explain Why Your Perpetual Growth Rate Is Justified, You Don’t Have a Terminal Value — You Have a Guess Dressed in a Formula.
Every g assumption needs a story: “I am using g=5% because India’s long-run CPI is ~5%, and CDSL’s monopoly position means it will grow at least at inflation forever. It will grow faster in Years 1–5 (captured explicitly), then mature to inflation rate.” That is a defensible assumption. “I am using g=8% because CDSL has been growing at 25% recently” is not defensible for a perpetuity. The Terminal Value formula is simple. The intellectual work is justifying the inputs. That justification — grounded in Screener.in data, industry research, and first-principles thinking — is what separates a professional DCF from a spreadsheet exercise.
07 — Reference Glossary

Terminal Value Glossary

Every key term used in this module — explained in plain English. Bookmark this as your reference before building any DCF model.

Term
What It Means in Simple Words
Key Point
── Terminal Value Fundamentals ──
Terminal Value (TV)
The value of all free cash flows a business will generate beyond the explicit forecast period (typically beyond Year 5), captured as a single lump sum and discounted back to today. TV usually represents 60–80% of total DCF value, making it the single most important — and most uncertain — component of any DCF model.
60–80% of DCF
Explicit Forecast Period
The years for which you build a detailed financial model — typically 5 years. During this period you explicitly project revenue, margins, capex, and working capital. Year 5 FCF becomes the base for calculating Terminal Value. Longer explicit periods (7–10 years) reduce the TV% of total DCF and make the valuation more reliable for high-growth companies.
5–10 years
Perpetuity
A financial instrument that pays cash flows forever at a constant or constantly growing rate. The Gordon Growth Model treats the business as a growing perpetuity beyond Year 5. The present value of a growing perpetuity = Cash Flow ÷ (Discount Rate − Growth Rate). This is mathematically elegant but requires extremely careful assumption setting.
CF ÷ (r−g)
── Gordon Growth Model (GGM) Terms ──
Gordon Growth Model (GGM)
A formula to compute Terminal Value by treating the business as a growing perpetuity: TV = FCFₙ × (1 + g) ÷ (WACC − g). Named after economist Myron Gordon. The most theoretically rigorous Terminal Value method, used by investment banks in DCF footnotes. Requires two key assumptions: perpetual growth rate (g) and discount rate (WACC).
TV = FCF×(1+g) ÷ (r−g)
Perpetual Growth Rate (g)
The rate at which FCF is assumed to grow forever beyond the explicit forecast period. Must always be: (a) less than WACC (or TV becomes negative/infinite), (b) less than India nominal GDP growth (~10%), (c) greater than zero for growing companies. India safe range: 4–6%. Bear case = 4% (inflation-only growth). Base = 5% (India CPI proxy). Bull = 6% (slight real growth above inflation).
4–6% for India
Normalised FCF
The “steady-state” free cash flow used as the base for the GGM formula — representing what the business earns in a normal (mid-cycle) year, not a peak or trough year. For cyclical companies, use the 5-year average FCF rather than the latest year. Using peak FCF as the normalised base is one of the most common and costly DCF errors, especially for steel, commodities, and hotel companies.
Mid-cycle FCF
Two-Stage DCF
A DCF model with two distinct growth phases: Stage 1 = explicit high-growth forecast (Years 1–5 with company-specific FCF projections at 15–25% growth) + Stage 2 = terminal GGM at mature perpetual growth (5%). Better than single-stage for companies still in high-growth mode. The terminal GGM is applied to the end of Stage 1, capturing the transition from high-growth to steady-state.
High-growth + Mature
── Exit Multiple Method Terms ──
Exit Multiple Method
Computes Terminal Value by multiplying a sector valuation multiple (EV/EBITDA, P/E, EV/FCF) by the company’s projected Year 5 earnings metric: TV = Exit Multiple × Terminal EBITDA. Market-anchored rather than formula-based. More intuitive but depends on comparable company multiples being stable and applicable. Used extensively in private equity and M&A contexts.
Multiple × EBITDAₙ
EV/EBITDA Multiple
Enterprise Value divided by EBITDA — the most commonly used valuation multiple for Exit Multiple TV calculations. Available for every listed company on Screener.in Compare tab. Use the sector peer median (not mean) to avoid outlier distortion. Apply a 10–20% discount to the current peer multiple for the Year 5 exit assumption, because companies often derate as they mature. Typical Indian sectors: FMCG ~35–45×, IT ~18–25×, Auto ~12–18×.
Sector peer median
Implied Growth Rate
The perpetual growth rate implied by the Exit Multiple — used to cross-check whether the Exit Multiple assumption is consistent with the GGM assumption. Formula: Implied g = WACC − (Year 5 FCF ÷ Terminal Value). If the implied g from Exit Multiple >7% for an Indian company, the Exit Multiple is likely too high and should be reduced. This cross-check ensures both methods are using internally consistent assumptions.
WACC − FCF/TV
── Discounting & WACC Terms ──
WACC (Discount Rate)
Weighted Average Cost of Capital — the hurdle rate used to discount all future FCFs (including TV) back to today’s value. Computed as a weighted blend of Cost of Equity (CAPM) and after-tax Cost of Debt. For Indian companies: Ke = 7% G-Sec + Beta × 7.5% ERP (from Damodaran India). Typical India WACC range: 11–16%. See Module 11 for the full WACC calculation workflow.
11–16% India
Discount Factor
The multiplier that converts a future cash flow into its present value today. For Year 5 TV: Discount Factor = 1 ÷ (1 + WACC)^5. At WACC=11.1% for 5 years: DF = 1 ÷ (1.111)^5 = 0.593. This means ₹1 of Terminal Value in Year 5 is worth only ₹0.593 today. The higher the WACC, the lower the discount factor, and the less today’s value you assign to far-future cash flows.
1 ÷ (1+r)^n
PV of Terminal Value
The Terminal Value discounted back to today using the WACC. PV(TV) = TV ÷ (1 + WACC)^n where n = number of years to end of explicit forecast. This is the number that goes into your DCF summary as the TV contribution to Enterprise Value. Example: CDSL TV (GGM) = ₹14,240 Cr ÷ (1.111)^5 = ₹8,440 Cr today. Always compute and report PV(TV), not the undiscounted TV.
TV ÷ (1+r)^5
Enterprise Value (EV)
The total value of the entire business (debt + equity combined): EV = PV of explicit FCFs (Years 1–5) + PV of Terminal Value. To get Equity Value (what shareholders own): Equity Value = EV + Cash & Investments − Total Debt − Minority Interest. From Screener.in: Market Cap = Equity Value as currently priced. Per-Share Value = Equity Value ÷ Number of Shares Outstanding.
EV = PV(FCFs)+PV(TV)
── Sensitivity & Validation Terms ──
Sensitivity Analysis
Testing how the output (intrinsic value) changes when input assumptions are varied. For TV: build a 4×5 or 5×5 table with WACC on one axis and g on the other. Each cell shows EV or per-share value. Colour-code: green = above current market price (buy zone), red = below (overvalued). The spread of values across the table shows the full uncertainty range — which is far more honest than a single-point estimate.
WACC × g table
Reverse DCF
Working backwards from the current market price to find what assumptions it implies. Instead of projecting FCF forward to find intrinsic value, you ask: “At this market price, what perpetual growth rate is baked in?” Formula: Implied g = WACC − (Normalised FCF ÷ Current EV). If implied g = 4–6%, market is pricing reasonable assumptions. If implied g = 9%+, market has priced in aggressive growth → stock may be overvalued. One of the fastest and most honest valuation tools.
Back-solve from price
TV as % of DCF
PV(TV) ÷ Total Enterprise Value × 100. Measures how dependent the valuation is on the perpetuity assumption. Healthy: 60–75%. High but acceptable: 75–85% (run full sensitivity). Concerning: >85% (explicitly state the model is driven by TV assumptions). If >90%: the explicit forecast period adds little analytical value — consider extending to 8–10 years or using relative multiples instead of DCF.
Target: 60–80%
Football Field Chart
A visual valuation summary showing multiple valuation methods as horizontal bars (ranges). Typical bars: DCF Bear, DCF Base, DCF Bull (from GGM sensitivity table) + EV/EBITDA Exit Multiple range + P/E multiple range + 52-week high/low. The overlap zone where multiple methods agree is the “conviction zone.” Current market price is shown as a vertical line to indicate where the stock falls relative to each method’s range. Standard presentation in equity research.
Visual valuation range
Margin of Safety
The gap between intrinsic value and market price — your buffer against assumption errors in the DCF. If intrinsic value (base case DCF) = ₹1,500/share and market price = ₹1,100/share, Margin of Safety = (1,500−1,100) ÷ 1,500 = 27%. Benjamin Graham recommended a 33%+ margin of safety. In DCF, because TV assumptions are inherently uncertain, a large Margin of Safety compensates for model error. A narrow Margin of Safety (<10%) in a TV-driven DCF is dangerous.
Aim for >25–30%
── Free Cash Flow Terms ──
Free Cash Flow (FCF)
Cash generated by the business after all operating costs and capital expenditure: FCF = Operating Cash Flow − Capex. This is the cash available to pay lenders (debt servicing) and equity holders (dividends, buybacks). FCF — not profit — is the correct numerator for the Gordon Growth Model. Available on Screener.in Cash Flow tab: (Cash from Operations) minus (Capital Expenditure). Always positive FCF is a prerequisite for a reliable GGM.
OCF − Capex
FCF Yield
Free Cash Flow as a percentage of Enterprise Value: FCF Yield = Normalised FCF ÷ Current EV. The inverse of the implied EV/FCF multiple. High FCF yield (>5%) = company is generating a lot of cash relative to its price → potentially undervalued. Low FCF yield (<2%) = company is priced expensively relative to cash generation → requires high growth assumptions to justify. Cross-check: if FCF Yield is 4% and WACC is 11%, implied g = WACC − FCF Yield = 7% → aggressive assumption.
FCF ÷ EV
EBITDA
Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation and Amortisation. A proxy for operating cash generation before accounting and financing effects. Used in the Exit Multiple method (TV = EV/EBITDA × Terminal EBITDA) and in the Bridge from Enterprise Value to Equity Value. Available on Screener.in P&L tab as “Operating Profit” (or calculate: Revenue − Operating Expenses). Note: EBITDA can overstate true cash generation for capex-intensive businesses — always cross-check with FCF.
Revenue − Opex

Terminal Value — Key Terms Explained Simply

Term
What It Means in Simple Words
Key Point
Terminal Value (TV) The estimated value of a business beyond the explicit forecast period (Years 1–5), assuming it continues operating forever. In most DCF models, TV accounts for 60–80% of the total enterprise value. Two calculation methods: Gordon Growth Model and Exit Multiple. Because it dominates the model, small changes in TV assumptions produce large swings in final valuation. 60–80% of DCF
Gordon Growth Model (GGM) A perpetuity formula that assumes free cash flows grow forever at a constant rate.
TV = FCFₙ × (1+g) ÷ (WACC − g)
The denominator (WACC − g) must always be positive. If g is close to WACC, TV approaches infinity — a mathematical and economic impossibility. Named after economist Myron J. Gordon.		 Perpetuity formula
Exit Multiple Method Estimates terminal value by assuming the business is sold at end of Year 5 at a valuation multiple comparable to current peer companies.
TV = EV/EBITDA × Terminal EBITDA
Uses the sector’s current median EV/EBITDA multiple (from Screener.in Compare tab), usually discounted 10–20% to account for maturity and potential sector derate over the forecast horizon.		 Market-anchored
Perpetual Growth Rate (g) The constant rate at which free cash flows are assumed to grow forever after Year 5. Must be less than WACC (mathematically required) and less than India’s long-run nominal GDP growth (~10%). For Indian companies, the safe range is 4–6%. Using g=5% (India’s long-run inflation) is the standard conservative base case. Bear: 4% • Base: 5% • Bull: 6%. India: 4–6%
WACC (Weighted Average Cost of Capital) The discount rate used to convert future cash flows into today’s value. Represents the blended cost of equity (what shareholders require) and debt (what lenders charge, after tax).
WACC = Ke × E/V + Kd×(1−T) × D/V
WACC appears in the TV denominator (WACC − g), so a 1% change in WACC can change TV by 15–25%. Covered in detail in Module 11.		 Discount rate
Free Cash Flow (FCF) The actual cash a business generates after all operating expenses, taxes, and capital expenditures.
FCF = Operating Cash Flow − Capex
This is the cash flow discounted in a DCF model — not revenue, not profit. FCF is available from Screener.in’s Cash Flow tab: Cash from Operations minus Capital Expenditure. The Year 5 FCF is the input to the GGM Terminal Value formula.		 OCF − Capex
EV/EBITDA Multiple Enterprise Value divided by Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation, and Amortisation. The most widely used valuation multiple in M&A and DCF Exit Multiple calculations. Available directly from Screener.in’s Compare tab for peer companies. Sector medians: FMCG ~35–50×, IT ~18–25×, Capital Goods ~20–30×, Auto ~12–18×. Use the median of 5–8 peers, not the mean. Peer multiple
PV of Terminal Value [PV(TV)] The terminal value is computed as a future (Year 5) value and must be discounted back to today.
PV(TV) = TV ÷ (1 + WACC)^5
This is the present value of all cash flows from Year 6 to infinity. Adding PV(TV) to the sum of discounted Year 1–5 FCFs gives total Enterprise Value. The ratio PV(TV) ÷ Total EV tells you how dependent the model is on the perpetuity assumption.		 TV ÷ (1+WACC)^5
Enterprise Value (EV) The total value of a business, including equity and debt.
EV = Market Cap + Debt − Cash
In a DCF model, the output is EV (from discounted FCFs). To get Equity Value (per share), add cash and subtract debt: Equity Value = EV + Cash − Debt. EV is the bridge between the DCF output and the per-share intrinsic value. Available on Screener.in’s company homepage.		 MCap + Debt − Cash
Sensitivity Analysis A table that shows how the model output (intrinsic value per share) changes as key assumptions (WACC and g) vary. A standard 4×5 or 5×5 table shows WACC on one axis and g on the other. Every professional DCF must include a sensitivity table. Colour-coded: green = bull case, amber = base, red = bear. Reporting a single point estimate from a DCF without sensitivity is considered incomplete analysis. Always include!
Reverse DCF Instead of projecting cash flows forward to find value, back-solve from the current market price to find the implied growth rate.
Implied g = WACC − (Normalised FCF ÷ EV)
If the implied g is 4–6% for an Indian company, the market is pricing reasonable assumptions. If implied g >8%, the market expects a company to grow faster than the economy forever — a signal of overvaluation. A fast, powerful reality check before any investment.		 Price → implied g
Two-Stage DCF A DCF model with an explicit high-growth period (Years 1–5 at company-specific growth rate) followed by a terminal period at a mature, stable growth rate. More accurate than single-stage GGM for companies still in high-growth phase. Example: CDSL growing at 18% in Years 1–5 (explicitly modelled), then transitioning to g=5% in perpetuity. The terminal GGM is applied only at the end of Stage 1. High growth + mature
Normalised FCF An estimate of sustainable, mid-cycle free cash flow — not the peak or trough year. For cyclical companies (steel, fertilisers, commodities), using the latest year’s FCF as the GGM base is dangerous because it may be at a peak or trough. Use a 5-year average FCF as a conservative proxy for normalised FCF. This prevents overvaluation at peaks and undervaluation at troughs. Available by averaging the CF tab data on Screener.in. 5-year average FCF
Margin of Safety The difference between the intrinsic value (from DCF) and the current market price, expressed as a percentage.
Margin of Safety = (Intrinsic Value − Market Price) ÷ Intrinsic Value
A 30–40% margin of safety is typically required before buying a stock, because DCF assumptions are uncertain. If intrinsic value = ₹1,500 and market price = ₹1,050: MoS = 30%. The higher the MoS, the more room for assumption error. Introduced by Benjamin Graham, popularised by Warren Buffett.		 Buy below IV
TV as % of Total DCF The proportion of total enterprise value attributable to Terminal Value vs explicit FCF years. Healthy range: 60–80%. Below 60%: explicit period growth is very high or over-optimistic. 80–90%: sensitivity analysis is critical. Above 90%: model is too dependent on perpetuity assumptions; consider extending explicit forecast period or using relative multiples instead of DCF. Formula: PV(TV) ÷ (PV FCFs + PV TV). 60–80% healthy
Implied Exit Multiple The EV/EBITDA multiple implied by the GGM Terminal Value. Used to cross-check whether the GGM result is reasonable relative to market multiples.
Implied Multiple = TV ÷ Terminal Year EBITDA
If GGM at g=5% implies EV/EBITDA 18× and peers trade at 20×, the model is slightly conservative — reasonable. If implied multiple is 60× when peers are at 20×, the growth rate assumption is too aggressive. This cross-check validates both methods simultaneously.		 TV ÷ EBITDAₙ
