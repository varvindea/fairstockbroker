01 — Key Learning Outcome

Create a Fully Linked Financial Model from Scratch

By the end of this module you will understand how the three core financial statements connect to each other, how one number flows automatically into another, and how to build a dynamic integrated model that updates itself when assumptions change.

📈
Your Core Takeaway — Three Statements, One Story
The P&L tells you what the company earned. The Balance Sheet tells you what it owns and owes. The Cash Flow Statement tells you whether those earnings were real. All three must tell the same story — or something is wrong.
Imagine running a restaurant. At the end of the month: your P&L says revenue ₹10L, costs ₹7L, profit ₹3L. Your Balance Sheet shows the cash register, equipment, food stock, and the loan you took to open. Your Cash Flow shows you actually collected only ₹2L in cash this month because two catering orders are not yet paid. The three statements together tell the full picture. The P&L can show profit while cash flow shows losses — this is exactly how companies manipulate earnings, and why analysts always build all three together. In a 3-statement model, net profit from the P&L flows to retained earnings on the Balance Sheet, and cash flow from operations validates whether that profit was actually collected in cash. These three linkages are the foundation of all advanced valuation work.
📊 P&L: Revenue → EBITDA → EBIT → PAT ⚖️ Balance Sheet: Assets = Liabilities + Equity 💵 Cash Flow: OCF = PAT + D&A − WC changes
02 — The Three Statements in Detail

P&L, Balance Sheet & Cash Flow — How Each Works

Before building a linked model, you must understand what each statement is designed to show and where its key line items appear on Screener.in. Each statement is the input or output of another — that is what makes them “integrated.”

📊
Statement 1 — Profit Driver
Profit & Loss (P&L)
The P&L statement shows all revenues earned and all expenses incurred over a period (quarter or year). It flows from revenue at the top to net profit at the bottom. In a 3-statement model, the P&L is typically built first because it drives the Balance Sheet and Cash Flow assumptions. Revenue growth rates, margin assumptions, and tax rates are the primary inputs.
Revenue → Drives trade receivables on Balance Sheet (DSO assumption)
COGS → Drives inventory and trade payables on Balance Sheet
D&A → Reduces Net Block on Balance Sheet + added back in CF
Interest → Determined by debt on Balance Sheet × interest rate
Net Profit (PAT) → Flows to Retained Earnings on Balance Sheet
PAT → Starting point of Cash Flow from Operations section
💡 On Screener.in: Profit & Loss tab shows 10 years of every line item. Revenue, EBITDA, EBIT, PAT, EPS — all pre-calculated. Use 5-year CAGR of revenue and margins as your baseline assumption for projections.
⚖️
Statement 2 — Financial Position
Balance Sheet
The Balance Sheet is a snapshot of everything the company owns (assets) and owes (liabilities) at a single point in time. In a model, the Balance Sheet is the most complex to build because it must balance (Total Assets = Total Equity + Total Liabilities) and every line item must be driven by an assumption from the P&L or Cash Flow. If the BS does not balance, there is an error in the model — guaranteed.
Retained Earnings → Increases by PAT, decreases by dividends paid
Net Block → Prior Net Block + Capex − D&A (from P&L)
Trade Receivables → Revenue × DSO / 365
Inventory → COGS × Inventory Days / 365
Trade Payables → COGS × DPO / 365
Cash & Equivalents → Prior cash + Net Cash Flow from CF statement
💡 On Screener.in: Balance Sheet tab shows all assets and liabilities. The plug line is always cash — if the model does not balance, adjust the cash figure. A balanced BS is the model’s health check: if it balances, all three statements are correctly linked.
💵
Statement 3 — Cash Reality Check
Cash Flow Statement
The Cash Flow Statement reconciles the profit shown on the P&L with the actual change in cash on the Balance Sheet. Profit is an accounting concept; cash is the reality. A company can show profit while running out of cash (if it is not collecting receivables or is investing heavily in capex). In a 3-statement model, the CF statement is built last — it takes inputs from both P&L and Balance Sheet changes to arrive at net cash change.
Operating CF = PAT + D&A − Change in Working Capital
Investing CF = −Capex ± Asset sales ± Investments
Financing CF = New debt − Debt repaid − Dividends − Buybacks
Net CF = OCF + ICF + FCF → updates Cash on Balance Sheet
Free Cash Flow = OCF − Capex → used in DCF valuation
💡 On Screener.in: Cash Flow tab shows all three sections. The most important check: Operating Cash Flow vs Net Profit. If OCF < PAT consistently, the company is not converting profit to cash — a red flag for earnings quality in any 3-statement model projection.

📋 Step 1: Build the P&L First

Start with revenue growth assumptions based on Screener.in’s 5-year historical CAGR. Then apply margin assumptions (EBITDA %, tax rate) as a % of revenue. Key P&L line items to model: Revenue, COGS, Gross Profit, EBITDA, D&A (linked to Balance Sheet), Interest (linked to Debt on BS), Tax, PAT. Every line should be a formula driven by either an assumption row or another cell in the model — no hard-coded numbers except in the assumption section at the top.

⚖️ Step 2: Build the Balance Sheet

The BS is built using operating drivers: Net Block = Prior Net Block + Capex − D&A. Trade Receivables = Revenue × DSO / 365. Inventory = COGS × Inventory Days / 365. Trade Payables = COGS × DPO / 365. Retained Earnings = Prior RE + PAT − Dividends. Debt is a schedule (prior debt + new borrowings − repayments). Cash is the PLUG LINE — it comes from the Cash Flow statement at the end. If BS balances (Total Assets = Total L+E), your model is correct.

💵 Step 3: Build the Cash Flow Statement

CF statement is built last because it pulls from both P&L and BS: Operating CF = PAT + D&A − Change in Working Capital (change in receivables + change in inventory − change in payables). Investing CF = −Capex + proceeds from asset sales. Financing CF = new debt raised − debt repaid − dividends − buybacks. Net CF = OCF + ICF + FinCF. This net CF is then added to the prior year cash balance to get the ending cash, which is fed back into the Balance Sheet cash line. This closes the loop.

🔍 Step 4: Verify & Stress Test

Four checks to verify your model is correctly linked: (1) Does the Balance Sheet balance? (Total Assets = Total L + E). (2) Does projected Free Cash Flow grow over the forecast period? (3) Is projected OCF / PAT ratio consistent with historical levels? (4) Do a sensitivity test: change revenue growth by +/− 5% — do all downstream cells update? If yes, your model is dynamic and correctly linked. If the BS does not balance, find the error by checking: RE, Net Block, or the cash plug.

Step 5 — Project the Future
📈 Extend the Model 3–5 Years Using Assumptions
Once the historical model balances correctly, extend it forward 3–5 years by changing assumptions. Revenue growth rate (from historical CAGR), EBITDA margin (5-year average), Capex (% of revenue), and DSO/DIO/DPO (5-year averages) are your four core assumption levers. Manufacturing is more FCF-sensitive than asset-light: Polycab’s FCF ranges from ₹385 Cr (Bear) to ₹970 Cr (Bull) — a 152% difference. Compare this to CDSL’s 69% range. Why? This is why manufacturing DCF valuations require wider scenario ranges and more conservative assumption choices.
🎯
The Polycab Model Takeaway
Polycab Proves That a Manufacturing 3-Statement Model Uses the Same Structure — Just with 3 Extra WC Assumptions and Bigger Capex Numbers
Start with the 5 CDSL assumptions, add DSO + DIO + DPO + Debt schedule, and you have the Polycab model. The Inventory line (COGS × 55 days ÷ 365) and the Net Block line (Prior NB + Capex − D&A) are the two new critical BS drivers that CDSL doesn’t need. All data for both models is freely available on Screener.in P&L, Balance Sheet, and Cash Flow tabs. Build CDSL first, then add the three WC drivers for Polycab. You are now modelling real companies the way professional analysts do.
05 — Tricks to Build Your 3-Statement Model Fast

5 Shortcuts to Model Any Company in 60 Minutes

These are field-tested shortcuts used by equity research analysts to build accurate 3-statement models quickly — all using freely available Screener.in data.

1
Screener.in — P&L Tab
The “5-Year Average Margin” Trick
Never project a single-year margin. Instead, open Screener.in → P&L tab → select Operating Profit % (EBITDA margin) for the last 5 years. Calculate the simple average. Use that average as your base case margin assumption, with ±200 bps as your bull/bear scenario. This anchors your model in reality rather than assuming the latest year’s margin (which may be unusually high or low) continues forever. The 5-year average automatically smooths out one-off cost spikes, commodity price cycles, and exceptional items.
→ Screener.in → P&L tab → note Operating Profit % for FY21–FY25
→ Average the 5 years: (Y1+Y2+Y3+Y4+Y5) ÷ 5 = Base margin
→ Set Bull = Base + 200 bps, Bear = Base − 200 bps
✔ Average margin = reliable projection base ⚠ Trending margin = use last 2-year average instead ✗ Single-year margin = unreliable, always average
2
Screener.in — CF Tab
The “OCF / PAT Health Check” Trick
Before building a single cell of your model, run the OCF/PAT check. Screener.in CF tab → Cash from Operations ÷ Net Profit for last 5 years. If consistently >80% — proceed. The working capital assumptions will be straightforward. If <60% — investigate the specific items causing the gap (receivables bloat? inventory buildup? capitalised costs?). These items will need specific WC assumptions in your model rather than simple DSO/DIO rules. This 2-minute check prevents 90% of model errors by identifying complexities before you start building.
→ Screener.in → Cash Flow tab → find “Cash from Operations”
→ P&L tab → note “Net Profit” for same years
→ Calculate OCF/PAT for each of last 5 years. Consistent? Declining?
✔ OCF/PAT >80% = clean, proceed with standard model ⚠ 60–80% = add specific WC assumptions ✗ <60% or declining = investigate before modelling
3
Screener.in — Ratios Tab
The “DSO/DIO/DPO from Ratios Tab” Trick
Building the Balance Sheet requires working capital assumptions. Instead of manually calculating DSO (Debtor Days), DIO (Inventory Days), and DPO (Payable Days), use Screener.in’s Ratios tab which has pre-calculated “Debtor Days” and “Inventory Days.” For DPO, calculate: Trade Payables ÷ COGS × 365. Use 5-year averages of all three as your working capital assumption inputs. If all three have been stable, your WC model is simple and reliable. If they are changing significantly year-on-year, you need to model the trend explicitly.
→ Ratios tab → note “Debtor Days” for 5 years → average
→ Ratios tab → note “Inventory Turnover” → convert to days
→ BS tab: Trade Payables ÷ COGS × 365 for each year → average DPO
✔ Stable DSO/DIO/DPO = simple WC model ⚠ Slowly trending = use last 2-year average ✗ Rapidly changing = investigate each driver separately
4
Screener.in — BS & CF Tabs
The “Capex as % of Revenue” Trick
Capex is the biggest driver of BS complexity (Net Block) and Investing CF. The simplest and most reliable approach for a first-pass model: use Capex as % of Revenue. CF tab → Capital Expenditure ÷ P&L tab → Revenue for each of the last 5 years. Calculate 5-year average Capex intensity. Use this as your Capex assumption for projection years. For maintenance capex companies (CDSL, TCS), this ratio is low and stable (1–4%). For growth capex companies (manufacturers, telecom), it is higher and more variable — in those cases, use management guidance from quarterly earnings calls.
→ CF tab → Capex (absolute) ÷ P&L Revenue for each year
→ Calculate 5-year Capex intensity % → average = base assumption
→ Net Block = Prior NB + Projected Capex − D&A (from P&L)
✔ Stable Capex % = reliable Net Block model ⚠ Guided capex = use management guidance instead ✗ Lumpy capex = model individual project schedule
5
The Complete Balance Check — Most Important Trick
The “Balance Sheet Balances” Trick — The Model’s Health Check
The most important thing about any 3-statement model: the Balance Sheet MUST balance in every projection year (Total Assets = Total Equity + Total Liabilities). If it does not balance, there is an error somewhere in the model. The trick: always leave Cash as the “plug” line — the cell that absorbs any rounding or linkage error. Cash = Prior Cash + Net Cash Flow from the CF statement. Build a check row: Assets − (Liabilities + Equity). If it is non-zero, highlight it in red and find the error before using the model.
→ Total Assets row = Sum of all asset line items
→ Total L + E row = Sum of all liabilities + all equity
→ CHECK row = Total Assets − Total (L + E) → must equal zero
→ If non-zero: check RE linkage → then Net Block linkage → then Cash plug
→ Most common error: Retained Earnings not linked to PAT correctly
→ Second most common: Depreciation not reducing Net Block on BS
→ Third: Cash plug not pulling from CF statement’s net cash line
→ When BS balances → all 6 linkages are working correctly
✔ CHECK = 0 in all years = model is correctly linked ⚠ CHECK non-zero = find which linkage is broken ✗ Never use a model where BS does not balance — results are meaningless
