01 — Key Learning Outcome

Apply Probabilistic Valuation Using Simulation

By the end of this module you will understand how Monte Carlo simulation transforms a single-point DCF into a probability distribution, know which inputs to vary and by how much, and be able to answer the most important investment question: “What is the probability this stock is undervalued right now?”

🎲
Your Core Takeaway — Valuation Is a Distribution, Not a Number
Every DCF assumption you make — revenue growth, EBITDA margin, WACC, terminal growth rate — is uncertain. Monte Carlo embraces this uncertainty instead of hiding it behind a single “intrinsic value.” The result is not one number but a bell curve of thousands of possible valuations.
Imagine a weather forecaster. They do not say “it will rain 14.3 mm tomorrow.” They say “there is a 70% chance of rain.” That probability is computed by running thousands of atmospheric simulations with slightly different starting conditions and seeing what fraction produce rain. Monte Carlo does the same for a DCF. You define each input as a range (e.g., revenue growth is normally distributed with mean 18% and standard deviation 4%) rather than a single value. The computer runs your DCF model 10,000 times, each time drawing a random value for each input from its distribution. The result is 10,000 intrinsic values. You then read the distribution: P50 (median) tells you the most likely value. P10 tells you the worst 10% scenario. P90 tells you the best 10%. And most importantly: what percentage of simulations produce an intrinsic value above the current market price? That percentage IS the probability the stock is undervalued today.
🎲 Random sampling from input distributions 📊 Output: bell curve of 10,000 valuations 📋 P10 / P50 / P90 percentiles ⚖️ P(undervalued) = buy signal
02 — How Monte Carlo Works — Six Steps

From Single DCF to 10,000 Simulations in Six Steps

Monte Carlo sounds complex but follows a clear six-step process. Each step is understandable, and the tools to run it range from free Excel plugins to Python scripts — all accessible to any investor.

1
Define Input Distributions
Turn Point Estimates into Ranges
Every assumption in your DCF (revenue growth, margin, WACC, terminal growth) gets replaced with a probability distribution instead of a single number. The most common distributions: Normal (bell curve, mean ± std dev) for revenue growth and margins. Uniform (equal probability anywhere in a range) for WACC. Triangular (min, most likely, max) for terminal growth rate. Each distribution captures your uncertainty honestly.
CDSL Revenue g: Normal(mean=18%, std=4%) → can be 10% to 26% with 68% probability between 14%–22%
2
Set Correlations Between Inputs
Connect Related Assumptions
Real-world inputs are correlated. When revenue grows fast, EBITDA margins often expand (operating leverage). When macro stress hits, revenue AND margins AND WACC all worsen simultaneously. Correlating your inputs prevents the simulation from producing unrealistic combinations (high revenue + collapsing margins + low WACC all at once). A simple positive correlation of 0.6 between revenue growth and margin expansion is sufficient for most models.
CDSL: Revenue g and EBITDA margin have correlation +0.55 (higher volumes → operating leverage → higher margin)
3
Run the DCF Engine 10,000 Times
Thousands of Simultaneous Scenarios
For each simulation (1 of 10,000), the model draws one random value from each input distribution, runs the complete DCF calculation (3-statement model → FCF → Terminal Value → discount to PV → equity value per share), and records the output. This is repeated 10,000 times in seconds using Excel with @Risk plugin, Python (numpy/scipy), or Google Sheets with Apps Script. Each of the 10,000 outputs is one possible intrinsic value under one possible set of realistic assumptions.
Simulation #4,721: Revenue g=16.2%, Margin=63.1%, WACC=11.4%, g=4.8% → IV = ₹1,480/share
4
Build the Output Distribution
Plot the Histogram of Valuations
The 10,000 intrinsic values are sorted and plotted as a histogram (bar chart of frequencies). This distribution is typically bell-shaped but may be right-skewed for high-growth companies (tail of very high valuations under optimistic scenarios). Key metrics extracted: Mean (average intrinsic value across all simulations), Median/P50 (half the simulations above, half below), Standard deviation (spread of uncertainty), P10 and P90 (10th and 90th percentile values).
CDSL distribution: Mean ₹1,680, Median ₹1,620, Std Dev ₹480, P10 ₹880, P90 ₹2,680
5
Read the Percentiles (P10 / P50 / P90)
What the Percentiles Tell You
P50 (Median) is the most likely intrinsic value — the value that half the simulations exceed. Use this as your primary valuation reference, analogous to the Base Case. P10 is the value such that only 10% of simulations produce a worse outcome — your stress-tested downside. P90 is the value that only 10% of simulations exceed — your realistic upside. The range P10–P90 is the 80th percentile confidence interval for the stock’s fair value.
CDSL: P10=₹880 (worst 10%), P50=₹1,620 (most likely), P90=₹2,680 (best 10%). 80% CI: ₹880–₹2,680
6
Calculate P(Undervalued) — The Buy Signal
What % of Simulations Say “Buy”?
The most powerful output: count how many of the 10,000 simulations produce an intrinsic value above the current market price. Divide by 10,000. This is P(Undervalued) — the probability the stock is undervalued right now, given your model assumptions. P(Undervalued) >60% = high conviction buy. P(Undervalued) 40–60% = fairly valued, marginal. P(Undervalued) <30% = likely overvalued, wait. CDSL at ₹2,590: only 31% of simulations produce IV >₹2,590 → the stock appears overvalued at current price.
CDSL at ₹2,590: 3,100 of 10,000 simulations → IV > ₹2,590 → P(Undervalued) = 31% → overvalued at current price

📈 Interpreting P(Undervalued) for Investment Decisions

P(Undervalued) >70%: Strong buy signal. More than 7,000 of 10,000 plausible futures produce intrinsic value above market price. High conviction entry. P(Undervalued) 50–70%: Moderate buy. Slightly more likely to be undervalued than overvalued. Consider a partial position. P(Undervalued) 30–50%: Fairly valued. No strong signal either way. Wait for price movement or new information. P(Undervalued) <30%: Likely overvalued at current price. Market is pricing in optimistic assumptions. Caution or avoid.

☀️ MC vs Scenario Analysis — Key Differences

Scenario Analysis (Module 13) gives 3 hand-crafted stories (Bull/Base/Bear) with specific, narratively coherent inputs. Best for communicating with stakeholders — each scenario has a clear story. Monte Carlo gives a full continuous distribution of 10,000 outcomes, captures interactions between variables, and produces statistically grounded probability statements. Best for quantitative risk assessment. Use both: Scenario analysis for the investment narrative, Monte Carlo for statistical confidence in the buy/hold/sell signal. They complement rather than replace each other.

🚨 The Garbage-In-Garbage-Out Warning

Monte Carlo is only as good as its input distributions. If you set revenue growth mean=25% (too optimistic) with std=2% (too narrow), the simulation produces 10,000 valuations all clustered around an over-optimistic value. The statistical precision (10,000 runs) gives false confidence. Always anchor mean to historical data from Screener.in and std dev to actual historical variability. A wide std dev that feels “uncertain” is usually more honest than a narrow one that feels “precise”. Garbage input distributions produce garbage probability distributions, regardless of how many simulations you run.

05 — Tricks to Run Monte Carlo Faster

5 Shortcuts to Reliable Monte Carlo in 30 Minutes

These practical tricks let you build and run a Monte Carlo simulation for any Nifty 500 company using free tools and Screener.in data in under 30 minutes.

1
Screener.in → Excel — Calibrate σ in 5 Minutes
The “10-Year STDEV from Screener” Trick
The fastest, most honest way to get distribution parameters: copy 10 years of data from Screener.in directly into Excel and use the STDEV() formula. For revenue growth: P&L tab → Sales row → paste 10 annual values into Excel → calculate year-on-year growth for each → =STDEV(growth range) = your revenue growth sigma. Repeat for OPM% (EBITDA margin sigma). These two calculations, taking 5 minutes each, give you the two most important input parameters for the Monte Carlo model.
→ Screener.in P&L tab → right-click Sales row → copy 10 years to Excel
→ Calculate 9 year-on-year growth rates → =STDEV(growth rates) = revenue σ
→ Repeat for OPM% row → =STDEV(OPM% values) = margin σ
✔ 5 minutes per company for μ and σ ⚠ Use 10-year data, not 3-year (too recent) ✗ Never estimate σ from memory
2
Excel MC Using NORM.INV + RAND
The “Poor Man’s Monte Carlo” in Excel (Free)
Run 1,000 MC simulations in Excel without any plugin. Step 1: In a column, create 1,000 rows of random revenue growth using =NORM.INV(RAND(), 18%, 4%). Step 2: Adjacent column for margin: =NORM.INV(RAND(), 62%, 3%). Step 3: Adjacent column computes intrinsic value from your DCF formula using those random inputs. Step 4: =PERCENTILE(IV column, 0.1) = P10, =PERCENTILE(IV column, 0.5) = P50, =PERCENTILE(IV column, 0.9) = P90. Press F9 to regenerate all random numbers and see new percentiles. 1,000 runs is enough for P50; use 5,000+ for stable P10/P90.
→ =NORM.INV(RAND(),18%,4%) in 1,000 rows = random revenue growth samples
→ DCF formula using those samples → 1,000 intrinsic values in output column
→ =PERCENTILE(output,0.1/0.5/0.9) for P10, P50, P90. Press F9 to refresh
✔ 100% free, no plugin needed ⚠ 1,000 runs = stable P50 but P10/P90 needs 5,000+ ✗ Slow for complex models; use Python for speed
3
Correlation Check from Screener.in
The “=CORREL() to Find Input Correlation” Trick
Before running Monte Carlo, check the actual correlation between your inputs using Screener.in historical data. Copy 10 years of revenue growth and 10 years of OPM% into Excel. Use =CORREL(revenue growth range, OPM% range). If correlation >0.3: use a correlated input model (Cholesky decomposition or simply use correlated RAND() pairs). The “P(Undervalued) = COUNTIF / Total” Trick
The most actionable MC output — P(Undervalued) — is computed with a single Excel formula. After generating 10,000 simulated intrinsic values in a column: =COUNTIF(IV_column, “>”&market_price) / 10000. This gives the fraction of simulations where IV > market price → the probability the stock is undervalued today. Set a decision rule: if this number is >60% → consider buying. 40–60% → fairly valued, watch. <30% → likely overvalued. This single number replaces an entire qualitative research process with a statistically grounded buy/hold/sell signal.
→ Run 10,000 MC simulations → IV column in Excel
→ =COUNTIF(IV_column,">"&B2)/10000 where B2 = current market price
→ Result = P(Undervalued). >60% = buy signal. <30% = caution

06 — Reference Glossary

Monte Carlo DCF — Key Terms Explained Simply

Every term used in this module — defined in plain English so you can apply Monte Carlo confidently without needing a statistics degree.

Term
What It Means in Simple Words
Key Point
Monte Carlo Simulation A computational technique that runs a model (your DCF) thousands of times, each time using a randomly sampled set of inputs drawn from probability distributions. Named after the Monte Carlo casino because it relies on random sampling, just as a casino relies on random outcomes. The output is not one number but a full distribution of possible values. Originally developed by scientists working on the atomic bomb at Los Alamos; now standard in finance, engineering, and risk management worldwide. 10,000 DCFs
Probability Distribution A mathematical description of how likely different values of a variable are. Instead of saying “revenue will grow 18%,” a distribution says “revenue growth is most likely 18% but could be anywhere from 6% to 30%, with probabilities for every possible value in between.” The three distributions used in MC DCF: Normal (bell curve, symmetric) for revenue and margins. Uniform (flat, equal probability in a range) for WACC. Triangular (defined by min, mode, max) for terminal growth rate. Range of values
Mean (μ) The average or central value of the distribution — your “best estimate” of the input. For Monte Carlo, the mean is the most important parameter because it determines where the output distribution is centred. Always anchor the mean to historical data from Screener.in: mean revenue growth = 5-year CAGR from P&L tab. Mean EBITDA margin = 5-year average OPM% from P&L tab. Using a mean that is more optimistic than history produces a systematically biased P(Undervalued) signal. Best estimate
Standard Deviation (σ) The measure of how spread out the distribution is — how uncertain or volatile the input is. A revenue growth distribution with mean=18% and std=4% means 68% of scenarios will have growth between 14% and 22%, and 95% between 10% and 26%. The most critical rule: derive σ from Screener.in historical data using =STDEV() on 10 years of annual values. Never set σ based on intuition. Underestimating σ creates false precision; overestimating creates unnecessarily wide output ranges. Historical spread
Normal Distribution The classic bell-curve distribution. Symmetric around the mean. 68% of values fall within ±1σ of mean. 95% within ±2σ. 99.7% within ±3σ. Used for revenue growth and EBITDA margins because these variables tend to cluster around their historical average with occasional deviations that follow a bell shape. In Excel: =NORM.INV(RAND(), mean, std_dev) generates a random value from a Normal distribution. Bell curve
Uniform Distribution A distribution where every value in a range [min, max] is equally likely. Used for WACC in Monte Carlo because the uncertainty in WACC (from beta estimation and ERP) is roughly flat within a range, not bell-shaped. =RAND()*(max-min)+min in Excel generates a Uniform random value. For WACC: Uniform(10.1%, 12.1%) says any WACC between 10.1% and 12.1% is equally plausible, which reflects genuine uncertainty in the CAPM inputs. Equal probability
Triangular Distribution A distribution defined by three parameters: minimum (most pessimistic value), mode (most likely value), and maximum (most optimistic value). Used for terminal growth rate g because analysts have a clear view of the most likely value (5% = India CPI) with bounded extremes (4% bear, 6% bull). Unlike Normal, it cannot go negative or exceed the maximum, which prevents the simulation from generating g=10% (economically impossible). More suitable than Normal when you have clear min/max constraints. Min / Mode / Max
Percentile (P10, P50, P90) A percentile tells you the value below which a given fraction of the simulation results fall. P10: 10% of simulations produced a lower value — your realistic downside/stress test. P50 (Median): 50% of simulations are above and 50% are below — your central estimate, analogous to the Base Case in Scenario Analysis. P90: 90% of simulations are below this — your optimistic upside. The P10–P90 range is the 80% confidence interval for the stock’s intrinsic value. In Excel: =PERCENTILE(range, 0.1/0.5/0.9). P10/P50/P90
P(Undervalued) The most actionable Monte Carlo output: the fraction of simulations where the computed intrinsic value exceeds the current market price.
P(UV) = COUNT(simulations where IV > market price) ÷ total simulations
This is the probability the stock is undervalued today, given your input distributions. >60% = high conviction buy. 40–60% = fairly valued. <30% = likely overvalued. In Excel: =COUNTIF(IV_column,">"&market_price)/10000.		 Buy signal %
Input Correlation A statistical measure of how two inputs move together. Correlation +1 = they always move in the same direction. Correlation 0 = independent. Correlation −1 = they always move in opposite directions. In DCF: revenue growth and EBITDA margin typically have a positive correlation (+0.3 to +0.6) because higher volumes create operating leverage (fixed costs spread over more revenue → margin expands). Ignoring this correlation lets the simulation generate unrealistic combinations (high revenue + collapsing margins). In Excel: =CORREL(revenue growth range, OPM% range). =CORREL()
Law of Large Numbers A mathematical theorem stating that as the number of random samples increases, the sample average converges to the true population mean. In Monte Carlo: as you increase simulations from 100 to 10,000, the estimated P50, P10, P90, and P(Undervalued) all converge to stable, reliable values. Below 1,000 simulations, percentile estimates are noisy (different each time you run). Above 10,000, they are effectively stable. This is why 10,000 simulations is the professional standard. 10,000+ runs
NORM.INV (Excel) Excel function that converts a random probability (0–1) into a value from a Normal distribution.
=NORM.INV(RAND(), mean, std_dev)
RAND() generates a uniform random number between 0 and 1. NORM.INV converts it to the corresponding value from the Normal distribution. For example, =NORM.INV(RAND(), 18%, 4%) generates a random revenue growth drawn from Normal(18%, 4%). Repeat 10,000 times to get 10,000 random revenue growth scenarios. This is the core formula for building Monte Carlo in Excel without any plugin.		 Excel formula
Garbage-In-Garbage-Out (GIGO) The principle that a Monte Carlo simulation is only as reliable as its input distributions. If you set an artificially narrow std dev (false precision) or an optimistic mean, the 10,000 simulations will all cluster around the wrong value, giving a misleading P(Undervalued) signal. The statistical precision of 10,000 runs cannot compensate for poorly chosen input parameters. Always anchor mean to Screener.in historical average and std dev to STDEV() of historical values. Monte Carlo amplifies whatever bias exists in the inputs. Honest inputs!
Confidence Interval (CI) A range of values that contains the true intrinsic value with a specified probability. The P10–P90 range from Monte Carlo is an 80% confidence interval: in 80% of plausible future scenarios, the stock’s intrinsic value will fall within this range. A tighter CI (narrow P10–P90) indicates less uncertainty. A wide CI means valuation is highly sensitive to input assumptions. An honest DCF always presents a confidence interval, not a single number. Monte Carlo provides this naturally; scenario analysis provides an approximation. P10–P90 range
