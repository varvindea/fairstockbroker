01 — Key Learning Outcome

Back-Solve Market Expectations from the Current Stock Price

By the end of this module you will be able to open Screener.in, get five numbers from the P&L and CF tabs, and compute in under five minutes the growth rate the market is implying for any Nifty 500 stock — then judge whether that expectation is achievable or not.

🔎
Your Core Takeaway — Read What the Market Believes
Every stock price contains a hidden assumption about future growth. The Reverse DCF makes that hidden assumption visible. Once you can see it, you can judge it — and that judgment is the basis of every intelligent buy or sell decision.
Think of a stock price like a bet. When you pay ₹2,590 for one share of CDSL, you are making a bet that CDSL’s future cash flows, discounted at WACC, are worth at least ₹2,590 today. But what growth rate does that price imply? The Reverse DCF answers this by rearranging the standard DCF formula. Normal DCF: “If growth is X%, the stock is worth ₹Y.” Reverse DCF: “If the stock is priced at ₹2,590, then growth must be X% for that to make sense.” Once you have the implied growth rate, you ask: has this company ever sustained that growth rate? Does the industry support it? Does management have the track record? If yes → the price may be fair. If no → the market is pricing in a scenario that is historically unprecedented, and the stock carries downside risk even without a catastrophe.

02 — Normal DCF vs Reverse DCF

Two Sides of the Same Equation — How Each Works

The Normal DCF and Reverse DCF use exactly the same model and the same inputs. The only difference is which variable is unknown. Understanding both directions makes you a far more complete analyst.

📊
Direction 1 — Forward
Normal DCF: Assumptions → Value
You define all assumptions (growth rate, margins, WACC, terminal growth rate) and the DCF model computes a single intrinsic value. The question answered: “Given these assumptions, what is this stock worth?” This is how Modules 9–14 work. The weakness: you can choose assumptions that make the stock look cheap or expensive depending on your bias. Normal DCF requires honesty about assumptions.
IV = PV(FCFs, Year 1–5) + PV(Terminal Value)
All inputs known • Revenue growth, Margin, WACC, Terminal g all specified by analyst • Output = Intrinsic Value per share • Compare vs market price for buy/sell signal
Input: Revenue growth = 18%, Margin = 62%, WACC = 11.1%, g = 5%
Process: Build FCF → Terminal Value → Discount all → Add cash → Divide by shares
Output: Intrinsic Value = ₹1,620/share. Market = ₹2,590. Verdict: overvalued by 37%
💡 Strength: Gives a precise valuation range with full scenario analysis. Weakness: The answer is only as good as the assumptions. An optimistic analyst and a pessimistic analyst will produce wildly different “intrinsic values” from the same company — both claiming their DCF is correct.
🔎
Direction 2 — Reverse
Reverse DCF: Price → Implied Growth
You fix WACC and terminal growth rate, then back-solve to find the revenue growth rate that makes the DCF output exactly equal to today’s market price. The question answered: “What must the company achieve for this price to be justified?” The result is an implied growth rate that you then compare to historical performance and industry fundamentals. This is assumption-free valuation — no analyst bias, just market-revealed expectations.
Market Price = PV(FCFs at implied g) + PV(TV) → Solve for g
Market Price known (Screener.in homepage) • WACC from Module 11 • Terminal g assumed 5% • Solve: what g makes DCF = Market Price? • Output = Implied growth rate
Input: Market Price = ₹2,590, WACC = 11.1%, Terminal g = 5%, Current FCF = ₹515 Cr
Process: Try different growth rates until DCF output = ₹2,590. Use Goal Seek in Excel.
Output: Implied g ≈ 26% p.a. for 5 years. Compare vs CDSL historical 25% CAGR. Verdict: stretched but achievable
💡 Strength: No assumptions to defend. The market price IS the input. The implied growth rate reveals what Mr Market believes. Use it to ask: Is implied g above or below historical g? If above → market is pricing in acceleration. If below → market expects deceleration. Either way, the insight is real and grounded.

📋 How to Run Goal Seek in Excel (30 Seconds)

Step 1: Build the DCF engine in Excel with the growth rate (g) in a single cell (e.g., B2). All FCF projections reference B2. Terminal Value references B2. Total EV is computed. Step 2: Data → What-If Analysis → Goal Seek. Set cell: [EV output cell]. To value: 26,165 (target EV in Crores). By changing cell: B2 (the growth rate). Step 3: Click OK. Excel iterates in under 1 second and places the implied growth rate in B2. Done. Alternative: Use Excel Solver for more complex models or if Goal Seek doesn’t converge. Python’s scipy.optimize.brentq achieves the same in 3 lines of code.

📈 How Terminal g Affects Implied Near-Term Growth

The terminal growth assumption significantly changes the implied near-term g. Try three terminal growth scenarios for CDSL at price ₹2,590: • If terminal g = 4% → implied near-term growth ≈ 29% p.a. (very aggressive) • If terminal g = 5% → implied near-term growth ≈ 26% p.a. (stretched) • If terminal g = 6% → implied near-term growth ≈ 22% p.a. (more achievable). This is why Reverse DCF must always be presented with sensitivity across at least three terminal g assumptions. The “implied growth” number is not a single fact — it is conditional on the terminal g you assume.

☀️ The Buy/Sell Decision from Implied Growth

Implied g below historical average → potential buy. The market is pricing in deceleration that history doesn’t support. Implied g near historical average → fairly valued. Market is pricing in continuation of existing trends. Implied g above historical best → caution. The market is pricing in a growth rate the company has never sustained. Any disappointment will disappoint buyers. Rule of thumb: Implied g > (historical 10Y CAGR + 3%) = overvalued signal. Implied g < (historical 10Y CAGR − 5%) = potentially undervalued signal.

🚨 The “Market Is Always Right” Trap

The Reverse DCF reveals market expectations, not market truth. The market can price in growth rates that are genuinely unrealistic. In 2000, Infosys’s market price implied 50%+ revenue growth for 10 years — a physically impossible expectation. In 2022, many EV stocks priced in implied growth of 60%+ annually. The Reverse DCF does not tell you the market is right; it tells you what the market believes. Your job is to judge whether the implied belief is realistic. Use Screener.in’s historical data as the reality anchor. If the implied growth rate has never been sustained by any company in the same sector for 5 years — the stock is likely speculative, not investment-grade.

General rule: Reverse DCF’s “compare implied g vs history” insight only works when the historical data represents the same business model as the current one. Major changes — SaaS pivot, geographic expansion, product diversification, management change — can make the historical comparison invalid. When in doubt, use peer comparable growth rates instead of own history as the benchmark for evaluating the implied growth rate.
How to identify this situation: Screener.in Ratios tab shows a sudden jump in Asset Turnover, Revenue per Employee, or EBITDA margin improvement that is inconsistent with prior history. If two or more ratios show a structural break in the last 2–3 years, the historical growth comparison may be invalid and peer comparison should be used instead.

The “5-Number Quick-Collect” Trick

If the Implied Growth Rate Has Never Been Sustained by Any Company in This Sector for 5 Consecutive Years, You Are Paying for a Miracle — Not a Business.

06 — Reference Glossary

Reverse DCF — Key Terms Explained Simply

Every term used in this module — defined in plain English so you can apply the Reverse DCF confidently and interpret the results correctly.

Term
What It Means in Simple Words
Key Point
Reverse DCF A valuation technique that starts with the current market price (which is known) and works backwards to find the growth rate that makes the standard DCF model produce exactly that price. Unlike a normal DCF (which asks “what is this stock worth?”), the Reverse DCF asks “what must this company achieve for the current price to be justified?” The answer is the implied growth rate, which is then compared to the company’s historical track record on Screener.in. No assumptions to defend; the market price IS the input. Price → Growth
Implied Growth Rate The revenue or FCF growth rate that the current market price is implicitly assuming for the company over the next 5 years. This is the key output of the Reverse DCF. If implied growth rate = 26%, it means: for an investor paying today’s price to earn a fair return (equal to WACC), the company must grow at 26% p.a. for 5 years. Compare this against the company’s historical CAGR from Screener.in P&L tab to judge whether the market’s expectation is realistic or not. The implied growth rate is also called the “market-implied growth rate” or “priced-in growth rate.” Market expectation
Back-Solve To start with the output (market price) and work backwards to find the input (growth rate) that produces that output, holding all other variables constant. In a standard DCF, growth is an input and intrinsic value is an output. In Reverse DCF, intrinsic value is set equal to market price (the target) and growth becomes the unknown. Excel Goal Seek automates the back-solving process: it iterates through growth rate values until the DCF output exactly matches the target. Data → What-If Analysis → Goal Seek → takes under 1 second to converge. Goal Seek in Excel
Goal Seek (Excel) An Excel built-in tool that automatically finds the input value that produces a desired output value. In Reverse DCF:
Data → What-If Analysis → Goal Seek
Set cell: the cell containing your computed Total Enterprise Value. To value: the target Enterprise Value (Market Cap − Net Cash). By changing cell: the growth rate cell (g). Excel iterates the growth rate until the DCF output equals the target. Returns the implied growth rate in under 1 second. No manual trial-and-error needed.		 30-second solve
Target Enterprise Value The Enterprise Value that the Reverse DCF model must match. Derived from the current market price:
Target EV = Market Cap − Net Cash (or + Net Debt)
If a company has Market Cap ₹27,065 Cr and Net Cash ₹900 Cr: Target EV = ₹27,065 − ₹900 = ₹26,165 Cr. This is the value that all future discounted cash flows must add up to (at the implied growth rate) for the current stock price to be justified. Found entirely from Screener.in: Market Cap from homepage, Net Cash from Balance Sheet.		 MCap − Net Cash
Net Cash (for Reverse DCF) Cash and liquid investments minus all financial debt.
Net Cash = Cash + Current Investments + Long-term Investments − Total Borrowings
Found entirely from Screener.in Balance Sheet tab. For CDSL: no debt + ₹900+ Cr in government securities = ₹900 Cr net cash. Net Cash is subtracted from Market Cap to get the Enterprise Value — the value attributable to the operating business (not the cash). For companies with net debt, it is added to Market Cap to get EV.		 B/S tab Screener
Historical CAGR (Benchmark) The Compound Annual Growth Rate of revenue over the past 10 years — the primary benchmark for evaluating whether the implied growth rate is realistic. Found directly on Screener.in:
P&L tab → Sales row → 10Y CAGR shown at the bottom of the row
The decision rule: implied g < historical 10Y CAGR −5pp = potential buy. Implied g ≈ historical CAGR = fairly valued. Implied g > historical CAGR +5pp = caution. Always use 10-year CAGR (not 3-year or 5-year) to avoid boom/bust distortion.		 10Y CAGR benchmark
Priced for Perfection An investment expression meaning the stock price already incorporates the most optimistic realistic scenario — leaving no margin of safety. In Reverse DCF terms: a stock is “priced for perfection” when the implied growth rate equals or exceeds the company’s best historical growth period. Even modest underperformance vs expectations will produce significant share price declines. Example: CDSL at ₹2,590 implies 26% growth — matching its best-ever 5-year period. Any disappointment (SEBI charge cut, market slowdown) risks a sharp correction because there is no margin for error built into the price. No margin for error
Margin of Safety (in Reverse DCF) The cushion between what the market implies (implied g) and what the company can realistically deliver (historical g). A large positive margin of safety means the market is pricing in slow growth when the company can likely deliver strong growth.
Margin of Safety = Historical CAGR − Implied Growth Rate
Positive margin of safety (implied g well below historical g) = high conviction buy. Zero margin of safety (implied g = historical g) = fairly valued. Negative margin of safety (implied g above historical g) = risk of disappointment. The larger the gap between implied and historical, the stronger the investment opportunity or risk.		 Historical − Implied g
Mid-Cycle FCF For cyclical companies (commodities, chemicals, steel, fertilisers), the “normal” free cash flow at a mid-point in the business cycle — not peak and not trough. Used as the starting FCF in Reverse DCF instead of the most recent year’s FCF.
Mid-Cycle FCF ≈ 5-year average of (Cash from Ops − Capex) from Screener CF tab
Using peak FCF as the Reverse DCF base makes the implied growth look artificially low (stock appears cheap at peak). Using trough FCF makes implied growth look artificially high (stock appears expensive at trough). Mid-cycle FCF eliminates this distortion.		 5-year avg FCF
Terminal Growth Rate Sensitivity (Reverse DCF) Because the terminal growth rate assumption significantly affects the implied near-term growth from Reverse DCF, it is essential to run three scenarios:
terminal g = 4% (bear) → 5% (base) → 6% (bull)
Each produces a different implied near-term growth rate. The range of implied growth rates (rather than a single number) is the honest output. If all three implied growth rates are above the historical CAGR, the stock is expensive across all terminal growth assumptions. If all three are below, it is cheap across all assumptions. The base case (terminal g = 5%) is presented as the primary output, flanked by the bear and bull sensitivity.		 3 scenarios always
Structural Business Model Change A fundamental shift in how a company generates revenue, making historical growth rates incomparable to future growth rates. Examples: traditional business pivoting to SaaS, a distributor becoming an e-commerce platform, a manufacturer adding a service segment. When a structural change has occurred, the historical CAGR comparison in Reverse DCF becomes invalid because the business generating future growth is fundamentally different from the business in the historical data. In this case, use sector peer CAGR (comparable companies in the new business model) as the benchmark instead of own history. Use peer CAGR
