01 — Key Learning Outcome

Compute WACC for Indian Listed Companies

By the end of this module you will be able to compute the Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) for any Nifty 500 company — step by step, using free Screener.in data and Damodaran’s India-specific inputs for the risk-free rate, equity risk premium, and sector betas.

⚖️
Your Core Takeaway — WACC is the Price of Capital
WACC is the minimum return a company must earn on its assets to satisfy both its equity investors and its lenders. It is the “hurdle rate” for all investments, and the discount rate that converts future free cash flows into today’s intrinsic value in a DCF.
Imagine you borrowed ₹50,000 from a bank at 10% interest and raised ₹50,000 from a friend who expects 15% return. Your total capital is ₹1 lakh. To keep both happy, you need to earn at least: (50% × 10%) + (50% × 15%) = 12.5% on your ₹1 lakh investment. That 12.5% is your WACC. If your business earns 18%, you are creating value (18% > 12.5%). If it earns only 9%, you are destroying value (9% < 12.5%). For a listed company: Equity = market cap (cost = CAPM return shareholders expect), Debt = bonds + loans (cost = interest rate × (1 − tax)). WACC brings both together into one discount rate used in every DCF model.

02 — Three Components of WACC

Beta, Cost of Equity & Cost of Debt Explained

WACC has three building blocks. Each must be calculated carefully for Indian companies, using India-specific inputs (not US defaults). Damodaran’s freely available India data is the professional standard.

β
Component 1 — Market Risk Measure
Beta (β) — How Risky is the Stock?
Beta measures how much a stock moves relative to the overall market. A Beta of 1.0 means the stock moves in sync with the market. Beta >1 = more volatile than market (riskier). Beta <1 = less volatile (more stable). Beta = 0 would be a risk-free asset. Beta is used in the CAPM formula to calculate the Cost of Equity. For Indian companies, use the unlevered beta from Damodaran’s India dataset and re-lever it using the company’s actual D/E ratio.
Levered Beta = Unlevered Beta × [1 + (1 − Tax Rate) × (D/E)]
Unlevered Beta = from Damodaran India sector data (pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar) • Tax Rate = effective tax rate from Screener.in P&L • D/E = Total Debt ÷ Market Cap from Balance Sheet + market data
Step 1: Go to Damodaran’s site (pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar) → Betas by Sector (Emerging Markets) → find the company’s sector row → note the Unlevered Beta
Step 2: Screener.in Balance Sheet → get Total Borrowings (Debt) and Market Cap (homepage) → calculate D/E ratio = Debt ÷ Market Cap
Step 3: Apply formula: Levered Beta = Unlevered Beta × [1 + (1 − 0.245) × D/E]. Higher D/E = higher Beta = higher Cost of Equity
💡 Example (CDSL): Depository sector Unlevered Beta = ~0.55 (Damodaran India). Zero debt → D/E = 0. Levered Beta = 0.55 × [1 + 0] = 0.55. CDSL is much less risky than the market (Beta <1) because its revenues are non-cyclical and monopolistic. Compare: Suzlon (wind energy, high debt) has a levered Beta of ~1.8 — much more volatile.
💲
Component 2 — Equity Investor Return
Cost of Equity (Ke) — CAPM Formula
Cost of Equity is the return equity investors require for taking on the risk of owning the company’s shares. If a company cannot deliver this return, shareholders will sell the stock. Cost of Equity is calculated using the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM): Ke = Risk-Free Rate + Beta × Equity Risk Premium. For India, the risk-free rate is the 10-year G-Sec yield and the ERP comes from Damodaran’s India-specific estimate.
Ke = Rf + β × ERP
Rf = India 10-Year G-Sec yield (~7.0% as of 2025) • β = Levered Beta (from Step 1) • ERP = Equity Risk Premium for India (~7.5–8.0% per Damodaran) • All three inputs are free and publicly available
Rf: RBI website → current 10-year G-Sec yield (benchmark bond). OR use NSE India G-Sec yields. Currently ~6.8–7.2% (check current rate before using)
ERP: Damodaran India → Country Risk Premium for India (~5%) + Mature Market ERP (~4.5%) = India ERP ~7.5–8%. Updated annually on Damodaran’s site
CAPM: Ke = 7.0% + 0.55 × 7.5% = 7.0% + 4.1% = 11.1% Cost of Equity for CDSL
💡 Plain English: A CDSL equity investor (buying the stock) requires at least 11.1% annual return. This includes 7% compensation for inflation/opportunity cost (G-Sec rate) + 4.1% compensation for the risk of owning a stock vs a G-Sec. If CDSL cannot deliver 11.1% total return (dividend + share price appreciation), investors will sell and move to safer assets.
🏭
Component 3 — Lender’s Required Return
Cost of Debt (Kd) — After-Tax Interest Rate
Cost of Debt is what the company pays lenders — the interest rate on its borrowings. Since interest payments are tax-deductible (they reduce taxable profit), the actual cost to the company is the interest rate after the tax benefit: Kd(after-tax) = Interest Rate × (1 − Tax Rate). This is why debt financing is cheaper than equity — the government subsidises it through tax deductibility. For Indian companies, the Interest Rate is easily derived from Screener.in P&L and Balance Sheet data.
Kd (after-tax) = (Finance Cost ÷ Total Debt) × (1 − Tax Rate)
Finance Cost = Screener.in P&L tab → “Finance Cost” row • Total Debt = Screener.in Balance Sheet → “Borrowings” row • Tax Rate = P&L → Tax ÷ PBT • Zero debt companies: Kd = 0, WACC = Ke
Pre-tax Rate: Finance Cost (P&L) ÷ Average Total Debt = actual interest rate charged. E.g., ₹200 Cr interest ÷ ₹2,000 Cr debt = 10% pre-tax rate
After-tax: 10% × (1 − 0.245) = 10% × 0.755 = 7.55% after-tax Cost of Debt. This is what actually costs the company
Zero-debt companies (CDSL, Bajaj Auto): Kd = 0 and WACC = Ke entirely. Much simpler WACC calculation
💡 Tax shield analogy: A company pays ₹100 Cr interest. This reduces taxable profit by ₹100 Cr → saves ₹24.5 Cr in tax (at 24.5% rate). So the actual out-of-pocket cost of that ₹100 Cr interest is only ₹75.5 Cr. The after-tax interest rate = effective rate × (1 − 24.5%). Equity has no such tax shield — dividends come from after-tax profits.

WACC = (Ke × E/V) + (Kd × (1−T) × D/V)
WACC = [Cost of Equity × Equity Weight] + [Cost of Debt × (1−Tax) × Debt Weight]

⚖️ WACC for Zero-Debt Companies vs Levered Companies

Zero-debt (CDSL, Bajaj Auto, TCS): D/E = 0 → Levered Beta = Unlevered Beta → WACC = Ke only. Simpler calculation, lower WACC (no debt amplification of risk). Levered companies (Polycab, Reliance): Debt amplifies Beta → higher Levered Beta → higher Ke. But lower WACC through tax shield on interest. The WACC formula trades off: more debt = higher Beta (bad) but lower after-tax cost (good). Optimal capital structure minimises WACC. In practice, for Indian companies with low D/E (<0.3), WACC is very close to Ke.

☀️ Common India-Specific WACC Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1: Using US 10-year Treasury (4.2%) as Rf instead of India G-Sec (7%). This underestimates WACC by ~3% — a massive valuation error. Mistake 2: Using a regression Beta from Yahoo Finance — often based on only 36 months, highly unstable, and uses the wrong market index. Use Damodaran sector Beta instead. Mistake 3: Not applying the country risk premium. India’s ERP is ~7.5–8%, not the US ERP of 4.5%. Missing the country risk premium understates Ke by 3–5%. Mistake 4: Using book value of equity (from balance sheet) instead of market cap for weights.

🚨 WACC Sensitivity — Why It Matters So Much

Example: Company with FY28E FCF of ₹500 Cr and 5% terminal growth rate. At WACC=10%: DCF Value = ₹500 ÷ (10%−5%) = ₹10,000 Cr. At WACC=12%: DCF Value = ₹500 ÷ (12%−5%) = ₹7,143 Cr. A 2% change in WACC produced a 28.6% change in valuation. This is why getting Beta, Rf, and ERP right is not academic — it is the difference between saying a stock is 30% undervalued or 10% overvalued. Always run a WACC sensitivity table (WACC vs terminal growth rate) in every DCF model.

This takes 10 minutes per company — not hours — and produces a WACC that is specific, defensible, and accurate.
When using one WACC is acceptable: If you are doing a relative valuation comparison across multiple companies and need a quick sanity check — a sector-average WACC can serve as a rough benchmark. But it must never be used as the final discount rate in a standalone DCF valuation. The final DCF must always use a company-specific WACC.

The Golden Rule for Indian Company WACC
Rf = India G-Sec (7%) · Beta = Damodaran Sector (Re-levered) · ERP = Damodaran India (~7.5%) · Kd = Finance Cost/Debt × (1−Tax) · Weights = Market Value
These five rules eliminate all the most common WACC errors seen in Indian DCF models. The data for all five is free: RBI website (Rf), Damodaran’s site (Beta + ERP), Screener.in P&L (Finance Cost, Tax), and Screener.in Homepage (Market Cap). A correctly computed WACC for any Nifty 500 company takes under 15 minutes. Getting it right is the difference between a DCF that finds genuine undervaluation and one that creates false confidence through a systematically wrong discount rate.
05 — Tricks to Compute WACC Fast

5 Shortcuts to WACC in Under 15 Minutes

These field-tested tricks let you compute a professional-grade WACC for any Indian listed company using free data — without spreadsheet complexity.

1
Damodaran India Data
The “Sector Beta in 60 Seconds” Trick
Google “Damodaran beta by sector emerging markets” → first result is the Damodaran Excel download. Download it, find your company’s sector, and note the Unlevered Beta corrected for cash (most accurate column). This entire step takes 60 seconds. The sectors most relevant for Indian companies: Software/IT, Consumer Staples, Auto, Pharma, Financial Services, Capital Goods, Metals & Mining, Telecom. Match to the closest one — do not try to find an exact match.
→ Google: “Damodaran betas by sector emerging markets”
→ Download Excel → Column B = Sector → Column E = Unlevered Beta (corrected)
→ Match your company to closest sector → note the Unlevered Beta
✔ IT/Software: ~0.75–0.85 ✔ Consumer Staples: ~0.55–0.70 ⚠ Auto: ~0.88–1.0
2
Screener.in — D/E in 30 Seconds
The “D/E Ratio Fast Calculation” Trick
You need D/E ratio to re-lever the Beta. On Screener.in: Market Cap is on the company homepage top bar (updated live). Total Debt = Balance Sheet tab → Borrowings row → latest year. D/E = Total Borrowings ÷ Market Cap. If D/E < 0.05, the leverage adjustment to Beta is negligible (<4% change) — just use the unlevered Beta directly as the levered Beta and save the calculation step. This covers most quality Indian companies (CDSL, Bajaj Auto, TCS, Infosys, Pidilite, Nestle) where debt is minimal.
→ Screener.in company page: note Market Cap (top bar)
→ Balance Sheet tab → Borrowings row → latest year value
→ D/E = Borrowings ÷ Market Cap. If <0.05: skip re-levering
✔ D/E <0.05: use Unlevered Beta directly as Levered Beta ⚠ D/E 0.05–0.3: apply full Hamada equation ✗ D/E >0.5: Beta and WACC change significantly with leverage
3
Screener.in — P&L Tab
The “Effective Tax Rate from Screener” Trick
You need effective tax rate for both the Beta re-levering formula and the after-tax Cost of Debt calculation. Never use the statutory 30% tax rate. Most Indian companies pay 22–25.17% under the new concessional regime (Sections 115BAA / 115BAB). Get the actual effective rate: Screener.in P&L tab → “Tax” row ÷ “Profit before tax” row for the latest 3 years → average. This is the rate the company actually pays — the one that determines the real after-tax cost of debt and Beta calculation.
→ Screener.in → P&L tab → Tax row ÷ PBT row = effective tax rate
→ Do this for 3 years → average (ignore one-off deferred tax years)
→ Use this rate in: (1) Hamada equation, and (2) Kd × (1 − Tax Rate)
✔ Most large companies: 24–25.17% effective rate ⚠ Some still at 34% (not opted for new regime) ✗ Never use 30% statutory rate for WACC
4
RBI + Damodaran — Two-Minute CAPM
The “India CAPM in 2 Minutes” Trick
Once you have Beta, Ke = Rf + Beta × ERP takes 10 seconds to compute. For India as of 2025: Rf = India 10-Year G-Sec yield (~7.0%) from RBI website or NSE bond data. ERP = India Total ERP (~7.5%) from Damodaran’s Country Risk Excel (updated January each year). For most companies, Ke will range between 10% (very low Beta, stable businesses) and 18% (high Beta, cyclical or high-debt). If your Ke is below 10% for an Indian company, double-check your Rf and ERP — you are likely using US inputs by mistake.
→ Rf: RBI → Markets → Bond market → 10Y G-Sec yield (or NSE bonds page)
→ ERP: Damodaran site → Country Risk → India row → “Total ERP” column
→ Ke = Rf + (Levered Beta × ERP). Cross-check: Ke should be 10–18% for Indian companies
✔ Ke 10–13%: stable, low-Beta businesses (CDSL, Nestle) ⚠ Ke 13–16%: moderate-Beta (auto, chemicals) ✗ Ke <10%: recheck — likely using wrong Rf or ERP
5
Screener.in + All Sources — Full WACC in 15 Minutes
The Complete “15-Minute WACC Worksheet” — Zero-to-WACC for Any Nifty 500 Company
Combine all four tricks into a systematic 15-minute workflow. Print this checklist, fill it in for any company, and you have a professionally defensible WACC. Everything below uses free, publicly available data.
1. Sector Beta: Damodaran EM Excel → find sector → note Unlevered Beta (corrected)
2. D/E ratio: Screener.in → Borrowings ÷ Market Cap (if <0.05: skip re-levering)
3. Tax rate: P&L tab → Tax ÷ PBT → 3-year average effective rate
4. Levered Beta: Unlevered × [1 + (1−Tax) × D/E]. Zero debt? Skip.
5. Rf: RBI 10-Year G-Sec yield (currently ~7%). Check before each model.
6. ERP: Damodaran Country Risk → India Total ERP (~7.5%). Updated annually.
7. Ke: Rf + (Beta × ERP). Sanity check: should be 10–18%.
8. Kd after-tax: (Finance Cost ÷ Avg Debt) × (1−Tax Rate). Zero debt? Kd=0.
Final WACC: (E/V × Ke) + (D/V × Kd). E/V = Market Cap ÷ (Market Cap + Debt). D/V = Debt ÷ (Market Cap + Debt). Sanity check: WACC for zero-debt Nifty companies should be 11–16%. WACC below 9% for an Indian company = input error. WACC above 18% = very high risk or high leverage.
✔ WACC 9–13%: low-risk, zero/low-debt quality businesses ⚠ WACC 13–16%: moderate risk, cyclical or modest debt ✗ WACC <9% for Indian co. = likely input error

The One-Line WACC Rule for Indian Companies
If WACC is Below 9% for Any Indian Equity — Check Your Inputs. You Are Almost Certainly Using a US Risk-Free Rate or a US ERP.
India’s 10-year G-Sec yield alone is ~7%. Even a company with zero Beta (hypothetically risk-free) would have a WACC of 7%. Any real company has Beta >0, which pushes WACC above 7%. Factor in India’s ERP of 7.5% and even a low-Beta company (Beta 0.5) gets a WACC of 7% + 3.75% = 10.75%. A WACC below 9% for an Indian company is a near-certain signal of using US inputs (Rf = 4.2%, ERP = 4.5%) incorrectly. This single sanity check catches the most expensive WACC error in Indian DCF models.
