01 — Key Learning Outcome

Select Peers · Spread Financials · Calculate Multiples

📊
Your Core Takeaway
Comparable Company Analysis (Comps) is the most widely used valuation method in investment banking and equity research. The logic is simple: if similar companies trade at a P/E of 25×, then your target company — if genuinely similar — should also trade at approximately 25×. The entire skill lies in (1) selecting truly comparable peers, (2) correctly spreading their financial data, and (3) calculating the right multiples. Garbage peers → garbage valuation.
Imagine you want to price a 2-bedroom flat in Pune's Koregaon Park. You'd look at what similar 2-bed flats in the same locality sold for recently. You wouldn't compare it to a 5-bedroom villa in Baner or a studio in Kothrud — that would be a bad comp. The same logic applies to stocks: compare a mid-cap IT services company to other mid-cap IT services companies — not to large-cap IT or to telecom. The closer the peers, the more reliable the valuation range.

02 — How Comps Analysis Works

The 5-Step Process Step by Step

Building a comps table is a systematic process. Each step must be done correctly — an error in peer selection invalidates everything that follows.

1
Peer Selection
Identify 5–8 Truly Comparable Companies
Choose companies in the same industry, of similar size (market cap within 0.3×–3× of target), similar business model (pure-play vs diversified), and operating in similar geographies. For an Indian mid-cap IT company: compare to other Indian mid-cap IT companies — not to TCS or Infosys (too large), not to US tech (different market), not to BFSI-focused IT (different segment).
Same sector ✅ Similar size ✅ Outliers distort median ❌
2
Spread Financials
Collect Revenue, EBITDA, PAT, Debt, Book Value
For each peer, collect: Revenue (Net Sales), EBITDA (Operating Profit), PAT (Net Profit), Total Debt, Cash & Equivalents, Book Value of Equity, and Share Count. Use the most recent full year (FY25) and next year estimate (FY26E) if available. On Screener.in, all historical data is on the P&L tab — consistent format across all companies.
Same fiscal year ✅ Consolidated P&L ✅ Standalone misleads ❌
3
Calculate Multiples
Compute P/E, EV/EBITDA, P/B, P/S for Every Peer
P/E = Market Cap ÷ PAT. EV/EBITDA = (Market Cap + Debt − Cash) ÷ EBITDA. P/B = Market Cap ÷ Book Value. P/S = Market Cap ÷ Revenue. These four multiples together capture profitability-based, cash-flow-based, asset-based, and revenue-based valuation angles. Use all four; never rely on just one.
P/E — earnings based EV/EBITDA — cash flow P/B — asset based P/S — revenue based
4
Find Median
Calculate Median (Not Mean) of Peer Multiples
Use the median — not the average — of all peer multiples. The median is less distorted by one extreme outlier. If 7 peers have P/E of 20, 22, 24, 25, 27, 30, 85 — the average is 33× (distorted by the outlier at 85×) but the median is 25× (robust). Always exclude clearly broken companies (negative EPS, distressed) from the peer set before calculating medians.
Median — outlier robust ✅ Mean — distorted by outliers ❌
5
Apply to Target
Derive Fair Value Range for Your Target Company
Implied Price (P/E method) = Median Peer P/E × Target's EPS. Implied Price (EV/EBITDA method) = (Median EV/EBITDA × Target EBITDA) − Net Debt ÷ Shares. Take the range from both methods. If target trades below this range → potentially undervalued. Above → potentially overvalued. This is not a guarantee — it's a range based on how the market values peers today.
Trading below range → investigate ✅ Wrong peers → wrong range ❌

📊 The entire Comps process: Target company → identify peers → spread their multiples → find median → apply to target → get fair value range. If market price is below fair value → potentially undervalued. Above → potentially overvalued.

03 — What Makes a Good Peer

Peer Selection: The Most Critical Step

A comps table is only as good as its peer selection. If you compare an FMCG company to a commodity chemicals company just because both have "consumer" in their description, your multiples will be meaningless.

🏭
Same Industry & Business Model
Companies must be in the same or closely adjacent industry. A pure-play IT services company should be compared to other IT services companies — not IT products, not hardware, not telecom. Sub-sector matters: pharma API vs branded pharma vs generic exports are three different businesses even within pharma.
✅ IT services vs IT services ❌ IT services vs IT products
📏
Similar Size (Market Cap)
Keep peers within 0.3×–3× of the target's market cap. TCS (₹12 lakh Cr) should not be in the same comps set as a ₹2,000 Cr IT company — they have completely different growth profiles, client quality, and pricing power. Small-cap companies typically trade at a discount to large-cap peers; mixing them distorts the median.
✅ Within 3× market cap range ❌ Large-cap vs micro-cap
📈
Similar Growth Rate
A company growing revenue at 30% annually should not be compared to peers growing at 5%. Higher growth typically commands a premium multiple. If growth rates are very different, the median multiple will misrepresent the target. Use P/E-to-Growth (PEG) as a supplementary check when growth rates vary significantly within your peer set.
✅ Similar 3yr revenue CAGR range ❌ 5% growth vs 40% growth peers
💰
Similar Profitability (Margins)
EBITDA margins matter because high-margin businesses deserve premium multiples. If your target has 20% EBITDA margin and peers have 35%, the peer multiples will overstate fair value. Sort peers by EBITDA margin — those within ±5 percentage points are most comparable. Exclude loss-making companies from the P/E comps (they make the median meaningless).
✅ EBITDA margin within ±5pp ❌ Include loss-making peers in P/E
🌍
Same Geography / Market
Indian-listed companies should be compared to other Indian-listed companies — not to US-listed ADRs or international stocks. Country risk premium, tax rates, regulatory environment, and market depth all affect multiples. Infosys India-listed vs Infosys NYSE-listed would have different multiples even for the same underlying business.
✅ NSE/BSE-listed Indian companies ❌ Indian vs US-listed comps
🏦
Similar Capital Structure
A debt-free company should ideally be compared to other low-debt peers. High leverage artificially suppresses P/E (interest cuts PAT) and inflates EV/EBITDA. When capital structures differ significantly, use EV/EBITDA (enterprise-value based) rather than P/E (equity-value based), because EV/EBITDA is capital-structure neutral.
✅ EV/EBITDA for debt-heavy sectors ❌ P/E comparison across debt levels

5 Sectors Where Comps Analysis Works Well

Comps work best in sectors with many listed, similar-sized peers, transparent financials, and comparable business models. These sectors are ideal for building reliable comps tables on Screener.in.

✅ Many listed peers ✅ Similar business models ✅ Transparent Screener data
✅ Indian mid-cap IT services is the best sector for comps in India. There are 15+ listed mid-cap IT services companies with transparent P&L data on Screener.in, similar business models (offshore software delivery to US/EU clients), comparable margin profiles (14–22% EBITDA), and similar revenue scales. P/E multiples tend to cluster in a 25–50× range with a clear median. Outliers (high-growth companies like Persistent at 50×+ P/E) can be separately identified. Comps gives a tight, reliable fair value range for any mid-cap IT company you're analyzing.
🏦
Private Sector Banks (Large-Cap)
HDFC Bank · ICICI Bank · Axis Bank · Kotak Mahindra · IndusInd Bank — P/B method preferred
✅ P/B is the correct multiple ✅ All Screener-listed, consistent data 📌 ROE matters for P/B premium
✅ For banks, P/B (Price to Book Value) is the standard comps multiple — not P/E. Banks create value by deploying capital (book value) at returns above their cost of equity. A bank with 18% ROE deserves a higher P/B than one with 12% ROE. The comps methodology: collect P/B for all major private banks, plot against their ROE, and see where your target bank falls on the ROE-P/B regression line. HDFC Bank at 3.5× P/B with 17% ROE vs IndusInd Bank at 1.2× P/B with 11% ROE — this differential makes intuitive sense and comes directly from the comps analysis.
💊

✅ Similar US FDA exposure ✅ Revenue and margin comparability ⚠️ Separate branded vs generic
✅ Pharma comps work well when you carefully separate sub-segments. Generic exporters (Cipla, Aurobindo, Dr Reddy's) are directly comparable — they all sell off-patent molecules to US, EU, and emerging market pharmacies. Their P/E multiples cluster in a 20–30× range. The critical segmentation: don't mix branded Indian domestic pharma (Alkem domestic) with generic exporters (Alkem international) — their growth drivers and margin profiles are completely different. Asian Paints, Berger, and Kansai all sell to the same customer (Indian household renovating their home), through similar distribution (dealer networks), with similar products. 06 — Bad Comps Pitfalls

5 Ways Comps Analysis Goes Wrong

Comps analysis fails when peers are poorly selected, when the wrong multiples are used, or when the analyst ignores structural differences between peers and target. These are the most common and dangerous errors.

⚠️
Using TCS as a Peer for a ₹3,000 Cr IT Company
Size mismatch error — different tier, different growth profile, different client quality
🚨 100× size difference 🚨 TCS's premium inflates median 🚨 Overvalues small company
🚨 The most common beginner mistake: including TCS or Infosys in a comps set for a small IT company. TCS trades at a premium P/E of 28–32× because it has ₹2.5 lakh Cr revenue, 400+ Fortune 500 clients, decades of relationships, and an unmatched talent pool. A ₹3,000 Cr IT company with 20 clients and ₹300 Cr PAT should not be valued using TCS's multiples — it will systematically overvalue the small company. The small company should trade at a meaningful discount to TCS (smaller client base, execution risk, key-man dependency, liquidity discount). Always keep peers within 0.3×–3× market cap range. If you can't find 5 comparable-sized peers, widen the sub-sector — don't add a company 50× larger.
❌
Including Loss-Making Companies in P/E Comps
Negative EPS makes P/E undefined — distorts the median and misleads
🚨 Negative P/E is meaningless 🚨 Inflates mean dramatically 🚨 Use EV/Sales instead for loss-makers
🚨 Including Vodafone Idea, MTNL, or any loss-making peer in a P/E comps table is a fundamental error. If a company has negative EPS, its P/E ratio is negative — which is mathematically meaningless in a valuation context. Including it in the peer set either (a) creates an undefined median, or (b) if you use absolute values, dramatically distorts the analysis. The correct approach: for loss-making peers, exclude them from the P/E section and use EV/Revenue (Price/Sales) instead — it's defined even when companies are unprofitable. Alternatively, use forward-year estimates for companies expected to return to profitability. Never include currently loss-making peers in a trailing P/E comps table.
🔀
Mixing Conglomerates with Pure-Play Companies
Reliance Industries vs Jio Platforms — comparing diversified group vs pure telecom
🚨 Conglomerates trade at discount 🚨 Different risk profiles 🚨 Use SOTP for conglomerates instead
🚨 Comparing a diversified conglomerate to a pure-play company produces systematically wrong multiples. Reliance Industries (retail + telecom + petrochemicals + O2C) cannot be compared to a pure-play telecom like Bharti Airtel or a pure-play retailer like DMart. Conglomerates typically trade at a "conglomerate discount" — a lower multiple than the sum of their parts — because investors cannot efficiently price diversified businesses. The correct approach for conglomerates: Sum-of-the-Parts (SOTP) valuation, where each business segment gets its own comps table with appropriate peers, and the values are added up. Using a single multiple from a mixed peer set will either significantly overvalue or undervalue the conglomerate.
📉
Using Historical P/E During Commodity Cycles
Steel / metals sector — cyclical earnings make P/E comps unreliable at cycle peaks
🚨 Cycle-peak P/E appears cheap 🚨 Earnings will mean-revert 🚨 Use EV/EBITDA through-cycle instead
🚨 Comps using trailing P/E for cyclical companies (steel, metals, cement, chemicals) at cycle peaks is deeply misleading. When steel prices are at all-time highs (like FY22), Tata Steel and JSW Steel report peak earnings. Using this peak P/E to value the sector gives a false impression of cheapness — the stocks appear at 5× P/E! But 5× P/E during a commodity super-cycle doesn't mean the stock is cheap; it means the "E" is temporarily very high and will fall sharply when the cycle turns. The correct approach for cyclicals: use EV/EBITDA on a mid-cycle (through-cycle average) EBITDA estimate — typically the average of 5–7 years, smoothing out the peaks and troughs. Alternatively, use P/BV (which doesn't depend on cyclical earnings).
🌐
Comparing Indian-Listed Stocks to US ADRs
Infosys India vs Infosys NYSE — same business, different valuation for structural reasons
🚨 Different country risk premium 🚨 Different investor base 🚨 Inflates Indian valuation estimates
🚨 Cross-border comps systematically distort valuations because different markets price risk, liquidity, and growth differently. US tech companies (similar business model to Indian IT) trade at much higher multiples — partly because of deeper liquidity, lower country risk premium, and higher institutional quality. If you build comps for an Indian IT company using US tech multiples (30–40× EV/Revenue for SaaS, 50–80× P/E for high-growth tech), you'll overvalue the Indian company by 2–4×. Always stick to NSE/BSE-listed peers. The one exception: if an Indian company has a genuine global peer (like HDFC Life being compared to global life insurance companies), use a country discount factor — typically 20–30% — to adjust the global peer multiples downward for India-specific risks.

⚡ 4 Advanced Comps Tricks — Each Saves You From Common Errors
A
The "PEG-Adjusted Comps" Method
When peers have very different growth rates, raw P/E comps are misleading. Use PEG (P/E ÷ EPS CAGR). If Peer A trades at 40× P/E with 30% EPS CAGR (PEG 1.33) and Peer B trades at 25× P/E with 10% EPS CAGR (PEG 2.5) — Peer A is actually cheaper on a growth-adjusted basis. Find the median PEG across peers, then multiply by the target's EPS CAGR to get a growth-fair P/E. This adjusts for the most common peer set bias.
B
The "Football Field" Chart
Present your comps as a "football field" — a horizontal bar chart with the valuation range from each method shown as a bar. P/E method: ₹900–₹1,200. EV/EBITDA: ₹850–₹1,150. P/B: ₹800–₹1,050. The overlap of all bars is your "conviction zone" — the price range where all methods agree. This is the most defensible target price range. In Excel or Google Sheets, this is a simple Gantt chart with min/max values.
C
Forward vs Trailing Multiples
Trailing P/E uses last year's actual PAT. Forward P/E uses next year's estimated PAT. Professional analysts use forward multiples because markets price future earnings. If a company is expected to grow 40% next year, the forward P/E is much lower than trailing. On Screener.in, use the current year financials as "trailing" and estimate forward by applying the 3-year average growth rate. Always state clearly whether your comps are trailing or forward — they are NOT interchangeable.

Comps & Multiples Glossary

Every term used in comparable company analysis — plain language, precise definitions, and when to use each.

Term What It Means in Simple Words When to Use
Trading CompsValuation method using multiples from publicly traded comparable companies. The most common form of relative valuation. Works best for companies with many listed peers in the same sector. Faster and more market-grounded than DCF, but only as reliable as the peer selection and current market conditions (avoids "garbage in, garbage out").Primary method
Enterprise Value (EV)EV = Market Cap + Total Debt − Cash. Represents the total value of the business to all capital providers (equity + debt holders). Capital-structure neutral — allows comparison between companies with different debt levels. Used in EV/EBITDA and EV/Sales multiples. On Screener.in, EV is shown directly on company pages.EV/EBITDA
P/E RatioPrice-to-Earnings = Market Cap ÷ PAT. The most widely used valuation multiple. Represents how many times earnings investors are paying. Use trailing P/E for stable, mature businesses; forward P/E for growth companies. Only valid for profitable companies — undefined for loss-makers. Distorted by one-time exceptional items.Profitable cos
EV/EBITDAEnterprise Value divided by EBITDA (Operating Profit). Capital-structure neutral — not affected by debt levels. Preferred for capital-intensive sectors (cement, steel, telecom) and M&A valuation. More stable than P/E because EBITDA excludes interest, tax, and depreciation. Range varies dramatically by sector: FMCG 35–55×, IT 15–25×, Steel 6–10×.Capital intensive
P/B RatioPrice-to-Book = Market Cap ÷ Book Value of Equity. Asset-based multiple. Preferred for banks and financial companies where book value (loan book, deposits) is the core of the business. A bank with 18% ROE deserves a higher P/B than one with 10% ROE. The P/B-ROE regression is the standard peer analysis framework for banks in India.Banks & NBFC
P/S RatioPrice-to-Sales = Market Cap ÷ Revenue. Revenue-based multiple — only valid comparison when peers have similar margin profiles. Use when companies are loss-making (EPS undefined) or when revenue is the most stable metric. SaaS and early-stage tech companies often valued on P/S multiples. Lower P/S with same growth = more attractive.Loss-making
Median MultipleThe middle value when all peer multiples are sorted from lowest to highest. Preferred over the mean because it is not distorted by one extreme outlier. If you have 7 peers and one has a P/E of 200× (due to a one-time low earning year), the median ignores this outlier while the mean would be dramatically inflated. Always use median in comps.Always use this
Conglomerate DiscountThe tendency for diversified companies (conglomerates) to trade at a lower multiple than the sum of their individual business segments valued separately. Investors apply this discount because conglomerates are harder to analyse, capital allocation decisions are opaque, and investors can't easily exit one subsidiary without the whole. Use SOTP valuation for conglomerates — not comps.Avoid in comps
SOTP ValuationSum-of-the-Parts = separate valuation of each business segment using appropriate comps for that segment, then adding them together. Used for conglomerates (Reliance: Jio + Retail + O2C + Upstream), holding companies (Tata Sons), or companies with clearly distinct business divisions. More complex than comps but more accurate for diversified businesses.Conglomerates
Football Field ChartVisual representation of valuation showing a horizontal bar for each valuation method (comps P/E, comps EV/EBITDA, DCF, P/B) with the range from low to high case. The overlap zone where all methods agree is the "conviction zone." Standard output format in investment banking pitch books and equity research — shows the range and convergence of multiple methods.Presentation
