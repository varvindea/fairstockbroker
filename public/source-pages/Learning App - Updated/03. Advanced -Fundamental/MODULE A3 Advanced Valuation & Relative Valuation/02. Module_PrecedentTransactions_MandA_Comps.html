01 — Key Learning Outcome

Find & Apply Acquisition Multiples for Control Premium

🤝
Your Core Takeaway
When Tata Group acquired Air India, or when Adani acquired NDTV, or when Hindustan Unilever acquired GSK Consumer Healthcare — each buyer paid a premium above the target's prevailing stock price. This premium is called the "control premium" — it compensates existing shareholders for giving up their stake and reflects the strategic value the acquirer expects to generate. Precedent Transaction Analysis uses historical deal data to estimate what a company is worth in an M&A context — always higher than its standalone trading value.
Imagine you own 1% of a restaurant. On the open market, someone might buy that 1% stake for ₹10,000 — just for the financial return. But someone who wants to buy 100% of the restaurant and run it themselves would pay much more — maybe ₹12,000–₹15,000 per 1% — because they can change the menu, cut costs, merge with their other restaurant, and capture synergies. That extra ₹2,000–₹5,000 per share is the control premium. Precedent Transaction Analysis tells you what comparable acquisitions have paid as this premium historically.

02 — Precedent Transactions vs Trading Comps

Why M&A Multiples Are Higher Than Trading Multiples

Trading comps show what minority shareholders pay for a small stake. Precedent transactions show what a controlling buyer pays for the whole company. The difference is the control premium — and it is always significant.

1

Trading Comps vs Precedent Transactions — The Key Difference

Trading comps: what a minority investor pays for a small stake in a listed company. Precedent transactions: what a strategic acquirer pays for 100% control. The difference is always positive — precedent transaction multiples are invariably higher than trading comps because: (a) control rights have value (change management, strategy, cost structure), (b) synergies — cost savings and revenue opportunities the acquirer can capture, (c) scarcity premium — this specific company in this geography is not easy to replace. Precedent transactions provide the ceiling of fair value; trading comps provide the floor.

2

What Drives the Size of the Control Premium?

Competitive bidding (multiple acquirers): highest premium — 40–65%. Strategic fit (unique capabilities, geography, customer base): higher premium — 30–45%. Financial buyer (PE fund buying for financial return): lower premium — 20–35%. Distressed target: may have negative premium or zero — seller has no choice. Market conditions matter too: in a bull market with easy credit, premiums rise as acquirers can afford to pay more. In a credit crisis, even strategic buyers become disciplined — premiums compress to 15–25%.

3

Finding Deal Data — Where to Look for Indian M&A Comps

India M&A data sources: (1) Screener.in — for target company financials at the time of acquisition. (2) SEBI open offer filings — any acquisition of >25% stake requires a public open offer; the offer price and deal rationale are public. (3) Stock exchange announcements — listed companies must disclose material acquisitions. (4) Company annual reports — typically describe significant acquisitions. (5) Financial newspapers (ET, Mint) — report deal values and premiums. For each past deal: note deal value, target EBITDA, target PAT, and implied multiples.

4

How to Apply Precedent Multiples to Your Target Company

Step 1: Find 5–8 comparable deals in the same sector (last 3–5 years). Step 2: Calculate the deal EV/EBITDA = Deal Value ÷ Target EBITDA at time of deal. Also calculate premium paid = (Offer Price − Unaffected Price) ÷ Unaffected Price. Step 3: Find median deal EV/EBITDA and median premium. Step 4: Apply: Implied EV = Median Deal EV/EBITDA × Your Target's LTM EBITDA. Step 5: Implied Equity Value = EV − Net Debt ÷ Shares = per-share M&A value. This per-share value will always be higher than trading comps because of the control premium embedded in historical deal multiples.

04 — Well-Executed Indian Acquisitions

5 Deals Where Transaction Analysis Worked Perfectly

These acquisitions were well-priced relative to precedent multiples, strategically justified, and created genuine value. They are ideal precedent transactions for future deals in their sectors.

🧴
HUL Acquisition of GSK Consumer Healthcare (Horlicks)
₹3,045 Cr · FY2020 · EV/EBITDA ~30–38× · Control Premium ~28% · Strategic fit
✅ Strong synergies realised ✅ Market leadership secured 📌 Premium justified by brand value
✅ The HUL-GSK deal is the textbook example of a well-justified precedent transaction in Indian FMCG. HUL acquired Horlicks, Boost, and other GSK consumer brands at ~₹3,045 Cr. The deal made perfect strategic sense: HUL gained leadership in the health food drinks (HFD) segment — a ₹7,000 Cr market it had virtually no presence in. The EV/EBITDA multiple of 30–38× was at the upper end of historical FMCG transactions, justified by: (a) irreplaceable brand equity (Horlicks is 140+ years old), (b) distribution synergies across HUL's existing trade network, (c) cross-sell opportunities into HUL's 8M outlet reach. Post-acquisition, HUL successfully integrated the business and maintained market share. This deal now serves as a reliable precedent for any future FMCG brand acquisition in India.

Tata Consumer Products — Capital Foods & Ching's Secret
₹5,100 Cr combined · FY2023 · EV/EBITDA ~29–30× · Premium ~32–35%
✅ Fills portfolio gap ✅ Accelerates premiumisation ✅ Distribution leverage immediate
✅ Tata Consumer's acquisition of Capital Foods (Ching's Secret, Smith & Jones) for ₹5,100 Cr at ~30× EV/EBITDA is a perfectly structured precedent transaction. Ching's Secret commands the "desi Chinese" instant noodles/sauces category — a fast-growing ₹2,000 Cr market. Tata Consumer's distribution network (7.5M outlets) immediately gave the acquired brands exponential reach vs. the 1.5M outlets they had independently. The premium paid (~32%) was calibrated against comparable Indian food brand acquisitions. Post-deal tracking shows both brands grew faster under Tata Consumer's distribution than their standalone trajectory. This is exactly the type of deal that becomes a reliable precedent multiple anchor for the packaged foods category.
🏥
Sun Pharma Acquisition of Ranbaxy (select business units)
Strategic asset pick-up · Domestic formulations · Synergy-driven · Below distressed book value
✅ Bought at significant discount ✅ Integration fully successful 📌 Seller distress created opportunity
✅ Sun Pharma's acquisition of select Ranbaxy businesses demonstrated how distressed precedent transactions create value for the acquirer while setting lower multiple benchmarks. When Ranbaxy faced severe USFDA issues (import alert, multiple plant bans), it became a forced seller. Sun Pharma, as India's largest generic pharma company, acquired the domestic branded formulations and selected manufacturing assets at well below what they would have traded at in a normal M&A context. The deal EV/EBITDA was in the 8–12× range — dramatically below the 20–25× that healthy Indian pharma companies commanded. This transaction anchors the lower end of the pharma precedent range and illustrates how deal context (distressed vs strategic) dramatically affects the applicable multiple.
🏦
HDFC Bank Merger with HDFC Ltd.
India's largest ever financial sector merger · Swap ratio 42 HDFC shares per 25 HDFC Ltd. shares
✅ No cash premium — swap merger ✅ Strategic logic overwhelming ✅ Regulatory complexity navigated
✅ The HDFC Bank-HDFC merger is a landmark precedent for financial sector consolidation in India, showing how swap mergers embed implicit control premiums. Rather than a cash acquisition, HDFC Ltd. shareholders received HDFC Bank shares at a carefully negotiated ratio (42 HDFC Bank shares per 25 HDFC Ltd. shares). The swap ratio implied a modest premium for HDFC Ltd. shareholders. The strategic logic: HDFC Bank gains access to HDFC Ltd.'s mortgage book (₹5+ lakh Cr), while HDFC Ltd. shareholders gain the banking license, deposits franchise, and superior liquidity of a listed bank. This merger precedent is critical for any future financial sector consolidation analysis — it shows that large-scale mergers use share swaps, not cash premiums, and the "premium" is embedded in the exchange ratio.

✅ Content + distribution integrated ⚠️ Premium to minority shareholders 📌 Open offer mandated by SEBI

Reliance Industries strengthened its position in the media ecosystem — combining Jio's 450M user base with TV18's news/entertainment network. The deal required a mandatory SEBI open offer, giving minority shareholders an exit at a premium. Media deal EV/EBITDA benchmarks were in the 15–25× range. This deal established reliable precedents for Indian media/OTT consolidation — any future media acquisition can benchmark against the premiums paid and multiples transacted here. Especially relevant as India's OTT and digital media consolidation continues.
05 — M&A Valuation Errors

5 Ways Precedent Transaction Analysis Goes Wrong

Precedent transaction analysis fails when deals are stale, when synergies were overestimated (leading to seller using inflated premiums), or when deal context is ignored.

⏰
Using 2007 Bull Market Deals as 2024 Precedents
Stale transactions from peak cycle distort current fair value estimates dramatically
🚨 5–10× above current fair multiples 🚨 Pre-GFC credit availability ≠ today 🚨 Different regulatory environment
🚨 The "stale comparables" problem: deals done at the peak of the 2006–2008 Indian growth cycle are dangerous precedents for current transactions. During the 2006–08 cycle, Indian infrastructure and real estate companies were acquired at EV/EBITDA multiples of 40–60× — driven by unlimited cheap credit, unrealistic growth projections, and multiple buyers competing. Using those multiples today would suggest Indian infrastructure companies are worth 2–3× their actual acquirable value. The rule: use only deals from the last 3–5 years (maximum 5 years). More than 5 years old = significantly different credit conditions, valuations, and regulatory environment. If forced to use older deals, apply a "vintage adjustment factor" — reduce the multiple by 20–30% to account for current market conditions.
🌏
Using US or European Deal Multiples for Indian Companies
Cross-border precedent error — different risk premia, growth rates, regulatory frameworks
🚨 US deals at 2–3× Indian multiples 🚨 Different WACC and risk premium 🚨 Overvalues Indian target by 50%+
🚨 Using US M&A precedents for Indian targets systematically overvalues the Indian company by 50–150%. US pharma acquisitions routinely happen at 20–35× EBITDA; Indian pharma deals at 12–22×. US consumer goods deals at 15–20× EBITDA; Indian FMCG deals at 20–35× (India FMCG commands higher multiples due to growth premium). The mismatches are sector-specific and directionally unpredictable — you cannot simply apply a country discount. The correct approach: always use India-specific deal data, supplemented by regional (Asian) deal data if insufficient India data exists. Never use US/EU deals directly for Indian targets. If an Indian company has significant offshore revenues (IT services), a partial weighting of US deal multiples may be appropriate — but only for the offshore revenue component.
😤
Including "Winner's Curse" Deals in the Precedent Set
Competitive bidding wars produce outlier multiples that don't reflect typical acquisition economics
🚨 Overpaying skews the median 🚨 Many acquirers destroyed value 🚨 Include but flag as outlier
🚨 The "winner's curse" in M&A: the company that wins a competitive auction frequently overpays, and its deal multiple then distorts the precedent database. When Ola bid for TaxiForSure (FY2015) or when early-stage digital companies were acquired at astronomical revenue multiples during the COVID-era tech bubble, those deals were done at 50–100× revenue — multiples that no rational acquirer would pay today. Including them in a precedent analysis inflates the median and suggests wildly optimistic valuations. The correct approach: flag deals with anomalously high multiples (more than 1.5× the inter-quartile range above Q3), include them in your presentation, but calculate the median both with and without outliers. Present both — and explain why the outlier was paid.
📉
Ignoring Target Financial Quality — Same Multiple, Different Risk
Applying a premium multiple to a low-quality business just because the sector traded at that multiple
🚨 Quality discount ignored 🚨 EBITDA margin differences matter 🚨 Growth rate drives multiple, not just sector
🚨 Applying a sector median M&A multiple to a fundamentally weaker company within that sector is a common and dangerous error. If FMCG deals in India typically happen at 25–35× EV/EBITDA, does that mean every FMCG company deserves that multiple? No. A company with 8% EBITDA margin (vs sector average 18%), declining revenue, and regional-only distribution should be valued at a significant discount to the sector median — perhaps 12–16× EV/EBITDA. The precedent transaction multiple is a starting point, not a universal answer. Adjust downward for: below-average margins, declining revenue growth, concentrated customer base, thin management bench, and regulatory vulnerabilities. Adjust upward for: premium brand equity, strong distribution moats, high recurring revenue, and significant synergy potential for a specific acquirer.
🔢
Using Last-Twelve-Months EBITDA at Cyclical Peak
Commodities/Steel/Cement — applying M&A multiples to peak-cycle earnings inflates deal value
🚨 EBITDA at cycle peak 🚨 5× LTM EBITDA = 15× normalised 🚨 Use mid-cycle EBITDA instead
🚨 For cyclical industries, applying a deal multiple to last-twelve-months (LTM) EBITDA at the peak of the cycle creates spectacular valuation errors. Example: A steel company with LTM EBITDA of ₹5,000 Cr at steel super-cycle peak. Historical steel deals at 5× EV/EBITDA suggests deal value of ₹25,000 Cr. But mid-cycle EBITDA for the same company is ₹1,500 Cr. At 5× mid-cycle EBITDA: deal value = ₹7,500 Cr — a 70% lower number! Any acquirer who paid ₹25,000 Cr based on peak EBITDA would have seriously overpaid. The lesson: for cyclical companies (steel, cement, chemicals, commodities), always use mid-cycle (5–7 year average) EBITDA in the denominator when calculating historical deal multiples. If you use LTM, explicitly note that EBITDA is at cycle peak and discount accordingly.

For each deal, open the target company on Screener.in. Find the EBITDA (Operating Profit) and PAT for the fiscal year in which the deal closed. 3
Spreadsheet → Build transaction table → Find median EV/EBITDA and median premium
In your spreadsheet, create columns: Target | Acquirer | Year | Deal Value | Target EBITDA | Deal EV/EBITDA | Control Premium % | Deal Type. Fill in for all 5–8 deals. Then: use MEDIAN() function on the EV/EBITDA column (excluding outliers) and separately on the Control Premium column. These two medians are your anchor numbers for the valuation.
💡 Quick hack — no SEBI filing needed for major deals
For well-publicised Indian deals (HUL-Horlicks, Tata-Capital Foods, etc.), the EV/EBITDA multiple is widely reported in financial press at deal announcement. Search "deal name EV/EBITDA" on Google — ET Markets, Mint, and ICICI Securities equity research notes routinely publish these multiples. Use these as proxies when you can't access the full regulatory filing.
4
Apply to target → Implied M&A price (EV/EBITDA method)
Apply the median deal EV/EBITDA to your target company's current EBITDA: Implied EV = Median Deal EV/EBITDA × Target's LTM EBITDA. Then: Implied Equity Value = Implied EV − Net Debt. Divide by shares for per-share M&A value. Separately: apply the median control premium to the target's current trading price: M&A Price = Current Price × (1 + Median Premium). If both methods give similar answers, your analysis is robust.
✅ Both methods agree within ±15% = robust M&A valuation. Proceed with the midpoint as the fair M&A price estimate for the target.
🚨 Methods differ by >30% = check your deal multiples and premium calculation. One set of comps may have outliers distorting the median.
5
Sanity check → Compare M&A value vs trading comps value
The final check: your precedent transaction M&A value should ALWAYS be higher than your trading comps value (from Module 08). If not, something is wrong — either your deal multiples are too conservative or your trading comps are overstated. Typical spread: trading comps value = 100, M&A precedent value = 125–150 (reflecting the 25–50% control premium). Present both in a football field chart. The range between trading comps floor and M&A ceiling is the full valuation range for the company.
✅ M&A value = 125–150% of trading comps value: consistent with historical control premiums. Your valuation framework is internally consistent.
🚨 M&A value below trading comps: error somewhere. Recheck deal multiples — you may have included distressed deals that drag the median down.
⚡ 4 Advanced M&A Comps Tricks
A
The "Unaffected Price" Rule
Always use the unaffected price — the stock price 4–6 weeks BEFORE the deal announcement leaked or was made. After announcement, the stock immediately jumps toward the offer price, making it useless as a base. For control premium calculation: Premium = (Offer Price − Unaffected Price) ÷ Unaffected Price. Using the day-before-announcement price often underestimates the true premium because rumours had already driven the price up. The 4-week-prior price is the clean baseline.
B
Synergy-Adjusted Multiple
Professional acquirers pay a premium today based on synergies they expect to capture later. If an acquirer expects ₹100 Cr annual synergies at 20× capitalisation = ₹2,000 Cr synergy value. They can pay ₹1,000 Cr more than standalone value and still create ₹1,000 Cr of value. So if a deal looks expensive at 35× EV/EBITDA (vs sector median of 25×), check if the acquirer publicly stated synergy targets — the "expensive" multiple may be synergy-justified. Always cross-check premium against disclosed synergy guidance.
C
IBC Deal Exclusion Rule
Never include deals done through India's Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) process in your precedent transaction set for healthy company valuation. IBC deals involve no control premium — the "seller" is a bankruptcy court and lenders who have already lost most of their investment. IBC deal multiples of 4–8× EV/EBITDA are acquisition prices for distressed assets, not going-concern businesses. Example: Bhushan Steel acquisition by Tata Steel through IBC (₹35,200 Cr) is NOT a valid precedent for valuing healthy steel companies.
D
The "Football Field" for M&A Context
Always present your M&A valuation alongside trading comps in a "football field" chart: (1) DCF value, (2) Trading comps range, (3) 52-week trading range, (4) Precedent transaction range. The precedent transaction bar will always be the highest — it's the acquisition value. Present the stock's current price on the chart too. If it sits below the trading comps floor, the stock may be undervalued. If it sits within the precedent transaction range, the market is pricing in M&A possibility.

Revenue synergies: cross-selling products through the combined distribution network, new geographies, premium pricing. Synergies justify paying above the standalone value — the acquirer pays a premium today to capture synergies in future years.Justifies premium
Open Offer (SEBI)Under SEBI's Takeover Code, any acquirer who purchases 25%+ of a listed company's shares must make a public offer to buy an additional 26% from existing public shareholders at the offer price. The offer letter (LOFA) filed with SEBI contains deal terms, offer price, and rationale — making it the most reliable publicly available source of Indian M&A deal data.SEBI data source
IBC DealAcquisition through India's Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code process. No control premium applies — the seller is a bankruptcy court, not a willing seller. IBC deal multiples (4–8× EV/EBITDA) reflect deeply distressed, going-concern-risk businesses. Must be excluded from any precedent transaction set used to value a healthy business. Including IBC deals systematically understates the fair M&A multiple.Exclude always
Merger Swap RatioIn a share-swap merger (rather than cash acquisition), the exchange ratio at which target shareholders receive acquirer shares. The implicit premium is embedded in the swap ratio relative to the market prices of both companies. Example: if Target trades at ₹100 and Acquirer at ₹200, a swap ratio of 0.6 gives Target shareholders ₹120 value — a 20% premium. HDFC Bank-HDFC Ltd merger used this structure.Implicit premium
Football Field ChartVisual valuation summary showing multiple methodologies as horizontal bars: DCF, Trading Comps (floor), 52-week range, and Precedent Transactions (ceiling). The precedent transaction bar is always the highest, reflecting the control premium above standalone value. The overlap zone of multiple methods is the "conviction zone" — where multiple approaches agree on value. Standard presentation format in investment banking.Presentation
LBO MultipleThe EV/EBITDA a Private Equity buyer can pay given their target returns and available leverage. Typically lower than strategic buyer multiples (15–25% lower) because PE buyers have no synergies — they rely purely on operational improvement and re-selling at a higher multiple later. Understanding the LBO floor (what PE can afford to pay) sets the minimum M&A multiple a seller should accept.PE floor value
