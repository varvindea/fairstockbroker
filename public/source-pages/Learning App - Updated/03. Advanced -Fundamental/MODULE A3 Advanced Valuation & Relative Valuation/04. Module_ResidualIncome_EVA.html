01 — Key Learning Outcome

Apply EVA Framework: Identify Value Creators vs Destroyers

⚡
Your Core Takeaway
Every business uses two types of capital: equity from shareholders and debt from lenders. Both have a cost. Shareholders expect returns (cost of equity). Lenders charge interest (cost of debt). Together, this is the Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) — the minimum return a company must earn to not destroy value. EVA measures whether the business earned more than this minimum. Positive EVA = value created. Negative EVA = value destroyed. A company can report accounting profit and still have negative EVA.
Imagine you borrow ₹10 lakh at 8% annual interest to start a business, and use ₹5 lakh of your own savings (expecting at least 15% return). Your total cost of capital is WACC = about 10.3%. Now your business earns ₹1.4 lakh profit on ₹15 lakh capital = ROIC of 9.3%. You're profitable — but you're still destroying value because 9.3% ROIC is below 10.3% WACC. Your EVA = (9.3% − 10.3%) × ₹15 lakh = −₹15,000. You earned profit but destroyed economic value. This is EVA thinking.

02 — The Three EVA States

Value Creator · Neutral · Value Destroyer

Every company falls into one of three states: it creates value (ROIC > WACC), it breaks even (ROIC = WACC), or it destroys value (ROIC < WACC). Only companies in the first category are truly compounding wealth for shareholders.

🏆
Value Creator
ROIC > WACC
EVA = NOPAT − (WACC × Capital Employed) > 0
Example: Asian Paints — ROIC ~35%, WACC ~10% → Spread +25%
Every rupee of capital deployed earns more than it costs. Shareholders are genuinely getting richer. These companies deserve premium P/E multiples because they compound value over time. The higher the spread and the larger the capital base, the more EVA they generate. Asian Paints, Nestlé, HDFC Bank, and TCS consistently sit in this category — justifying their premium valuations.
⚖️
Break-Even
ROIC ≈ WACC
EVA = NOPAT − (WACC × Capital Employed) ≈ 0
Example: Capital-intensive midcap — ROIC ~12%, WACC ~11% → Spread +1%
The business earns just enough to cover its cost of capital — no more, no less. Shareholders receive their required return but no surplus value. These companies should theoretically trade at book value (P/B ≈ 1×). When ROIC exactly equals WACC, the company earns accounting profit but zero economic profit. Growth at break-even ROIC adds zero value — every rupee invested returns exactly what it cost.
🔥
Value Destroyer
ROIC < WACC
EVA = NOPAT − (WACC × Capital Employed) < 0
Example: Telecom pre-Jio — ROIC ~6%, WACC ~13% → Spread −7%
The business is profitable on paper but destroys economic value. Management might report "₹500 Cr profit" while the EVA is −₹800 Cr — meaning the business consumed ₹800 Cr more than it generated on a risk-adjusted basis. These companies should trade below book value (P/B < 1×). Capital-intensive industries with low margins often fall here. Growth here makes things WORSE — more capital deployed at below-WACC returns destroys more EVA.

⚡ The same ₹15,000 Cr capital base can produce +₹555 Cr EVA (if ROIC=16%) or −₹645 Cr EVA (if ROIC=8%). Profitable in both cases. Value-creating in only one. The WACC is the critical hurdle rate.

1

NOPAT: Net Operating Profit After Tax — Not PAT

NOPAT is not the same as PAT (Net Profit). NOPAT adds back interest expense (after-tax) because EVA values the operating business independently of how it's financed. NOPAT = EBIT × (1 − Tax Rate). Example: EBIT ₹1,000 Cr, Tax Rate 25% → NOPAT = ₹750 Cr. Using PAT would understate operating profitability for debt-heavy companies. NOPAT is the true operating return on capital before capital structure decisions.

2

Capital Employed: What the Business Actually Uses

Capital Employed (CE) = Total Assets − Current Liabilities (non-interest-bearing). Or: Fixed Assets + Working Capital. This is the total capital at work in the business. ROIC = NOPAT ÷ Capital Employed. The Capital Charge = WACC × CE is the minimum this capital must earn. Companies that bloat their working capital (excess inventory, long debtor days) inflate CE and reduce ROIC — even without changing NOPAT. Screener.in shows "Capital Employed" in the financial ratios section.

3

WACC: The Hurdle Rate Every Rupee Must Clear

WACC = (Cost of Equity × Equity Weight) + (After-tax Cost of Debt × Debt Weight). Cost of Equity = Risk-Free Rate + Beta × Equity Risk Premium (CAPM). For India: Risk-Free Rate ~7% (10-yr G-Sec), Equity Risk Premium ~5–6%, typical Beta 0.8–1.2 → Cost of Equity = 11–14%. After-tax Cost of Debt = Interest Rate × (1 − Tax Rate). A typical Indian mid-cap WACC ranges from 10–13%. High-risk companies have higher WACC — making value creation harder.

4

Why EVA Matters for Stock Valuation

Stocks trading above book value (P/B > 1×) require positive EVA to justify that premium. The relationship: Market Value = Book Value + PV of all future EVAs. A company with sustainably positive EVA should trade at a premium to book value — investors pay for the value yet to be created. A company with persistently negative EVA should trade below book value. This framework explains why Asian Paints trades at 12× P/B while Tata Motors historically traded at 0.8× P/B — EVA spread explains most of the P/B premium across industries.

5 Indian Companies with Exceptional EVA Track Records

These companies consistently earn ROIC far above their cost of capital — generating massive EVA year after year. Their premium valuations are justified by sustained positive EVA, not just earnings growth.

🖥️
TCS — The Ultimate EVA Machine
ROIC ~130%+ · WACC ~11% · Spread ~119% · Capital Employed: People + Software · No physical factories
✅ ROIC 10× above WACC ✅ Asset-light = low capital base ✅ EVA > ₹45,000 Cr annually
✅ TCS is the single largest EVA generator in India — by a massive margin. The reason: TCS employs ~600,000 people who create intellectual value, but requires almost zero physical capital (no blast furnaces, no drilling rigs, no factories). Its Capital Employed is primarily working capital and some office equipment. NOPAT of ~₹49,000 Cr on a capital base of ~₹38,000 Cr means ROIC exceeds 100%. Even with a 11% WACC, the EVA spread is 119%+. The capital charge is ~₹4,180 Cr while NOPAT is ₹49,000 Cr — a 12× ratio. This is why TCS deserves a 28–32× P/E — not just because it earns well, but because it generates astronomical EVA on a small capital base. The EVA framework perfectly explains TCS's premium.
🎨
Asian Paints — Consumer Franchise = High EVA
ROIC ~40–45% · WACC ~10% · Spread ~30–35% · Moat from brand + distribution
✅ 30-year consistent ROIC > 25% ✅ P/B premium = market pricing future EVA ⚠️ FY25 EVA narrowed on competition
✅ Asian Paints has compounded EVA for 30 years — which is precisely why it trades at 10–15× P/B while most companies trade at 2–4× P/B. ROIC of 40–45% on consistently growing capital employed means EVA has grown year after year. The formula: higher ROIC + growing capital base (reinvesting in distribution, factories, retail touchpoints) = compounding EVA. P/B premium = Book Value + PV(future EVAs). Asian Paints' book value per share is ~₹220, but the stock has traded at ₹2,500–₹3,000 — the ₹2,300–₹2,800 premium above book is the market pricing in all future EVAs from the moat (12,000+ dealer network, colour matching technology, brand). The FY25 EVA compression (due to Birla Opus competition) is exactly why the stock derated — the market is pricing in lower future EVA.
🍫
Nestlé India — FMCG Capital Efficiency Champion
ROIC ~45–50% · WACC ~11% · Capital Employed minimal relative to revenue · Asset-light FMCG model
✅ ROIC consistently above 40% ✅ Working capital negative (paid before delivered) ✅ Brands = EVA moat
✅ Nestlé India is the perfect example of why consumer brand businesses generate exceptional EVA. Nestlé's Capital Employed is low because: (1) distributors hold inventory on their behalf — Nestlé often has negative working capital (it gets paid before it delivers), (2) manufacturing is efficient with high asset utilisation, (3) brands (Maggi, KitKat, Munch) require no incremental capital to maintain — they're self-reinforcing through consumer habits. ROIC of 45–50% on ~₹3,200 Cr capital employed generates ~₹1,200 Cr of EVA annually. At 25× EVA = ₹30,000 Cr implied value from EVA alone — aligned with Nestlé's market cap. High P/E of 60–70× is justified when you decompose it through EVA: the market is paying for decades of future positive EVA from Maggi's market position.

This sustained positive spread explains why HDFC Bank trades at 3–4× P/B while PSU banks trade at 0.8–1.2× P/B — PSU banks have ROEs that barely match or fall below their cost of equity. HDFC Bank's P/B premium = present value of all future positive ROE-CoE spreads.

05 — Persistent Value Destroyers

5 Companies Where ROIC Chronically Trails WACC

These companies report profits but destroy economic value year after year. Negative EVA explains why they trade below book value and why growth in these businesses makes things worse — not better.

📡
Vodafone Idea — The Most Extreme EVA Destruction in Indian Corporate History
ROIC deeply negative · WACC ~15% · Net Loss ₹31,000 Cr in FY24 · Survival in question
🚨 Negative NOPAT every year 🚨 Capital base shrinking + loss widening 🚨 EVA = −₹47,000+ Cr annually
🚨 Vodafone Idea is the starkest example of sustained EVA destruction in Indian corporate history. With ₹1.1+ lakh Cr of capital employed (spectrum licenses, towers, fiber) and a WACC of ~15% (high risk premium due to survival uncertainty), the minimum capital charge alone is ~₹16,500 Cr annually. Against this, Vi has been generating negative NOPAT (operating losses). The EVA is not just negative — it's deeply negative every single year since 2019. The EVA framework explains precisely why Vi's stock has collapsed from ₹100+ to under ₹10: negative EVA means the company destroys more value each year than it earns. Shareholders are not just missing returns — they are having their capital consumed. Any P/E analysis on Vi is meaningless; EVA correctly signals the distress.
🏭
SAIL (Steel Authority of India) — Capital-Intensive at Below-WACC Returns
ROIC ~6–8% · WACC ~11% · ₹52,000 Cr capital employed · Steel price sensitivity
🚨 ROIC below WACC in most years 🚨 Massive capital base → large EVA deficit 🚨 Government ownership = no capital discipline
🚨 SAIL demonstrates how a capital-intensive business with structurally low returns destroys value even when reporting accounting profit. With capital employed of ~₹52,000 Cr and an estimated WACC of 11%, the capital charge is ~₹5,720 Cr annually. In typical years, SAIL's NOPAT is ₹3,000–₹4,000 Cr — well below the capital charge. EVA = −₹1,500 to −₹2,700 Cr. Only in steel super-cycle peaks (FY22) does SAIL briefly produce positive EVA. The lesson: for commodity companies, consistently report whether ROIC exceeds WACC — in most years, it doesn't. This is also why government-owned steel companies trade at 0.5–0.8× P/B. The EVA framework correctly signals that SAIL should trade below book value — the business destroys value in normal years, and the government's social objectives prevent capital discipline.
✈️
Airlines (Structural EVA Destroyers) — SpiceJet / IndiGo Capital Intensity
Aviation globally earns below WACC · Massive lease obligations · Fuel price exposure
🚨 ROIC below WACC in most years 🚨 Aircraft leases = massive capital charge 🚨 Warren Buffett: "Investors would have done better shorting airlines"
🚨 Airlines are the textbook example of an industry that earns below its cost of capital on a long-run average basis — making the entire sector a persistent EVA destroyer. Aircraft leases under Ind AS 116 are now capitalised on the balance sheet — inflating capital employed. High fixed costs (aircraft, crew, maintenance), fuel price sensitivity, and intense price competition prevent airlines from earning sustained ROIC above WACC. Even IndiGo (India's most profitable airline) has historically earned ROIC that barely covers its WACC in good years, and deeply negative EVA in bad ones. SpiceJet has been in persistent distress. The EVA framework explains why airline stocks in India have been terrible long-term investments despite India being one of the world's fastest-growing aviation markets — high growth + below-WACC ROIC = value destruction at scale.
🏗️
Infrastructure PSUs — Over-Capitalised, Under-Return
NHPC · NMDC · ONGC · Asset-heavy with government-regulated returns below market WACC
🚨 Regulated returns capped below WACC 🚨 Government mandates below-market pricing 🚨 P/B below 1× justified by negative EVA spread
🚨 Many PSU infrastructure companies (NHPC for hydropower, NMDC for iron ore, ONGC for oil) operate under regulated return frameworks that cap ROIC below the free-market WACC. NHPC earns a government-regulated return on equity of 15.5% on its hydro assets — but its cost of equity in the free market may be 14–16%. The spread is negligible or negative once adjusted for actual risks. ONGC's ROIC fluctuates with oil prices — in low-oil years, it earns below WACC on its massive capital base. These companies trade at 0.7–1.2× P/B precisely because the EVA framework shows either zero or negative economic value generation. The EVA analysis correctly predicts that growth in these businesses adds little value — investing more capital at below-WACC returns destroys shareholder value, even as the company grows.
🔌
Highly Leveraged Promoter-Heavy Companies — Capital Structure EVA Trap
High debt → high WACC → EVA destruction even when operating margins are acceptable
🚨 Debt raises WACC dramatically 🚨 Operating ROIC may be fine; EVA negative 🚨 Financial distress premium in WACC
🚨 Over-leveraged companies illustrate how capital structure can turn positive ROIC into negative EVA. Consider a company with operating ROIC of 10% — which would be decent in many sectors. But if it carries ₹20,000 Cr of debt at 12% interest, plus equity requiring 18% return (due to financial distress premium on equity), the WACC could be 14–15%. Now 10% ROIC against 14–15% WACC produces EVA of −4% to −5% spread on the capital base. This is the EVA trap for companies like Reliance Communications (before collapse), Yes Bank (before crisis), or various over-leveraged infrastructure companies. The danger: management shows "operating profit improvement" and "revenue growth" — while EVA continues to be deeply negative due to the capital structure. Always calculate EVA using the actual WACC reflecting financial distress risk, not the theoretical WACC at target leverage.

Calculate → EVA = NOPAT − (WACC × CE) — Is it positive or negative?
Now combine: EVA = NOPAT − (WACC × Capital Employed). Or equivalently: EVA = (ROIC − WACC) × Capital Employed. Both give the same answer. The sign tells you everything: Positive → value creator, premium P/B justified. Negative → value destroyer, should trade below book value. Then trend-check: is the EVA spread widening (great) or narrowing (risk of derating)?

5
Interpret → EVA vs P/B — Is the market pricing it correctly?
Compare EVA spread to P/B ratio. The relationship: P/B should be above 1× only if EVA is positive. P/B > 1× means the market is paying for future EVA. If a stock trades at P/B 5× but ROIC barely exceeds WACC (+1% spread), investors are overpaying — the future EVA won't justify that premium. If a stock trades at P/B 0.9× with ROIC consistently 20%+ above WACC, the stock is deeply undervalued — the market is incorrectly discounting the EVA generation. This EVA-to-P/B crosscheck is one of the most powerful yet underused tools in Indian equity analysis.
✅ P/B < 5× with ROIC−WACC spread > 15%: likely undervalued. The market is not fully pricing in the EVA generation. Worth investigating as a potential investment.
🚨 P/B > 3× with ROIC−WACC spread < 2%: likely overvalued. Investors are paying for EVA that isn't actually being generated. High risk of P/B compression.

The resulting list will be almost exclusively value-creating businesses — TCS, Nestlé, Asian Paints, Pidilite, HDFC Bank will all appear. This is your hunting ground for compounders.
B
EVA Improvement Signal — Widening Spread
A company whose ROIC−WACC spread is widening is even more valuable than one with stable high spread. Why? Widening spread means the company is deploying capital at ever-higher returns — a sign of improving competitive moat. Compare ROCE from 5 years ago vs today on Screener: if ROCE went from 12% to 22% while WACC stayed stable at 11%, the EVA spread jumped from 1% to 11% — a 10× improvement in value creation per rupee of capital. These "improving EVA" stories command P/B rerating as the market catches up.
C
Asset-Light = EVA Superpower
The fastest way to improve EVA is to reduce Capital Employed while maintaining NOPAT — this is what asset-light business models do. Companies that use franchising (McDonald's model), distribution-led brands (Pidilite), or pure software (TCS) carry minimal fixed assets. Their Capital Employed is tiny relative to their revenue, so even moderate NOPAT produces 30–100%+ ROIC. When analysing EVA, always check: is the company asset-light (low CE) or asset-heavy (high CE)? Asset-light companies structurally generate more EVA per rupee of revenue.
D
Working Capital as the Hidden EVA Lever
Companies can improve EVA without changing NOPAT — simply by reducing working capital (which reduces Capital Employed). A company that collects receivables faster (lower debtor days), reduces inventory, and pays suppliers slower (higher creditor days) compresses its Cash Conversion Cycle — reducing Capital Employed. This mechanical improvement boosts ROIC and EVA with no change in operating performance. On Screener: watch "Debtor Days" and "Inventory Days" trends. Declining working capital ratios = improving EVA, even when NOPAT is flat.

07 — Reference Glossary

EVA & Residual Income Glossary

Term What It Means in Simple Words Key Point
EVA (Economic Value Added)EVA = NOPAT − (WACC × Capital Employed). The rupee amount of value created above the minimum required return. Positive EVA = true value creation beyond accounting profit. Negative EVA = destruction of economic value even when the company reports accounting profit. Invented by Stern Stewart & Co. and widely used in executive compensation and investment analysis.Core metric
NOPATNet Operating Profit After Tax = EBIT × (1 − Tax Rate). The after-tax operating profit the business generates regardless of its capital structure. Unlike PAT (Net Profit), NOPAT is not reduced by interest expense — making it directly comparable across companies with different debt levels. On Screener.in, start with Operating Profit (EBIT) and apply the effective tax rate.Not PAT
ROICReturn on Invested Capital = NOPAT ÷ Capital Employed. The operating return on every rupee of capital invested in the business. Comparable to ROCE (Return on Capital Employed) which Screener.in shows directly. The single most important financial ratio for assessing whether a business creates or destroys value. Companies with ROIC consistently above 20% are typically exceptional businesses with durable competitive advantages.Key ratio
WACCWeighted Average Cost of Capital = the blended minimum return required by all capital providers (equity shareholders + debt holders). WACC = (Cost of Equity × Equity Weight) + (After-tax Cost of Debt × Debt Weight). Cost of Equity estimated using CAPM: Risk-Free Rate + Beta × Equity Risk Premium (India: ~7% + Beta × 5.5%). WACC is the hurdle rate — every rupee of invested capital must earn at least this to create value.Hurdle rate
EVA SpreadROIC − WACC. The percentage points by which returns exceed (or fall short of) the cost of capital. A positive spread means value is being created; a negative spread means value is being destroyed. The spread times the Capital Employed gives the rupee EVA. A company growing its capital base at a positive EVA spread creates compounding value; a company growing at a negative spread destroys compounding value.The key signal
Capital Employed (CE)The total capital at work in the business = Fixed Assets + Net Working Capital. Or: Total Assets − Non-interest-bearing Current Liabilities. This is the denominator of ROIC. Companies that reduce CE while maintaining NOPAT (by reducing inventory, collecting receivables faster, or using asset-light models) improve ROIC mechanically. Screener.in shows CE in the Ratios section.ROIC denominator
Residual Income (RI)Equity-based equivalent of EVA: Residual Income = Net Income − (Cost of Equity × Book Value of Equity). While EVA uses WACC and all capital, Residual Income uses only the equity perspective. For banks, RI is more intuitive: ROE − Cost of Equity = RI spread. HDFC Bank's ROE of 17% vs Cost of Equity of 14% = +3% RI spread, justifying P/B premium above 1×.Equity focus
P/B and EVA LinkageMarket Value = Book Value + PV(all future EVAs). This is the fundamental valuation relationship in EVA framework. P/B above 1× implies the market expects future positive EVA. P/B below 1× implies negative EVA expected. The P/B premium is literally the price investors pay for future EVA generation. High ROIC companies with moats deserve high P/B; low ROIC companies should trade below book.Valuation link
Capital ChargeWACC × Capital Employed. The minimum return the business must generate to simply cover its cost of capital — not create any surplus. If NOPAT equals the Capital Charge exactly, EVA = 0 and the business earns its required return but creates zero surplus value. Any NOPAT above the Capital Charge is true economic profit (EVA). Any NOPAT below is economic loss.Minimum bar
ROCE vs ROICROCE (Return on Capital Employed) = EBIT ÷ Capital Employed — the pre-tax version. ROIC = NOPAT ÷ Capital Employed — the after-tax version. Screener.in shows ROCE; to get ROIC, multiply by (1 − Tax Rate). Example: ROCE 30%, Tax Rate 25% → ROIC = 30% × 0.75 = 22.5%. When comparing to WACC (which is also after-tax), always use ROIC, not ROCE — otherwise you're comparing pre-tax returns to post-tax costs.Use ROIC for EVA
