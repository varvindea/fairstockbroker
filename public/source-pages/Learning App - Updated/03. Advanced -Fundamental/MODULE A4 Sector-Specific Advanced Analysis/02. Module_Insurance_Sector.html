01 — Key Learning Outcome

Why Standard Accounting Cannot Value an Insurance Company

🛡️
Your Core Takeaway
When you buy a life insurance policy, you commit to paying premiums for 20–30 years. The insurance company commits to paying you a death or maturity benefit. Standard P&L accounting cannot capture the full economic value of this long-term relationship. That is why the insurance industry developed its own valuation framework — Embedded Value (EV) — which shows what the company is worth today based on all future cash flows from existing and new business.
Imagine a fruit garden. The accounting P&L shows only this year's fruit harvest — the premiums collected and claims paid. But the real value of the garden is all the future fruit from existing trees (Value in Force — VIF) plus the value of new trees being planted each year (Value of New Business — VNB).

Embedded Value = Net Asset Value (shareholder funds already in the business) + Value in Force (present value of future profits from existing policies). This is the fair value of a life insurer — not its P&L profit for the year.

VNB (Value of New Business) tells you how much value is being created from new policies written this year — the growth engine of a life insurer. VNB Margin tells you the profitability of each rupee of new premium collected: a VNB Margin of 30% means ₹30 of present-value profit is embedded in every ₹100 of new premium.

Persistency Ratio tells you what percentage of policyholders are still paying premiums in subsequent years. A 61st-month persistency of 85% means 85 of every 100 original policyholders are still with the company after 5 years — an excellent franchise indicator. Low persistency = policies lapsing = VIF eroding = EV impacted.

For General Insurance (motor, health, property): the key metric is the Combined Ratio = Claims Ratio + Expense Ratio. A Combined Ratio below 100% means the insurer makes underwriting profit. Above 100% = underwriting loss — it only survives by earning investment income on the float.
📊 EV = NAV + Value in Force (VIF) 💰 VNB = new business value created 🔄 Persistency = renewal rate ⚖️ Combined Ratio <100% = profit
02 — The Core Insurance Metrics Explained

Five Numbers That Define Every Insurer — Plain Language Explanations

Insurance valuation uses different metrics for life vs general insurers. Master these five — and you can assess the quality, growth, and valuation of any listed insurer in India.

The garden analogy: a life insurer's existing policies are like mature trees bearing future fruit (Value in Force). New policies written each year are new trees (VNB). Lapsed policies are dead trees — the customer stopped paying, future premiums are lost, and VIF declines. The actuary measures the present value of all future fruit from all trees — that is the Embedded Value.

The Five Core Insurance Metrics — Life and General
💎
EV
Embedded Value
Life Insurers — Core Value
EV = Adjusted Net Asset Value (NAV) + Value In Force (VIF). VIF is the present value of all future profits from existing policies, discounted at a risk-adjusted rate. You value life insurers at a multiple of EV — just as you value regular companies at P/E or P/Book.

P/EV above 2× = market expects strong VNB growth. P/EV near 1× = value play or stressed insurer. ROEV (Return on EV) shows how efficiently the insurer grows its EV — like ROE for banks. Best-in-class: 20%+ ROEV.
P/EV 2–4×✅ Growth Insurer
P/EV <1×🚨 Distressed
🌱
VNB
Value of New Business & VNB Margin
Growth Engine
VNB measures the present value of profits embedded in policies sold this year. VNB Margin = VNB ÷ APE (Annualised Premium Equivalent). APE = Regular premiums + 10% of single premiums — the standardised measure of new business volume.

A VNB Margin of 30% means ₹30 of actuarial profit is embedded in every ₹100 of new premium. High-margin products (term plans, non-par savings) generate 35–50% VNB Margins. Low-margin products (ULIP, par policies) generate 10–15%. Tracking VNB Margin tells you whether the growth is quality growth.
>25%✅ High Quality
<12%🚨 Low Quality Mix
🔄
Persist
Persistency Ratio
Policy Renewal Quality
Persistency measures what percentage of policies written in a year are still active (being paid) in subsequent years. IRDAI mandates disclosure at 13th, 25th, 37th, 49th, and 61st months (1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 years).

61st-month persistency of 85%+ is excellent — 85% of policyholders are still with you after 5 years. Below 50% = massive lapse problem — half your policies lapse within 5 years, destroying VIF. Persistency is the single best indicator of customer satisfaction and mis-selling culture. High-pressure distribution agents create low persistency; trust-based distribution creates high persistency.
>80%✅ 61-mth Excellent
<55%🚨 Mis-selling Signal
⚖️
CombR
Combined Ratio (General Insurance)
General Insurer Health
Combined Ratio = Claims Ratio + Expense Ratio. This is the primary metric for general insurers (motor, health, property, marine). A Combined Ratio below 100% means the insurer makes underwriting profit — it earns more from premiums than it pays in claims and expenses. Above 100% = underwriting loss — the insurer survives only by earning investment income on the premium float.

Claims Ratio = Net Claims ÷ Net Premiums. Expense Ratio = Management Expenses ÷ Net Premiums. Best-in-class general insurers maintain Combined Ratio of 95–98%.
<98%✅ Underwriting Profit
>108%🚨 Chronic Loss
📈
ROEV
Return on Embedded Value
EV Compounding Rate
ROEV = (EV at Year End − EV at Year Start + Dividends Paid) ÷ EV at Year Start. This is the equivalent of ROE for life insurers — it measures how fast the insurer is growing its intrinsic value. ROEV = Expected Return on EV + VNB Margin × New Business Strain ± Operating Experience Variances ± Economic Variances.

Best-in-class life insurers generate 20–25% ROEV. This means the EV doubles every 3–4 years. An investor buying at 2× P/EV gets full return if ROEV is 20%+ over a 4-year hold.
>18%✅ EV Compounder
<10%🚨 Slow Growth
🏛️
Solvency
Solvency Ratio / Capital Adequacy
Regulatory Safety
Solvency Ratio = Available Solvency Margin ÷ Required Solvency Margin. IRDAI requires minimum 150% for both life and general insurers. Well-capitalised insurers maintain 180–250%. This is insurance's equivalent of banking's Capital Adequacy Ratio.

Low solvency signals the insurer may need to raise capital — diluting equity. Very high solvency (above 300%) may signal under-deployment of capital — the insurer is not growing fast enough to utilise its capital. Ideal range for growth insurers: 175–220%.
175–220%✅ Growth Capital
<150%🚨 Near Minimum

61-month persistency below 55% — nearly half of all policyholders lapse within 5 years
LIC's 61-month persistency below 55% means that nearly half of all policies sold in any given year are lapsed (the customer has stopped paying premiums) within 5 years. This is the forensic indicator of mis-selling or product mismatch — customers who didn't fully understand or need what was sold to them stop paying as soon as financial pressure arises or they understand the product wasn't appropriate. Each lapsed policy: (1) destroys VIF (the actuarial present value of that policy's future premiums is lost), (2) forces LIC to pay back part of the premium (surrender value), and (3) erodes the EV growth that VNB was supposed to generate. LIC's new business is overwhelmingly in Participating (par) policies — products where policyholders share in investment returns through bonuses. Par products have VNB Margins of 10–15% because: (1) the insurer must pass most investment returns to policyholders, (2) the guaranteed benefit creates high actuarial liability, and (3) distribution costs for agent-sold par products are high. Compare to non-par savings at 25–35% VNB Margin and term protection at 50–70%. LIC selling predominantly par products means each ₹100 of premium creates only ₹10–15 of shareholder value — vs ₹28–30 at HDFC Life or ₹35 at protection-focused players.
🚨 LIC structural lesson: LIC's scale (67% market share by first-year premium) masks its structural quality weakness — low persistency + low VNB Margin + par-heavy mix + agent-commission culture. Track the persistency improvement as the primary indicator of LIC's franchise quality change — it's the metric that matters most for EV durability.

ULIP mis-selling era 2005–2010 — low persistency destroyed reported VNB, demonstrating it was never real
In the period 2005–2010, multiple life insurers (including bank-promoted private players) aggressively pushed ULIP products through bancassurance channels by offering high commissions to bank relationship managers. ULIPs were sold as "investments" with 30–40% first-year charges — meaning 40% of the first year premium went to distribution charges before any investment. 🚨

ULIP products in the high-charge era generated VNB Margins of 8–12% because 30–40% of the premium went to distribution (agent/banker commissions), and the policyholder kept the remaining 60–70% invested in market-linked funds — leaving very little spread for the insurer. ⚠️ The ULIP mis-selling lesson: Low persistency + low VNB Margin is a definitive forensic signal that a life insurer is selling products customers don't want or need. The VNB at inception does not materialise as actual value if policies lapse. Track 61-month persistency alongside VNB Margin — an insurer with VNB Margin 25% but persistency 50% is not creating as much value as the VNB headline suggests. Adjust for the expected lapse rate to get the "effective VNB" that will actually materialise.

Life insurance is a scale business — below ₹8,000–10,000 Cr APE, operating expense ratios destroy VNB
Life insurance requires massive fixed cost infrastructure: actuarial teams, claims processing systems, compliance departments, investment management operations, and persistency management teams. These fixed costs are largely the same whether the insurer has ₹3,000 Cr or ₹30,000 Cr of APE. A small insurer with ₹3,000 Cr APE might have operating expenses of ₹1,000 Cr — a 33% expense ratio. 🚨
EV stagnation or decline signals a capital trap — shareholder money deployed but not compounding
When a life insurer's EV is not growing (ROEV below 10%), it is a capital trap — equity is deployed in a business that does not generate adequate returns. Small private life insurers (10+ exist in India) that have not achieved scale profitability are effectively burning shareholder capital: they write policies (generating apparent VNB), but the VNB is absorbed by operating expenses, leaving EV flat or declining. This is analogous to a factory that produces goods but sells them at cost — all the activity is real but no value is created. The ROEV below cost of equity is the definitive signal to exit or avoid such insurers.
⚠️
Industry consolidation is the solution — scale unlocks operating leverage
The long-term outcome for sub-scale life insurers is consolidation — larger players acquire them to capture distribution networks and policyholders at below-replacement-cost valuations. When an acquisition is announced (e.g., HDFC Standard Life acquiring Exide Life, Max Life absorbing smaller players), investors should evaluate: (1) is the acquired insurer's book of business persistency above 60%? (2) what is the VIF being acquired, and at what multiple to EV? (3) will the combined entity achieve expense synergies that improve ROEV? Sub-scale + high expense ratio + low persistency acquisitions are value-destructive even at seemingly cheap P/EV multiples.
⚠️ Insurance scale lesson: Life insurance is fundamentally a scale business. An insurer below ₹8,000–10,000 Cr APE with ROEV below 12% is not compounding EV — it is a capital trap. Avoid small life insurers unless there is a clear path to scale (strategic parent commitment, rapid distribution expansion, or imminent acquisition by a larger player). The economics of insurance only work at scale — and ROEV is the definitive test of whether scale has been achieved.

The One-Line Insurance Rule
For Life Insurers: Persistency × VNB Margin × ROEV = Quality of EV Compounding. For General Insurers: Combined Ratio Below 100% = Underwriting Profit. Everything Else Is Secondary.
Insurance is deceptively simple — a life insurer collects premiums today, invests them, and pays claims in the future. The difference between premiums collected and claims paid (discounted back to today) is the Embedded Value. Growing EV quickly (high ROEV) with high-quality new business (high VNB Margin) and sticky customers (high persistency) is the entire investment thesis for life insurers in one sentence.

For general insurers, the equivalent simplicity: collect more in premiums than you pay in claims and expenses (Combined Ratio below 100%), grow the premium base, and earn investment income on the float. Every good general insurer does this. Every distressed general insurer doesn't — and makes up the shortfall with investment income on legacy portfolios.

The five metrics — EV, VNB Margin, Persistency, Combined Ratio, Solvency — are not five separate analyses. They are five windows into one question: Does this insurer price risk correctly, retain its customers, grow its franchise, and compound its capital? The garden analogy says it best: a life insurer with 87% persistency is tending its trees well — they keep bearing fruit. A life insurer with 45% persistency is watching half its trees die each year before they can produce the fruit the actuary assumed they would.

Insurance & Actuarial Glossary

Every insurance-specific term used in this chapter — defined in plain language so you never have to stop reading to look something up. Bookmark and refer back whenever you encounter these terms in quarterly investor presentations, IRDAI filings, or annual reports.

Term What It Means in Simple Words Key Point
Embedded Value (EV) The present value of all future profits from a life insurer's existing book of policies, plus its Net Asset Value. EV = Adjusted Net Asset Value (ANAV) + Value In Force (VIF). Think of it as the "fair value" of a life insurer — the amount the business would be worth if no new policies were ever written again. The standard valuation metric for life insurers; equivalent to "book value" for banks. Core valuation base
VIF — Value In Force The present value of future profits from all currently active (in force) policies. Calculated by actuaries using models projecting premiums, claims, lapses, expenses, and investment returns over the full remaining term of each policy. VIF is the "future fruit" from existing trees. It grows when new policies are added (VNB) and shrinks when policies lapse (low persistency). EV component 2 of 2
VNB — Value of New Business The present value of profits embedded in policies sold this year — the annual growth engine of EV. VNB answers: "How much shareholder value are we creating from this year's new business?" A growing VNB year-on-year means the insurer is compounding its intrinsic value faster. VNB is calculated at policy sale using the same actuarial assumptions as EV. Annual value created
VNB Margin VNB expressed as a percentage of APE (Annualised Premium Equivalent). A VNB Margin of 28% means ₹28 of actuarial present-value profit is created for every ₹100 of new premium. Higher is better. Reveals product mix quality: term/non-par = 30–50% margins; ULIP = 10–15%; par = 12–20%. Track the direction quarterly — rising margin = quality improvement. Product mix quality
APE — Annualised Premium Equivalent Standardised measure making regular and single-premium policies comparable. APE = Annual Regular Premiums + 10% of Single Premiums. The 10% factor normalises single premiums (one-time payment) against recurring ones. APE is the denominator of VNB Margin and the headline new business volume metric. Always use APE — not raw premiums — when comparing insurers. Standardised volume
ROEV — Return on Embedded Value The rate at which EV grows each year — the life insurer's equivalent of ROE. ROEV = (Closing EV − Opening EV + Dividends Paid) ÷ Opening EV. A 20% ROEV means intrinsic value doubles approximately every 3.6 years. The most important metric for judging whether a high P/EV multiple is justified. An insurer with 20% ROEV at 2.5× P/EV delivers ~20% annual returns if the multiple holds. EV compounding rate
Persistency Ratio Percentage of policies still active (being paid) after a given number of months. IRDAI mandates disclosure at 13th, 25th, 37th, 49th, and 61st months. The 61-month (5-year) figure is most important: 87%+ = trust-based distribution (HDFC Life benchmark); below 55% = mis-selling signal. Low persistency destroys VIF — future premiums assumed in the actuarial model never arrive, eroding EV. 61-mth >78% = good
Lapse Rate Inverse of persistency — the percentage of policies surrendered, lapsed, or cancelled. A 61-month lapse rate of 45% means nearly half of all policies were discontinued within 5 years. High lapse rates shrink VIF and create negative "experience variance" in EV movement disclosures. When actual lapses exceed the actuarial model's assumed lapse rate, this flows through as a direct deduction from EV. VIF destroyer
Combined Ratio The primary health metric for general insurers. Combined Ratio = Claims Ratio + Expense Ratio. Below 100% = underwriting profit. Above 100% = underwriting loss — the insurer survives only if investment income on the premium float compensates. Best-in-class private general insurers maintain 95–98%. PSU general insurers often run 110–120% — structurally loss-making at the underwriting level. <100% = profit
Claims Ratio (Loss Ratio) Net Claims Paid ÷ Net Premiums Earned. What percentage of every rupee collected in premium goes back out as claims. A ratio of 72% means 72p in every ₹1 of premium is paid as claims. Rising claims ratio signals deteriorating underwriting quality, adverse events (pandemic, floods), or pricing below risk. Strong private general insurers target 68–74%. Part 1 of CombR
Expense Ratio Management Expenses ÷ Net Premiums Earned. Operational efficiency — commissions, salaries, technology, admin as a percentage of premiums. Private sector leaders maintain 18–22%. PSU general insurers often exceed 28% due to large permanent headcount and government procurement constraints. A falling expense ratio as premium scale grows = operational leverage being realised. Part 2 of CombR
ULIP — Unit Linked Insurance Plan Insurance + market-linked investment in one product. Premium is split — part covers insurance risk, the rest is invested in equity or debt funds. Generates low VNB Margins (10–15%) because most of the premium is deployed for the policyholder. Susceptible to mis-selling because the investment component makes it easy to pitch as a "savings product." IRDA's 2010 ULIP regulations capped first-year charges and mandated 5-year lock-ins. Low VNB margin
Non-Participating (Non-Par) Policy Insurance policy where the insurer keeps all investment returns — the policyholder receives only the guaranteed benefit. Because the insurer takes all investment risk and retains all investment upside, non-par products generate superior VNB Margins (25–40% for guaranteed savings, 50–70% for pure term protection). The industry-wide shift from ULIP and par toward non-par is the primary driver of VNB Margin improvement across Indian life insurers. Highest VNB margin
Participating (Par) Policy Policy where policyholders share in the insurer's investment returns through annual reversionary bonuses and terminal bonuses. Because a large share of investment returns must be passed to policyholders, par products generate lower VNB Margins (12–20%). LIC's book is predominantly par — the key reason its VNB Margin is structurally below private insurer peers despite massive scale. Shared returns
Bancassurance Distribution of insurance products through bank branches. A life insurer partners with a bank — HDFC Life with HDFC Bank, SBI Life with SBI — and sells policies through the bank's branch network. The most efficient insurance distribution channel: low cost per policy, large captive customer base, and typically higher persistency than pure agency because customers have an existing trusted relationship with the bank. Distribution moat
Solvency Ratio Available Solvency Margin ÷ Required Solvency Margin. Insurance's Capital Adequacy Ratio equivalent. IRDAI mandates a minimum of 150% for all insurers — life and general. Falling toward 150% = any adverse claims event triggers regulatory intervention and growth restrictions. Ideal range for a growing insurer: 175–220%. Very high solvency (300%+) may indicate the insurer is not deploying capital efficiently enough. Min 150% IRDAI
Actuarial Liability / Policy Reserve Money set aside today to pay future policyholder benefits — claims, maturity payments, surrenders. The "Change in Actuarial Liability" is the largest expense line in a life insurer's P&L — the reason PAT is small relative to EV. This is policyholder money, not shareholder money. A growing actuarial liability is not a cost — it means the insurer is growing its VIF. Investors who mistake this line for "expense" will dramatically underestimate EV quality. Not a cost — it's growth
GWP — Gross Written Premium Total premium collected from policyholders before reinsurance costs are deducted. GWP is the top-line revenue measure for general insurers — equivalent to revenue for a regular company. GWP growth is the primary volume metric for tracking business momentum. Net Earned Premium = GWP minus reinsurance ceded. Strong general insurers grow GWP 15–25% annually while maintaining Combined Ratios below 100%. General insurer top line
P/EV — Price to Embedded Value Market Capitalisation ÷ Embedded Value. The standard valuation multiple for life insurers — equivalent to P/E or P/Book for regular companies. P/EV 2–4× = growth insurer with expected strong VNB and ROEV. P/EV near 1× = moderate growth or market concern about EV quality. Below 1× = distressed. Sanity check: P/EV ÷ ROEV below 12 = attractive; above 20 = expensive unless ROEV is set to accelerate sharply. Life insurer multiple
IRDAI Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India. Sets solvency requirements, mandates persistency disclosures, approves product designs, caps expense ratios, and regulates commission structures. The 2010 ULIP reforms (capping first-year charges at 2.25%, mandating 5-year lock-ins) reshaped industry economics and forced a quality improvement across the sector. Mandatory IRDAI disclosures — persistency tables, solvency ratios — are among the most investor-useful data in insurance analysis. Industry regulator
Operating Variance (EV Movement) The difference between actual and expected actuarial experience in the EV movement analysis — disclosed in the annual EV report. Positive operating variance = fewer lapses, fewer deaths, and lower expenses than the model assumed → EV grows faster than expected. Negative variance = actual experience worse than modelled → EV grows slower or declines. Consistently positive operating variance = conservative actuarial assumptions + better-than-expected business execution. EV quality signal
Mortality Experience The actual rate of death claims compared to the actuarial model's expected rate (based on India Assured Lives Mortality tables). Favourable mortality experience = fewer deaths than expected → positive EV variance → good for VIF. Adverse experience = more deaths than expected → negative EV impact. COVID-19 created severe adverse mortality experience across all Indian life insurers in FY2021–22, causing one-time negative EV variances that reversed once the pandemic subsided. Actuarial assumption
Where to find these metrics: EV, VNB, VNB Margin, ROEV → Quarterly investor presentation (search "[Insurer Name] Q4 Investor Presentation" on BSE). Persistency table → Annual Report → Business Review section (IRDAI mandated). Combined Ratio, Claims Ratio, Expense Ratio → General insurer quarterly results. Solvency Ratio → Quarterly investor deck → Capital section. Actuarial Liability movement → Annual report → P&L notes. All data is publicly available and free.

Important Disclaimer
For educational purposes only. Insurance sector analysis involves complex actuarial and regulatory considerations. Embedded Value, VNB, and persistency figures cited are illustrative approximations based on publicly available data — always verify from official company disclosures, IRDAI filings, and investor presentations. Actuarial assumptions (mortality, lapse rates, discount rates) significantly impact EV and VNB calculations. Past persistency ratios do not guarantee future renewals. All equity investments carry market risk. Consult a SEBI-registered investment advisor and an insurance specialist before making investment decisions.
