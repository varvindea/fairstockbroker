01 — Key Learning Outcome

Why IT Analysis Is Really About People, Pipelines, and Pricing Power

💻
Your Core Takeaway
An IT company sells one thing: human intellectual effort delivered as software, infrastructure management, or consulting. Revenue = Headcount × Utilisation Rate × Billing Rate. Everything else in IT analysis flows from understanding how these three drivers change — and what the deal pipeline says about where they're going next.
Think of an IT company as a professional services firm with thousands of employees. Each employee is a "billing unit." The company makes money when: (1) those employees are working on client projects (high utilisation — the equivalent of a hotel's occupancy rate), (2) clients pay higher rates over time (pricing power — the equivalent of Revenue Per Available Room), and (3) the company wins new contracts to replace expiring ones (deal wins — the equivalent of advance bookings).

Revenue Levers are the drivers of IT revenue growth: volume growth (more headcount and higher utilisation) and pricing growth (billing rate increases, mix shift toward higher-value services like cloud, AI, and consulting). Volume × Realisation growth analysis tells you whether revenue growth is real (pricing power) or just hiring more people cheaply.

Attrition is the percentage of employees who leave voluntarily each year. In a people-intensive business, high attrition destroys value: it increases training costs, disrupts client relationships, forces continuous hiring at market rates, and signals that competitors are paying more. Post-COVID attrition of 25–30% forced IT companies to hire at inflated rates and then fire — destroying margins when demand slowed in FY24.

Deal TCV (Total Contract Value) is the pipeline indicator. When a client signs a 5-year ₹500 crore IT outsourcing contract, the TCV is ₹500 crore. Tracking TCV wins quarterly tells you whether the company is building or depleting its future revenue backlog. Large deal TCV wins (above $50M per deal) signal competitive wins, often at the expense of a competitor. Weak deal TCV is the earliest warning of a revenue growth problem — 6–9 months before it shows in revenue.

Currency Hedging matters because 70–85% of IT revenue is in USD/EUR/GBP, while costs are predominantly in INR. Every ₹1 depreciation against USD adds approximately 0.3–0.5% to EBIT margins. Companies with forward hedges lock in an exchange rate for 12–18 months, smoothing out currency volatility. Companies without hedges have inherently volatile margins — good when rupee depreciates, catastrophic when rupee strengthens.

02 — How IT Revenue Works

The IT Company as a Talent Factory — Four Levers That Drive Everything

Unlike a product company that sells widgets, an IT company's entire output is human effort organised into billable projects. Once you understand the factory metaphor — machines (people) × occupancy (utilisation) × price (billing rate) — every IT metric clicks into place.

The IT talent factory: each developer is a billable unit. Revenue = headcount × utilisation × billing rate. Attrition drains the factory of trained workers. Deal TCV fills the order book for future quarters. Currency hedges protect the factory's margins from exchange rate swings. All four levers must be tracked together.

The Four Core IT Metrics — Explained for Non-Tech Readers
📊
Rev Levers
Revenue = Volume × Realisation
Core Revenue Driver
Every quarter, IT companies disclose constant currency revenue growth. Decompose it: Volume growth = more billable headcount + better utilisation. Realisation growth = higher billing rates + service mix shift toward cloud, AI, consulting (which bill higher than legacy IT maintenance).

Quality IT companies grow both: 8–10% volume + 3–5% realisation = 11–15% USD revenue growth. Warning sign: revenue growing only through headcount (volume) without realisation means pricing power is eroding — margin pressure follows. Best signal: realisation growing even when volume is flat = pricing power intact.

Vol Only / -Real🚨 Price Erosion

Attrition
Voluntary Employee Turnover (LTM %)
People Risk Indicator
Attrition % is the percentage of employees who voluntarily resigned in the last 12 months (LTM — Last Twelve Months). It is the single most important people metric in IT. High attrition forces companies to: (1) pay premium salaries to attract replacements, (2) spend 3–6 months training new hires before they're productive, (3) disrupt client relationships when experienced staff leave mid-project.

Post-COVID peak attrition (25–30% in FY23) forced IT companies to hire 50,000–100,000 people at inflated salaries — and then reduce headcount when demand slowed in FY24, causing massive margin compression and restructuring charges.
<15% LTM✅ Stable Workforce
>22% LTM🚨 Cost Spiral Risk
💼
Deal TCV
Total Contract Value — Order Book
Pipeline Visibility
TCV is the total value of new contracts signed during the quarter. A ₹500 crore, 5-year contract has a TCV of ₹500 crore — but only ₹100 crore will convert to annual revenue. TCV is the IT company's order book — it tells you future revenue 12–24 months out.

Key signals: (1) Large deal TCV (individual contracts above $50M) = competitive wins, often displacing an existing vendor. (2) TCV growth rate above revenue growth rate = future acceleration. (3) Total contract duration matters — a 5-year deal is higher quality than five 1-year deals at the same TCV. Track "deal wins" in the quarterly earnings call — it's the closest thing IT has to a forward-looking revenue signal.
TCV > Revenue×4✅ Strong Backlog
TCV declining🚨 Revenue Risk
💱
FX Hedge
Currency Hedging — Margin Shield
Margin Stability
IT companies earn 70–85% of revenue in USD, EUR, GBP, and AUD — but pay 60–70% of costs in INR (employee salaries, office rentals, domestic infrastructure). Every rupee that depreciates against USD adds approximately 0.3–0.5% to EBIT margins. Conversely, rupee appreciation hurts margins.

Hedging strategy: companies use forward contracts and options to lock in exchange rates for 6–18 months. The hedge ratio (% of forecast foreign revenue hedged) determines how protected the company is from near-term currency moves. TCS/Infosys typically hedge 65–80% of near-term exposure. Under-hedged companies have more volatile margins — which can be positive (rupee depreciating) or negative (rupee strengthening).
60–80% hedged✅ Margin Stable
<30% hedged🚨 Volatile Margins
📈
EBIT%
EBIT Margin — Operating Profitability
Efficiency Metric
EBIT margin is the percentage of revenue left after paying all operating costs — employee costs, subcontracting, technology, admin, and depreciation. In IT, EBIT margin reflects: (1) attrition-driven salary cost, (2) utilisation rate (idle benched employees reduce margins), (3) pricing power vs competition, and (4) currency tailwind/headwind.

Margin band: Tier-1 IT (TCS, Infosys) target 23–26% EBIT. Mid-tier IT (LTIM, Persistent) target 16–20%. Margin below 12% signals structural problems — over-hiring at inflated costs, pricing concessions to retain clients, or major project losses. Improving EBIT Margin over 3 years = operational leverage; declining margin = cost pressure without pricing offsets.
20%+ (Tier-1)✅ Operational Leverage
<12%🚨 Structural Concern
⚙️
Utilisation
Billable Utilisation Rate — Bench Size
Efficiency Indicator
Utilisation rate is the percentage of total employees who are working on billable client projects (vs sitting "on the bench" waiting for project assignment). It is the IT equivalent of a hotel's occupancy rate or a plane's seat load factor. High utilisation = low bench = high revenue efficiency. Low utilisation = large bench = revenue being lost on unproductive employees.

Typical range: 80–85% utilisation = healthy. Below 75% = over-hiring relative to project demand. Above 87% = risk of project quality issues (no slack capacity) and high attrition (employees are over-utilised and burnt out). Watch for utilisation declining 2–3 quarters before margin compression appears in P&L.
80–86%✅ Optimal Range
<75%🚨 Bench Too Large

EBIT Margin 24–25% sustained through multiple demand cycles — operational excellence at scale
TCS has maintained EBIT margins of 24–26% through demand slowdowns (FY24 BFSI freeze), high attrition years (FY23 post-COVID), and USD currency headwinds — demonstrating that its margin is structural, not cyclical. The key drivers: (1) Industry-low attrition (12.5% vs sector average 15–18%) means lower replacement hiring costs. (2) Strong utilisation management — the company rapidly moves bench employees to new project allocations across its diversified client base. (3) Revenue mix increasingly toward higher-margin services (AI, cloud transformation) offsetting commoditised application maintenance. EBIT margin sustained above 24% for 15+ consecutive years is TCS's primary quality proof point.
✅
$30B+ TCV in FY25 — the largest annual deal win in TCS's history signals strong FY26 visibility
TCS's FY25 deal TCV of $30B+ (including several mega-deals above $1B each) means the company has signed contracts that will convert to revenue over the next 3–5 years. The mix is equally important: large deals above $50M accounted for approximately 60% of TCV — indicating competitive wins at the expense of rivals, not just small renewals. Mega-deals also have longer contract durations (5–7 years), providing multi-year revenue visibility. For investors: when a company's TCV run rate is significantly above its annualised revenue ($29B), it signals future revenue acceleration — a leading indicator available 12–18 months before actual revenue growth shows up.
✅ IT quality verdict: TCS is the reference standard for Indian IT. EBIT margin 24–25% + attrition 12.5% + TCV $30B+ + $29B revenue scale = the best risk-adjusted IT franchise in India. The margin stability across cycles demonstrates operational excellence; the TCV run rate provides multi-year revenue visibility. Use TCS as the benchmark when evaluating all other Indian IT companies.

400–600bps revenue growth gap vs TCS/Infosys sustained for 8+ years — market share loss, not cyclical
Wipro's revenue growth consistently lagged TCS and Infosys by 400–600 basis points per year for nearly a decade. This is not a cyclical problem — the gap persisted through good (FY22 +26%) and bad (FY24 +1.5%) IT demand environments. A persistent multi-year growth gap against comparable peers is the clearest signal of structural market share loss. The root causes: fragmented organisational structure (multiple P&L silos competing internally rather than presenting a unified front to clients), inconsistent deal pursuit strategy, and go-to-market structure that was reorganised multiple times (Strategic Market Units, then Global Business Lines, then back to SBUs) without resolving the underlying execution gaps.
⚠️
Multiple CEO changes + strategy pivots = organisational instability that top clients and top talent avoid
Large enterprise clients (Fortune 500 companies) building multi-year IT outsourcing relationships with Wipro needed consistent leadership, consistent delivery methodology, and consistent strategic vision from their vendor. When Wipro changed its strategy multiple times and brought in a new CEO (Thierry Delaporte in 2020, then succeeded by Srinivas Pallia in 2024), each transition triggered re-evaluation of client relationships, portfolio prioritisation, and talent management. Top IT talent — the actual delivery resource — prefers employers with visible growth trajectories and stable culture. Wipro's inconsistency created a self-reinforcing cycle: strategic uncertainty → talent uncertainty → delivery risk → client confidence erosion → slower deal wins.
⚠️ Wipro structural lesson: Revenue CAGR gap vs sector peers is the most important IT metric for relative quality assessment. If a company grows 8% CC while TCS grows 13% and Infosys grows 12% in the same demand environment — year after year — it is losing market share. This is the IT equivalent of GNPA rising in banking. The investor lesson: compare IT company revenue growth to its closest peer cohort, not to absolute benchmarks. Consistent underperformance vs peers = structural issue, not bad luck.

Telecom vertical at 35%+ of revenue — when telco capex spending froze globally, Tech Mahindra had no hedge
Tech Mahindra built its IT services business heavily around telecom clients — AT&T, Verizon, Deutsche Telekom, and telecom OEMs (Nokia, Ericsson ecosystem). This was a logical strategy when 5G investment cycles were accelerating (FY21–22). But when global telco capex froze in FY23–24 (rising interest rates making debt-funded network investment uneconomical), Tech Mahindra's concentrated telecom exposure became a structural liability. 35% of revenue from a single vertical that simultaneously cut IT spending = revenue absolute decline and margin collapse. The lesson for IT investors: single-vertical concentration above 25–30% of revenue is a structural risk that becomes existential when that vertical enters a capex downcycle.
🚨
EBIT margin collapse from 11% to 5% in one year — project-specific losses and revenue decline combined
Tech Mahindra's FY24 margin collapse had two components: (1) Revenue decline from telecom vertical reducing fixed-cost absorption — when revenue falls but employee costs and infrastructure costs are fixed, margins compress mechanically. (2) Project-specific losses on large fixed-price contracts that were bid aggressively at thin margins and then suffered cost overruns. Fixed-price IT projects are inherently riskier than time-and-material (T&M) because the IT company absorbs cost overruns. When attrition was 24%+ and replacement hiring costs surged, projects bid at 2021 attrition assumptions became loss-making at 2022–23 attrition reality. The combination of revenue decline + project losses = EBIT margin collapse from 11% to 5%.
🚨 Tech Mahindra lesson: Track vertical concentration in every IT company's revenue mix disclosure. More than 30% in any single vertical = elevated cyclicality. Tech Mahindra's telecom + Verizon concentration was visible in every quarterly segmental disclosure for 5 years before the FY24 collapse. Combined with high attrition (24%+) and fixed-price contract exposure, the margin risk was computable in advance — not hindsight.

28%+ LTM attrition forced hiring 300,000+ fresh graduates at inflated salaries across the sector in FY23
Post-COVID demand surge (FY22: Infosys +19.7% CC, TCS +15.4% CC) combined with a hot labour market as tech companies globally competed for the same talent pool pushed LTM attrition to 25–30% across Indian IT. To replace departing employees and service accelerating demand, companies hired 300,000+ employees collectively — many at 30–50% salary premiums vs pre-COVID. These hires had: (1) 3–6 month ramp-up time before billing, (2) higher base salaries than incumbents, and (3) project allocation challenges as demand began slowing in late FY23. The result: by FY24, utilisation fell from 85% to 72–76% across mid-tier IT — creating a massive bench of unbillable employees on inflated salaries. This was the single largest margin compression event in Indian IT history.
🚨
The attrition signal was visible 6–9 months before margin compression — investors who tracked LTM attrition could see it coming
LTM attrition rising from 14% (FY21) to 20% (Q2 FY22) to 28% (Q4 FY23) was disclosed in every quarterly earnings call. The margin impact was computable: each 1% increase in attrition requires approximately 1.5% additional salary spend for replacement hiring, training, and retention bonuses. At 28% LTM attrition vs 14% baseline, the incremental salary cost was 21% of the payroll base above normal — or approximately 12–15% of total revenue in salary cost inflation. This was a 250–350bps margin headwind visible 6 months before it appeared in P&L. Companies reporting rising attrition in Q1 FY23 saw margin compression in Q3 FY23 and Q4 FY23 — investors who reduced exposure on the attrition signal avoided the margin compression trade.
🚨 The attrition-to-margin lead-lag lesson: Rising LTM attrition → 2–3 quarter lag → margin compression. This lead-lag relationship is one of the most reliable forward indicators in IT analysis. When LTM attrition rises more than 3 percentage points in a single quarter, model in a 150–250bps EBIT margin headwind over the following 6 months. The reverse is equally reliable: declining LTM attrition → 2–3 quarter lag → margin recovery. This is why attrition is the most important number to track in every IT earnings call.

Rupee strengthening 8% in a year = 300–400bps EBIT margin hit for an unhedged IT company
In 2012–13, the Indian rupee strengthened against the USD by approximately 8% over 6 months (partially reversing the post-2008 depreciation). IT companies with low hedge ratios — under 30% of forward revenue covered — saw EBIT margins compress by 300–400bps in a single fiscal year purely from currency movement, with no change in their operational performance. The mechanics: if 80% of revenue is in USD and USD weakens against INR by 8%, INR revenue from USD sources falls 6.4% (80% × 8%) even with flat operational performance. With costs mostly in INR (fixed), the margin compression is direct and immediate. Companies with 70–80% forward cover locked in rates before the rupee strengthened — their margins were insulated for 12–18 months.
🚨
Currency as a profit lever (not managing it) = management quality signal
Some mid-tier IT companies historically used currency depreciation as a margin "free lunch" — when the rupee depreciated, margins expanded nicely, and management claimed "operational improvement." When the rupee strengthened, margins compressed, and management cited "currency headwinds" as an excuse. Disciplined management (TCS, Infosys, HCL) maintains consistent hedge ratios regardless of currency direction — because their goal is to show investors that EBIT margins reflect genuine operational performance, not random currency movements. An IT company that credits "rupee depreciation" for margin improvement in a good year and blames "rupee appreciation" for margin decline in a bad year has not built a genuine operational business — it has been speculating on currency. Check the annual report's hedging footnote: stable hedge ratios = management discipline; fluctuating hedge ratios = gambling on currency direction.
🚨 Currency hedging lesson: The investor check for hedging quality: (1) Annual report → Notes to Accounts → "Derivative Financial Instruments" — find the outstanding forward cover in USD/EUR/GBP. (2) Divide outstanding hedge value by quarterly revenue × 4 to get approximate hedge ratio. (3) Compare this to 60–80% benchmark. (4) Check if hedge ratio is consistent year over year (disciplined policy) or fluctuating (gambling on currency). Under-hedged IT companies look like heroes when rupee depreciates and victims when it appreciates — but neither reflects management quality. Hedge consistently and let operations drive margins.

The One-Line IT Analysis Rule
Revenue = Headcount × Utilisation × Billing Rate. Attrition Predicts Margin. TCV Predicts Revenue. Hedge Ratio Predicts Margin Volatility. Four Levers, Eight Minutes, Free Data.
IT analysis is simpler than it appears. Ignore the technology jargon, the service line names, and the marketing buzzwords. Ask four questions every quarter: (1) Is attrition rising or falling — and what does that say about margins 2 quarters from now? (2) Is large deal TCV growing — and what does that say about revenue 4–6 quarters from now? (3) Is EBIT margin expanding or contracting — and is it driven by operations or currency? (4) What is the hedge ratio — is this year's margin protected from currency volatility?

These four questions, answered with public data from earnings calls and Screener.in, give you a more accurate picture of an IT company's near-term trajectory than most analyst research reports — because they focus on leading indicators (attrition, TCV) rather than lagging ones (quarterly revenue, annual margin).

The attrition lead-lag is the most actionable: when LTM attrition rises from 14% to 22% in two quarters, model 200bps margin compression in the next two quarters. When it falls from 22% to 14%, model 200bps margin expansion. This single signal, tracked consistently, would have told you to reduce IT exposure in Q2 FY22 and rebuild it in Q2 FY24 — capturing the full cycle.
