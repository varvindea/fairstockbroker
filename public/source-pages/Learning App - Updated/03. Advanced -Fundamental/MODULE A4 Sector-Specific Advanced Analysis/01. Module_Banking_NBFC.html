01 — Key Learning Outcome

Why Bank Analysis Needs a Completely Different Framework

🏦
Your Core Takeaway
A bank borrows money from depositors at low rates and lends it to borrowers at higher rates. The difference is its "profit margin" — called the Net Interest Margin (NIM). But unlike a factory, a bank's biggest risk is not competition or raw material prices — it is whether its borrowers repay. Bad loans (NPAs) can wipe out years of profits in a single year. This is why bank analysis uses completely different metrics from regular company analysis.
Think of a bank as a water tank system. CASA deposits (Current Account & Savings Account) are cheap water flowing in at the bottom. Fixed Deposits are more expensive water. The bank pipes this water out as loans to earn interest. The difference between what it costs to bring water in and what it earns by piping it out is the Net Interest Margin.

Now imagine some pipes develop leaks — borrowers who stop repaying. These are NPAs (Non-Performing Assets). If the bank doesn't seal these leaks quickly (through provisions = money set aside as a buffer), the leaks grow larger. The Provision Coverage Ratio (PCR) tells you what percentage of bad loans the bank has already provisioned for. High PCR = well-prepared. Low PCR = exposed.

Finally, every bank must keep its own money (capital) as a safety cushion — so even if borrowers default, the bank can absorb the losses without failing. This cushion is measured as Tier 1 Capital / Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR). The higher the Tier 1, the safer the bank. RBI requires a minimum 9% CAR; strong banks maintain 15–20%.

Master five numbers: NIM (profitability of lending), GNPA (gross bad loans as % of total loans), PCR (coverage against bad loans), CASA (quality of deposit franchise), and Tier 1 Capital (safety buffer). These five define whether a bank is a compounding machine or a time bomb.
📊 NIM = lending profit margin 🚨 GNPA = bad loan %, lower = better ✅ PCR >70% = well-provisioned 💰 CASA = cheap deposit franchise
02 — The Five Core Banking Metrics Explained

Five Numbers That Define Every Bank — With Plain-Language Explanations

Before reading a bank's P&L, understand these five metrics. Every good bank has specific thresholds for each. Every stressed bank violates at least two of them — usually GNPA and PCR together.

The bank as a water system: CASA deposits flow in cheaply, Fixed Deposits cost more. Loans flow out at higher rates. NIM is the price difference kept as profit. NPAs are the leaks. PCR is the repair budget pre-set aside. Tier 1 Capital is the emergency fund if the leaks overwhelm the repair budget. A strong bank maximises NIM, minimises GNPA, maintains high PCR, grows CASA, and holds surplus Tier 1.

The Five Core Metrics — Explained for Non-Finance Readers
📈
NIM
Net Interest Margin
Core Profitability
NIM is the most important measure of how profitably a bank lends. It equals: (Interest Earned on Loans − Interest Paid on Deposits) ÷ Average Earning Assets. Think of it as the bank's gross profit margin — the spread between what it charges borrowers and what it pays depositors.

A bank with high CASA (cheap deposits) automatically has a lower cost of funds → higher NIM. A bank lending to higher-risk segments (MSME, unsecured personal loans) charges higher rates → higher NIM, but more NPAs. The trade-off between NIM and asset quality is the central tension in bank analysis.
3.5–5%✅ Strong NIM
<2.5%🚨 Thin Margin
⚠️
GNPA
Gross Non-Performing Asset Ratio
Loan Book Quality
GNPA % is the percentage of total loans where borrowers have stopped repaying for 90+ days. A loan becomes an NPA after three missed EMIs. GNPA measures the scale of the problem before any provisions are applied. This is the most important single metric for assessing a bank's asset quality.

Net NPA = GNPA minus provisions already set aside. A bank with GNPA 5% but PCR 80% has Net NPA of only 1% — genuinely well-covered. A bank with GNPA 5% and PCR 40% has Net NPA 3% — dangerously exposed. Always read GNPA and PCR together.
<2%✅ Healthy Book
>5%🚨 Stressed Book
🛡️
PCR
Provision Coverage Ratio
Loss Preparedness
PCR measures how much of the Gross NPAs have already been provisioned (set aside as a buffer). PCR = Provisions Held ÷ Gross NPAs × 100. A PCR of 80% means for every ₹100 of bad loans, the bank has already set aside ₹80 as a buffer.

High PCR is a quality indicator — it shows management has been conservative and honest about losses. Banks with low PCR may have been under-provisioning — deferring recognition of losses and boosting short-term profitability at the cost of balance sheet honesty. RBI's minimum is 70% (technical write-offs included). Best-in-class banks maintain 80%+.
>75%✅ Well Covered
<50%🚨 Exposed
💰
CASA
Current & Savings Account Ratio
Funding Quality
CASA % is the share of total deposits that sit in Current Accounts (0% interest — float money from businesses) and Savings Accounts (3–3.5% interest — retail deposits). These are the cheapest sources of funding for a bank. The higher the CASA %, the lower the overall cost of deposits.

CASA is also a franchise indicator. Customers maintain current and savings accounts with banks they trust and use daily. High CASA = large retail network + brand trust + sticky low-cost deposits. Banks with 40%+ CASA ratios have structural cost advantages that are very hard for competitors to replicate.
>40%✅ Strong Franchise
<25%🚨 High Cost Base
🏛️
Tier 1
Tier 1 Capital / CAR
Safety Cushion
Tier 1 Capital Ratio is the bank's own equity (and retained earnings) as a percentage of its risk-weighted assets. It measures how much loss a bank can absorb before depositors' money is at risk. Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR) includes Tier 2 capital (subordinated debt, provisions).

RBI mandates minimum CAR of 9% (Tier 1 at 7%). Strong banks maintain 15–20%. The 2008 global financial crisis happened partly because many banks had thin capital ratios — losses wiped out equity quickly. High Tier 1 = bank can withstand NPA shocks without external capital infusion (dilution). Low Tier 1 = one NPA cycle away from distress.
>14%✅ Well Capitalised
<10%🚨 Near Minimum
💹
ROA/ROE
Return on Assets / Equity
Profitability
ROA (Net Profit ÷ Average Total Assets) measures efficiency — how much profit a bank generates per rupee of assets managed. Banks are highly leveraged (assets 10–20× equity), so even 1.5% ROA translates to 15–20% ROE. ROA is the better comparator across banks because it removes leverage effects.

ROE (Net Profit ÷ Average Equity) reflects returns to shareholders. A bank with 20% ROE and 15% Tier 1 capital is genuinely creating value. A bank with 20% ROE but 8% Tier 1 is taking excessive risk to generate returns — leverage is doing the work, not quality.
ROA>1.5%✅ Efficient
ROA<0.5%🚨 Struggling

🚨
GNPA understated for 3+ years — divergence between reported and RBI-assessed NPAs was the forensic signal
RBI's annual inspection of Yes Bank (SPARC — Supervisory Programme for Assessment of Risk and Capital) consistently found higher NPAs than what Yes Bank reported. In FY2019, Yes Bank reported GNPA of 2.09%. RBI's inspection (results always disclosed with a lag) found divergence of ₹3,277 crore — meaning NPAs should have been approximately ₹5,600 crore more than reported. The RBI divergence data is public — published annually in the RBI Annual Report and in the SEBI disclosures of the bank. Investors tracking the divergence trend (which grew each year from FY2016 to FY2019) had a clear forensic signal that NPA classification was being manipulated.
🚨
CASA declining while expensive wholesale deposits grew — liquidity risk building visibly
Yes Bank's CASA ratio declined from 40%+ (FY2016) to 26% (FY2020) as the bank grew its wholesale funding — institutional deposits and borrowings — to fuel rapid loan growth. Wholesale deposits are "hot money" — they flee at the first sign of distress. When Yes Bank's NPA problems became public, wholesale depositors withdrew immediately, creating a bank run. A bank where CASA is consistently declining and wholesale funding is growing is building a structural liquidity risk. HDFC Bank's CASA is stable at 38–46%; Yes Bank's went from 40% to 26% in four years — the trend was unmistakable and alarming.
🚨
AT1 (Additional Tier 1) bond write-down — ₹3,780 crore of investor wealth wiped out
During the Yes Bank reconstruction (March 2020), RBI and the government wrote down ₹3,780 crore of Yes Bank's AT1 (Additional Tier 1) bonds to zero. AT1 bonds are quasi-equity instruments that absorb losses before equity holders when a bank fails. Many retail investors held these bonds believing them to be safe "bank bonds." The write-down was the consequence of insufficient Tier 1 Capital and unrecognised NPAs combining in a stress event. The lesson: when a bank's Tier 1 Capital falls toward the RBI minimum AND GNPA is understated, AT1 bonds face existential risk even before equity does.
🚨 The Yes Bank forensic checklist: Three specific checks would have flagged Yes Bank 2 years before RBI acted: (1) RBI SPARC divergence data — annually published, showed growing gap between reported and inspected NPAs; (2) CASA declining from 40% to 26% in 4 years — wholesale funding dependence growing; (3) Tier 1 Capital trending toward 9–10% — minimal buffer. All three were public data. Track all three for any bank where NIM looks too good relative to sector peers.

When a bank's NPAs generate credit losses that exceed its equity (retained earnings + paid-up capital), Tier 1 Capital turns negative. 🚨 The LVB GNPA trajectory lesson: GNPA rising from 2% to 25% over 5 years is not sudden — it's visible every year. The forensic rule: GNPA rising more than 1 percentage point per year for 3 consecutive years = structural credit problem, not a one-time shock. Exit or avoid when GNPA crosses 8% with Tier 1 below 11% — the provision requirement at that GNPA level will consume multiple years of profits even with PCR of 70%.

Reported NIM masked fraud through inflated loan book — Bandra Book fake accounts
DHFL's NIM appeared healthy at 2.5–3% on reported AUM. But the loan book included thousands of fictitious borrowers ("Bandra Book" — fake retail accounts used to divert ₹31,000+ crore to promoter-connected entities). The NIM was calculated on a fictitious denominator. When investigators discovered the fake accounts, the effective AUM was materially lower, and the NIM picture changed completely. For investors: when any NBFC's AUM grows significantly faster than its branch/distribution count, or when disbursals to stated geographies don't match economic activity in those areas — the loan book may contain fictitious or inflated entries.
🚨 The NBFC ALM lesson: Any NBFC funding long-duration assets (housing, infrastructure, equipment) with short-duration liabilities (commercial paper, short-term NCDs) has structural ALM risk that becomes existential in a liquidity freeze. The investor check: CP + Short-term NCDs as % of total borrowings. If above 30% for a long-duration lender — the ALM risk is material. DHFL's short-term borrowings were over 40% of total liabilities — visible in every quarterly balance sheet for 3 years before collapse.

GNPA 12% + PCR <55% = the most dangerous combination in banking
When PNB's Nirav Modi fraud was discovered, the bank was already carrying 12.1% GNPA with a PCR of approximately 55% — meaning 45% of its gross NPAs had no provision cover. This combination (high GNPA + low PCR) meant that: (1) the bank was already absorbing massive credit losses on a large NPA base, (2) it had insufficient buffers to absorb further losses, and (3) the ₹13,500 crore LoU fraud exposure added to an already stressed Tier 1 Capital. The GNPA + PCR combination was visible in every quarterly result for 8 quarters before the fraud discovery. Investors who tracked both metrics together could see the vulnerability.
🚨
Letters of Undertaking off-balance sheet — the fraud bypass that GNPA metrics couldn't capture
The Nirav Modi fraud used Letters of Undertaking (LoUs) — essentially guarantees issued by PNB Brady House branch to foreign banks, allowing Nirav Modi's companies to borrow from those banks with PNB's guarantee. These LoUs were not recorded in PNB's SWIFT messaging system (deliberately bypassed) and therefore didn't appear as contingent liabilities in PNB's balance sheet. No standard banking metric captures off-balance sheet fraud. However, the pre-existing 12% GNPA was a clear indicator of PNB's broader credit culture — a bank with strong internal controls and credit discipline doesn't arrive at 12% GNPA. Weak credit culture and weak internal controls are correlated.
🚨 The PNB combined risk lesson: GNPA above 8% + PCR below 60% in any bank deserves immediate exit or avoidance. The capital consumption of provisioning up to 70%+ coverage on 12% GNPA is so large that it consumes multiple years of profit and compresses NIM. PNB needed to provision approximately ₹40,000+ crore to bring PCR to 75% on its GNPA base — an amount that would have required government capital infusion to maintain Tier 1. The combination was always visible in quarterly disclosures.

Accrued interest inflation — NIM looked healthy while actual cash collection collapsed
Under Indian accounting standards (Ind AS), interest on loans continues to accrue on the income statement even when the borrower hasn't paid — as long as the loan hasn't been classified as NPA (which happens after 90 days of non-payment). During COVID moratoriums (April–August 2020) and the subsequent recovery period, many MFI/NBFC borrowers stopped paying. Interest accrued on these loans inflated reported NIM and PAT. But no actual cash was being received. The difference between reported interest income and cash collected (visible in the cash flow statement) was the forensic signal: revenue looked fine, but CFO/PAT was far below 1.0×, revealing the accrual inflation.
🚨
PCR insufficient for the scale of NPA shock — structural undercapitalization of credit risk
Multiple small finance banks and MFI-focused NBFCs entered COVID with PCR of 50–60% — adequate for normal NPA levels but completely insufficient when GNPA spiked to 8–12%. Provisioning 70% of 12% GNPA requires setting aside 8.4% of the loan book as provisions — a massive immediate hit to capital. Institutions with PCR 50–60% at the start of the shock had to rapidly increase provisions, collapsing PAT and, in some cases, requiring fresh equity capital at distress valuations. The investors who tracked PCR and maintained a rule of "minimum 70% PCR at any point" would have avoided these institutions entering the COVID period.
⚠️ The MFI-NBFC cyclical risk lesson: Microfinance and small-ticket unsecured lending generates the highest NIM in banking but concentrates credit risk in the most vulnerable borrowers. The forensic rule: in any MFI/SFB/NBFC-MFI, monitor collection efficiency monthly (published in MFIN data and investor presentations). When collection efficiency falls below 95%, begin reducing exposure. At 90% or below, the upcoming GNPA spike is mathematically inevitable. The NIM premium only pays off if PCR is maintained above 70% and capital is sufficient to absorb a 10%+ GNPA scenario.

Five fast checks on Screener.in and investor presentations that give you the complete banking quality picture in under 8 minutes. Run these before analysing any bank or NBFC's valuation.

Trick #2 — 90 Seconds · GNPA + PCR Together
The "Net NPA Calculation" — The Number That Actually Matters
90 seconds
Never look at GNPA alone. Always calculate Net NPA = GNPA × (1 − PCR%). This is the actual uncovered bad loan exposure as a percentage of the loan book — the number that will hit equity if those loans are never recovered.
✅ Good Combination
GNPA 5% + PCR 80% = Net NPA 1%
Gross problem exists but well covered. Bank has already absorbed most of the loss. Real exposure is only 1% of loan book. Manageable.
🚨 Dangerous Combination
GNPA 5% + PCR 40% = Net NPA 3%
Three rupees of every hundred loaned out is neither being repaid nor provisioned. This 3% must come from future profits or equity. At GNPA 12% + PCR 50% = Net NPA 6% = bank may need recapitalisation.

Trick #3 — 2 Minutes · GNPA 5-Year Trend
The "GNPA Trend Line" — Direction Matters More Than the Level
2 minutes
Screener Ratios tab → GNPA % → 5-year history. Plot the five numbers mentally. Is GNPA improving (declining each year) or deteriorating (rising each year)? The direction is more important than the current level — a bank at 5% GNPA but declining is usually safer than a bank at 2% GNPA but rising.
📈
GNPA: 8% → 6% → 4% → 3% → 2% (5 years, declining)
Even though GNPA started high, the improving trajectory shows recovery. Management is resolving NPAs faster than new ones form. Improving asset quality + growing loan book = quality improvement cycle. SBI's turnaround is this pattern.
📉
GNPA: 1.5% → 2% → 3% → 5% → 8% (5 years, rising)
Even starting from a low base, rising GNPA is a structural alarm. Each year the problem deepens. Yes Bank: 0.8% → 1.3% → 2.1% → 3.2% → 16% (actual). The early years looked manageable. They weren't.
💧
Trick #4 — 60 Seconds · CASA Trend
The "CASA Trend" — Cheap Deposits Are the Competitive Moat. Is It Growing or Eroding?
60 seconds
Screener → Ratios → CASA % (or quarterly investor presentation). Track 5 years. CASA growing = franchise strength and cost of funds improving. CASA declining = bank becoming more dependent on expensive wholesale/term deposits → NIM pressure → margin compression → valuation risk.

One additional check for NBFCs (which have no CASA): look at the CP (commercial paper) + short-term NCD % of total borrowings. Above 30% for a long-duration lender = structural ALM risk. DHFL example: CP + short NCD was 40%+ of borrowings in 2018.
✅ CASA Growing
More customers trusting the bank with daily banking → franchise expansion → cost of deposits improving → NIM protection. Every 1% CASA increase ≈ 8–10 bps NIM improvement at scale.
🚨 CASA Declining
Customers withdrawing day-to-day deposits → franchise erosion → bank must offer higher term deposit rates → NIM compression → PAT growth slows. Yes Bank CASA fell from 40% to 26% in 4 years.

The One-Line Bank Analysis Rule
GNPA × (1 − PCR%) = Net NPA. This Single Number — Trended Over 5 Years — Tells You Everything About a Bank's Credit Culture.
Banking is a deceptively simple business: borrow cheap, lend dear, collect reliably. The complexity comes from the fact that lending is inherently risky — any loan can become an NPA, and large NPAs can wipe out years of interest income in a single provisioning cycle.

The five metrics in this chapter — NIM, GNPA, PCR, CASA, Tier 1 Capital — are not five separate analyses. They are five windows into a single question: Does this bank lend at sustainable rates, to borrowers who can repay, with enough capital to absorb the inevitable mistakes?

HDFC Bank answers "yes" to all five. Yes Bank in FY2019 answered "apparently yes" to NIM and "no" to everything else — but the NIM was inflated by accrued interest on loans that were actually bad. SBI in FY2018 answered "no" to GNPA but "yes" to everything else — and the trend showed it was fixing the problem. LVB answered "no" to everything and was eventually merged.

Five metrics. Eight minutes. Free data. The difference between investing in a compounding machine and investing in a time bomb depends entirely on whether you ran these checks before buying.
Reference Glossary

Banking & NBFC Glossary

Every banking and NBFC metric used in this chapter — defined in plain language. Refer back whenever you encounter these terms in quarterly investor presentations, RBI reports, or annual disclosures.

Term What It Means in Simple Words Key Point
NIM — Net Interest Margin The spread between what a bank earns on its loans and what it pays on its deposits, expressed as a percentage of earning assets. NIM = (Interest Earned − Interest Paid) ÷ Average Earning Assets. Think of it as the bank's gross profit margin — the difference between the cost of money and the price it lends at. A bank with CASA-dominated funding earns a higher NIM because its cost of deposits is lower. Core profitability
GNPA — Gross Non-Performing Asset The percentage of total loans where borrowers have not paid for 90+ days (three missed EMIs). GNPA is the gross problem before any provisions are deducted. It is the primary indicator of a bank's credit quality and underwriting discipline. Below 2% = healthy; above 5% = stressed; above 10% = distressed. Always read GNPA alongside PCR — a high GNPA with high PCR is far safer than a high GNPA with low PCR. Loan quality signal
Net NPA Gross NPA minus the provisions already set aside against those bad loans. Net NPA = GNPA × (1 − PCR%). This is the actual uncovered bad loan exposure that will hit equity if those loans are never recovered. Net NPA below 1% is excellent; above 3% signals capital strain. Always calculate Net NPA before drawing any conclusions from GNPA alone. Real exposure metric
PCR — Provision Coverage Ratio The percentage of Gross NPAs already covered by provisions (money set aside as a buffer). PCR = Provisions Held ÷ Gross NPAs × 100. A PCR of 80% means for every ₹100 of bad loans, ₹80 has already been set aside. High PCR signals that management has been honest and conservative about credit losses. RBI's minimum guideline is 70% (including technical write-offs). Best-in-class banks maintain 80–90%+. Min 70% ideal
CASA — Current & Savings Account Ratio The share of total deposits sitting in Current Accounts (0% interest — business float money) and Savings Accounts (3–3.5% interest — retail daily deposits). CASA represents the cheapest source of funding. A high CASA ratio (above 40%) means the bank pays very little for a large portion of its deposits — directly improving NIM. CASA is also a franchise moat indicator: customers maintain these accounts with banks they trust for daily transactions. Cheap funding moat
Tier 1 Capital / CAR Tier 1 Capital is the bank's own equity and retained earnings as a percentage of risk-weighted assets — the safety cushion that absorbs losses before depositors' money is at risk. Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR) includes Tier 2 capital (subordinated debt + provisions). RBI mandates minimum CAR of 9% and Tier 1 of 7%. Well-capitalised banks maintain Tier 1 of 14–20%+. High Tier 1 = freedom to grow loans and absorb NPA shocks without diluting equity. Safety cushion
ROA — Return on Assets Net Profit ÷ Average Total Assets. Measures how efficiently a bank generates profit from each rupee of assets it manages. Banks are highly leveraged (assets 10–20× equity), so even 1.5% ROA translates to 15–20% ROE. ROA is the better cross-bank comparator because it removes the leverage effect. Above 1.5% = top-quartile; above 1% = acceptable; below 0.5% = struggling. Use ROA alongside NIM to assess the full profitability picture. Best profitability proxy
NII — Net Interest Income Interest Earned on Loans minus Interest Paid on Deposits. NII is the core banking revenue — the absolute rupee amount of the spread the bank earns. NII growing at 15%+ while NIM remains stable signals both volume (loan book growing) and quality (spread maintained) growth. NII declining despite rising loan book = NIM compression. Watch for NII vs PAT divergence — if PAT grows slower than NII, provisions are increasing (potential NPA buildup). Core banking revenue
Cost-to-Income Ratio Operating Expenses ÷ Net Revenue (NII + Other Income). Measures operational efficiency — what percentage of income is consumed by running costs (salaries, branches, technology, compliance). Below 40% = highly efficient (HDFC Bank ~40%). Above 60% = bloated cost structure. A declining Cost-to-Income ratio means the bank is scaling faster than costs — operational leverage kicking in. Rising ratio despite revenue growth = structural inefficiency. Below 40% = efficient
Provisions & Contingencies Money set aside in the P&L as a buffer against expected future credit losses. Provisions reduce PAT in the current year to protect the balance sheet from NPA losses in future years. Rising provisions in proportion to loan growth = healthy conservatism. Provisions surging abnormally relative to loan growth = management recognising hidden NPAs. Provisions falling while NPAs are rising = management under-provisioning to protect near-term PAT — a red flag. NPA buffer
AUM — Assets Under Management For NBFCs, AUM is the total loan book outstanding — the amount of money lent to customers that is yet to be repaid. It is the NBFC equivalent of a bank's "loan book." AUM growth rate is the headline volume metric. A strong NBFC grows AUM at 20–30% CAGR while maintaining Stage 3 (NPA equivalent) below 2%. AUM growth without credit quality = dangerous growth; AUM growth with stable Stage 3 = compounding franchise value. NBFC loan book
Stage 3 Assets (Ind AS 109) Under the Expected Credit Loss (ECL) model of Ind AS 109, loans are classified into three stages. Stage 1: performing loans. Stage 2: significant increase in credit risk. Stage 3: credit-impaired — equivalent to NPA under RBI's old framework. Stage 3 % is the NPA equivalent for NBFCs reporting under Ind AS. Use Stage 3 for NBFCs the same way you use GNPA for banks. Bajaj Finance maintains Stage 3 below 1.1% — exceptional for consumer lending. NBFC NPA equivalent
Collection Efficiency For NBFCs, the percentage of scheduled repayments (EMIs due) that are actually collected in a given month. Collection efficiency of 99%+ means 99 paise of every ₹1 due is being paid — the NBFC equivalent of a bank's CFO/PAT quality test. When collection efficiency falls below 95%, upcoming GNPA spikes are mathematically inevitable. Bajaj Finance publishes monthly collection efficiency — it is the earliest NPA warning signal available for any NBFC. Early NPA warning
ALM — Asset-Liability Mismatch When a bank or NBFC funds long-duration assets (20-year home loans) with short-duration liabilities (3-month commercial paper). This creates liquidity risk — if short-term lenders refuse to roll over borrowings, the institution cannot meet obligations even if its long-term assets are performing. DHFL's collapse was an ALM mismatch crisis — long housing loans funded by short-term NCDs. NBFCs are more vulnerable to ALM risk than banks because they lack stable deposit funding. Liquidity risk
AT1 Bonds — Additional Tier 1 Perpetual bonds issued by banks that absorb losses before equity when a bank faces financial distress. AT1 bonds are classified as Tier 1 capital for regulatory purposes. They pay a higher coupon than regular bonds because they carry additional risk — they can be written down to zero by the regulator if the bank's capital falls below a trigger level. Yes Bank's ₹3,780 Cr AT1 write-down in 2020 demonstrated that these are not "safe bonds" when a bank is in distress. Quasi-equity risk
SPARC — RBI Supervisory Assessment Supervisory Programme for Assessment of Risk and Capital — RBI's annual internal inspection of every bank. SPARC results include the RBI-assessed NPA level, which is often higher than what the bank reports. The "divergence" between reported NPAs and RBI-assessed NPAs is disclosed in the bank's annual report and RBI's annual publication. Yes Bank's SPARC divergence grew from ₹500 Cr to ₹3,277 Cr over 4 years before its collapse — visible in public disclosures every year. NPA divergence signal
ECL — Expected Credit Loss Under Ind AS 109 (adopted 2019), banks and NBFCs must estimate and provision for credit losses based on probability-weighted future scenarios — not just actual defaults. ECL provisioning is more forward-looking than the old RBI provisioning norms. Conservative ECL assumptions (higher macro stress scenarios, larger management overlays) lead to higher provisions and lower near-term PAT — but better balance sheet quality. Bajaj Finance's conservative ECL methodology is a key quality indicator. Forward-looking buffer
IBC — Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code India's 2016 law that allows creditors to initiate insolvency proceedings against defaulting borrowers. IBC was transformational for PSU banks — it gave them a time-bound mechanism (180–270 days) to resolve large corporate NPAs. SBI's dramatic GNPA reduction from 10.91% to 2% between FY2018 and FY2025 was largely driven by IBC resolutions of large steel, power, and telecom sector NPAs. IBC resolutions often involve significant haircuts (losses on loans) — but allow clean-up of the NPA book. NPA resolution tool
Bancassurance / Fee Income Non-interest income earned by banks through selling third-party products (insurance, mutual funds) and services (transaction fees, forex, investment banking). Fee income is high-quality because it doesn't require deploying capital at credit risk. Banks with growing fee income (15–25% of total revenue) have more diversified, capital-light earnings. "Other Income" in the bank P&L contains fee income, trading gains, and treasury profits — decompose it carefully as trading gains are volatile and not recurring. Capital-light income
Where to find these metrics: NIM, ROA, Cost-to-Income → Screener.in Ratios tab. GNPA, PCR, Net NPA, Tier 1, CASA → Quarterly investor presentation (search "[Bank Name] Q4 Investor Presentation" on BSE). RBI divergence data → Bank annual report → "Divergence in Asset Classification" note. Stage 3, Collection Efficiency, AUM → NBFC quarterly results and investor decks. ECL assumptions → Annual report → Notes to accounts → ECL methodology.
