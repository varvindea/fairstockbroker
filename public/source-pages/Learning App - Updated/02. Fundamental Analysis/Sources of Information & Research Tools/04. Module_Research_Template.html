01 — Key Learning Outcome

Why Write a Research Note? Because Thinking Without Writing Isn't Thinking

✍️
Your Core Takeaway
A stock research note is a structured document where you write down everything you know about a company — its business, financials, management, risks, valuation — and conclude with a clear investment decision. Writing forces clarity that reading alone cannot provide.
Think about the last time you told someone about a movie you watched versus actually writing a review. When you write, you're forced to organise your thoughts, confront gaps in your knowledge, and commit to a position. The same happens with stocks. You can read about Nestle for hours and feel like you understand it. But the moment you start writing "Why should I buy Nestle at ₹2,200?", gaps appear instantly — you realise you don't know what the P/E was in 2020, or what percentage of revenue comes from Maggi, or what the key risk is to their distribution model.

Professional equity analysts at HDFC Securities, Motilal Oswal, or Kotak write a formal note for every stock they cover — typically 20–40 pages. You don't need 40 pages. A disciplined 1–2 page research note on each stock you own or watch is sufficient. This template gives you the exact 7 sections every good research note must contain, explained step by step, with real examples from Nestle, Polycab, Sun Pharma, Maruti, and HFCL — showing how the same template reveals very different investment stories.

02 — The 7-Section Research Template

Every Section, Explained Step by Step

This template mirrors what professional equity analysts at top brokerages use — simplified to what a retail investor can complete in 2–3 hours using free data from Screener.in and Moneycontrol.

A complete stock research note has 7 sections: Business overview, Financial quality, Management quality, Competitive moat, Bulls vs Bears, Valuation, and Verdict. Each section has a clear data source and a specific question to answer. Writing all 7 forces you to confront every important aspect before committing capital.

1
Business Overview — What Does This Company Actually Do?
5 minutes · Screener.in company page + Wikipedia
Foundation
Write 3–5 sentences describing the company's core business — as if explaining it to someone who has never heard of the company. What does it sell? Who are its customers? Where does it operate? What percentage of revenue comes from its main product? This section forces you to verify whether you truly understand the business before investing.

Many investors skip this section because they think they know the business. Don't. Nestle India sells packaged food and beverages — Maggi (30% of revenue), KitKat, Nescafe, Munch, Milkmaid — through 4+ million retail outlets across India. It sources dairy from 150,000+ farmers in Maharashtra and Himachal. Understanding this precise picture reveals why rural distribution depth is a moat and why dairy price cycles affect margins.
Company Name & NSE Code
"Nestle India Ltd · NESTLEIND · Food Manufacturing"
Core Products / Services
"Maggi (30%), Beverages (20%), Chocolates (18%), Dairy (15%)"
Revenue Model
"B2C consumer goods · Direct + distributor + e-commerce"
Market Position
"#1 in instant noodles · #2 in chocolates · Strong in premium"
✍️ The 3-sentence test: If you cannot describe this company's business in 3 sentences clearly, you don't know the business well enough to invest. Keep researching until you can.
2
Financial Quality Check — Is the Business Financially Healthy?
20 minutes · Screener.in → 10-year financials · All 5 metrics required
Core Analysis
Use Screener.in to pull 5-year averages of these five numbers. If any of them is significantly below the thresholds listed, note it — you need an explanation before investing. Don't use current-year numbers alone; 5-year averages reveal whether quality is structural or cyclical. Record both the 5-year average AND the most recent year — divergence between the two is often the most important signal.
Revenue CAGR (5 yr)
"15–20% = excellent · 10–15% = good · <8% = investigate growth"
Return on Equity (avg)
">20% = excellent · 12–20% = good · <12% = capital inefficient"
Debt / Equity ratio
"0–0.5× = excellent · 0.5–1× = acceptable · >2× = red flag"
CFO / PAT ratio (avg)
">0.9× = elite · 0.7–0.9× = good · <0.6× = earnings quality concern"
Operating Margin (OPM)
">20% = high quality · 10–20% = moderate · <8% = watch"
FCF trend (5 yr)
"Positive and growing every year = ✅ · Negative 2+ years = ⚠️"
✅ If all 5 metrics pass, this is a financially healthy business. Write a one-line summary: "Nestle India: ROE 98%, D/E 0×, CFO/PAT 1.11×, OPM 24%, Revenue CAGR 14% — exceptional across all five dimensions."
3
Management Quality — Who Is Running This Company?
15 minutes · Annual Report · Concall transcripts · Moneycontrol interviews
Qualitative
Numbers tell you what happened. Management tells you why it happened and what will happen next. Evaluate management on three dimensions: (1) Capital allocation — do they pay out excess cash as dividends/buybacks, or do they waste it on value-destroying acquisitions? (2) Guidance accuracy — do they deliver on what they promise in concalls? Check: did Q1 guidance come true by Q4? (3) Disclosure quality — do they explain problems honestly, or do they use vague language to hide issues?

Read at least one annual report and two concall transcripts before writing this section. Pay attention to how management describes bad news — honest managers say "we made an error in our pricing strategy" while poor communicators say "headwinds in the external environment impacted our results."
CEO / MD Name & Tenure
"Name · Years in role · Background (operator vs finance vs promoter)"
Capital Allocation Score
"Dividends growing with profits? Buybacks at fair prices? No vanity acquisitions?"
Guidance Track Record
"Met FY24 guidance? FY23? FY22? Consistent = trustworthy management"
Promoter Holding Trend
"Stable or rising = aligned · Falling significantly = warning signal"
🚨 Red flag statements in concalls: "We are evaluating synergies from our recent acquisition" (no tangible benefit yet), "The market is not mature for our products" (blaming customers for poor sales), "We expect meaningful growth in H2" — same statement 3 years in a row.
4
Competitive Moat — Why Won't a Competitor Steal This Business?
10 minutes · Your own analysis · No data source required — reasoning only
Most Important
The moat section is the most important and the hardest — it requires judgment, not data. A moat is anything that protects a company's profitability from competition. Write 3–5 sentences explaining exactly what would prevent a new well-funded competitor from taking 30% of this company's market share within 5 years. If you cannot articulate a clear answer, the company may not have a durable moat.

Five types of moats to look for: (1) Brand — Nestle's Maggi commands 60%+ market share despite multiple brand crises because mothers trust it. (2) Cost advantage — scale economies that let incumbents produce cheaper. (3) Switching costs — IT software that clients can't easily migrate away from. (4) Network effects — a marketplace with more buyers attracts more sellers. (5) Regulatory — pharma patents or telecom spectrum licenses block competition.
Primary Moat Type
"Brand / Cost / Switching / Network / Regulatory — pick the strongest one"
Moat Strength (1–5)
"5 = impenetrable · 3 = solid but attackable · 1 = no real moat visible"
Evidence of Moat
"Consistent ROE>20% for 10 years = strong evidence of durable moat"
Moat Threat
"What could destroy this moat? Honest answer required here."
✅ Moat test: If Nestle raises Maggi price 15%, do customers switch to Top Ramen? (Answer: rarely, because Maggi is a habit, not a commodity.) If Asian Paints raises price 8%, do painters switch brands? (Answer: occasionally, but painters prefer consistency.) These real reactions confirm the moat.
5
Bulls vs Bears — What Are the Best and Worst Case Scenarios?
15 minutes · Your judgment + analyst reports + Moneycontrol news
Balanced View
This is the most honest section — and most investors skip it because they only want to confirm what they already believe. Forcing yourself to write 3 strong bear cases about a stock you like is uncomfortable but essential. The bears might be right. If you cannot write 3 compelling bear arguments, you don't understand the risks well enough to size a position confidently.

A good bulls vs bears section reads like a debate — not a sales pitch. The bull case should be genuinely exciting. The bear case should be genuinely scary. If your bear case sounds like "valuation is a little stretched" and nothing else, you haven't thought deeply enough about the risks. What could make this stock fall 40% from current price? Write it down.
Bull Case #1 (strongest)
"The clearest reason this stock could double in 3 years"
Bull Case #2 & #3
"Additional catalysts: new product, geographic expansion, margin improvement"
Bear Case #1 (most dangerous)
"The scenario that could cause a 30–50% drawdown from here"
Bear Case #2 & #3
"Competition, regulation, margin compression, demand slowdown"
📊 Probability weighting: After listing all bulls and bears, assign rough probabilities. "Bull case probability: 60%. Base case: 30%. Bear case: 10%." This forces you to think about conviction, not just direction. Only invest if expected value across scenarios is positive.
6
Valuation — At What Price Is This Business Worth Buying?
20 minutes · Screener.in PE history · Trendlyne · Simple FCF math
Price Discipline
Valuation is not about predicting where the stock will go tomorrow. It's about answering: "At the current price, am I paying a fair price for the earnings power and growth I'm getting?" Three simple methods suitable for retail investors:

(1) PE vs History: Compare current PE to 5-year median PE on Trendlyne/Screener. If current PE is 20% above median, the stock is pricing in optimistic expectations — higher margin of safety needed. (2) FCF Yield: FCF ÷ Market Cap. Above 4% = reasonably valued. Below 2% = expensive. (3) Price to Fair Value estimate: Your estimate of fair intrinsic value vs current price = margin of safety.
Current PE vs 5-yr Median PE
"Current: XX× · Median: XX× · Premium/Discount: +/-XX%"
FCF Yield
"FCF ₹XXX Cr ÷ Mkt Cap ₹XXXX Cr = X.X% — target >3–4%"
Fair Value Estimate
"Bull: ₹XXXX · Base: ₹XXXX · Bear: ₹XXXX"
Margin of Safety
"Buy zone: 20–30% below base fair value estimate for quality stocks"
✅ Simple rule: Avoid calculating precise target prices with 3 decimal points. The uncertainty is too high. Instead, define a "zone": "This stock is fairly valued at 50–60× PE. Currently at 70×, it's trading in the upper range. I'll watch for a correction to the 55–60× zone before adding."
7
Investment Verdict — The Clear, Committed Decision
5 minutes · Your judgment · No data source needed — just honesty
Final Answer
The verdict section must be decisive. Four possible verdicts — and each must come with a specific condition that would make you change your mind:

BUY: "I am buying X shares at current price because [specific reason]. I will sell if [specific trigger]."
WATCH: "This is a great business but too expensive. I will buy if price falls to ₹XXXX or if the next quarterly result shows EPS growth above 20%."
HOLD: "I already own this. I won't add at current prices but won't sell unless [specific trigger]."
AVOID: "The business quality is insufficient because [specific reason]. I will reconsider only if [specific condition changes]."

Also write your "review trigger" — the next event that will make you re-evaluate this note (quarterly result date, SEBI announcement, new competitor launch).
Verdict (choose one)
"BUY / WATCH / HOLD / AVOID — and the specific reason in one sentence"
Position Size Conviction
"High conviction (4–5% of portfolio) · Medium (2–3%) · Low (1–2%)"
Price Action Plan
"Buy now at ₹XXX / Wait for ₹XXX / Add on dip to ₹XXX"
Review Trigger & Date
"Review on: Next quarterly result [Date] or if stock falls >20%"
✅ The most powerful sentence: "I will sell this stock if _______________." Fill this in now, before you own it. Pre-committing to a sell trigger removes emotional attachment and prevents holding a stock through a fundamental deterioration because you "believe in it too much."

The Master Research Note Rule
Write the Note Before You Check the Price — Not After
The most common mistake: investors see a stock rising 20% and THEN write a research note to justify buying it. This is confirmation bias dressed as analysis. The correct sequence is: (1) Identify the stock through a screen. (2) Write the research note — all 7 sections — without knowing the recent price performance. (3) Only then look at the current price and decide if it fits within your valuation range.

When you write the note first, you're forced to assess intrinsic value independently. When you check price first, your entire analysis is unconsciously anchored to the recent price movement. The 12-question pre-investment checklist exists precisely to force this sequencing discipline.

Keep all your research notes in one document or folder. Review them quarterly. When a stock you own or watch reports quarterly results, pull out your original research note and ask: "Has my thesis played out? Has anything fundamentally changed?" This quarterly review discipline — not daily price checking — is what separates genuine investors from noise traders.
