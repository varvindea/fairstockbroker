01 — Key Learning Outcome

Strip Non-Recurring Items for Clean Earnings

🔍
Your Core Takeaway
A company's reported PAT (Net Profit) includes three categories of items that are NOT from its core business: Other Income (interest earned, dividend received, asset sales), Exceptional Items (one-time gains or losses like factory sales, impairments, restructuring), and Adjustments (accounting changes, prior period corrections). A skilled investor strips all of these away to calculate "Core Operating PAT" — the true, recurring profit the business actually generates from selling its products and services.
Imagine you earn ₹60,000/month from your job. In March, you also sold your old car for ₹4 lakh profit. Your "total income" that month was ₹4,60,000 — but your real recurring income is still ₹60,000. If a job recruiter sees your March income and thinks you earn ₹4,60,000 every month, they're being misled. The same happens with company profits: reported PAT includes one-time windfalls that will never repeat. Investors must identify and remove these to assess the true earning power of the business.

02 — The Three Categories

Other Income · Exceptional Items · Adjustments Decoded

Each of these three categories represents money that flows through the P&L but doesn't come from the core business of selling products or services. Understanding the difference between them — and whether each item recurs — is what separates a surface reader from a genuine analyst.

🏦
Other Income
Income from Non-Core Activities
Money earned from activities outside the company's main business. Some of this is genuinely recurring (interest income from surplus cash), while some is truly one-time (profit from selling a subsidiary). The investor's job is to classify each sub-item as recurring or non-recurring.
🏦 Interest income on fixed deposits & bonds
💰 Dividend received from investee companies
🏢 Profit on sale of non-core assets
💱 Foreign exchange gains (can be recurring)
📋 Rental income from surplus property
🔄 Royalty income from licensing
💥
Exceptional Items
Extraordinary, Non-Recurring Events
Items that SEBI and accounting standards define as "exceptional" — they are by definition not expected to recur. They are disclosed separately in the P&L so analysts can strip them out. Both gains and losses can be exceptional. Always remove these when assessing core earnings.
🏭 Profit / loss on sale of a whole division
📉 Impairment of goodwill or assets
⚖️ One-time litigation settlement gain/loss
🔥 Loss due to fire, flood, force majeure
✂️ Restructuring / VRS (voluntary retirement)
💊 COVID-era government relief / write-offs
📊
Adjustments & Prior Period
Accounting Changes & Corrections
Corrections to prior year numbers, changes in accounting policies (like adoption of Ind AS 116 for leases), or restatements. These are necessary and legitimate adjustments but should not be confused with the company's ongoing earning power.
📜 Prior period income/expense corrections
🔄 Change in depreciation policy impact
🏢 Ind AS 116 lease adoption adjustment
💰 Deferred tax asset/liability re-measurement
📋 Restatement of comparative figures
🔧 Change in inventory valuation method

🔍 The reported PAT of ₹500 Cr looks impressive — but only ₹100 Cr is the real, recurring business profit. The other ₹400 Cr came from one-time events that won't repeat. Always strip non-recurring items to find the true earning power.

1

Other Income — Not All of It Is "Bad" to Include

Other Income is nuanced. Interest income on surplus cash (like TCS or Nestlé holding thousands of crores in FDs) is genuinely recurring — the company will always earn this. But a one-time gain from selling a piece of land? That won't repeat. Your job: for each "Other Income" sub-item, ask "will this appear again next year?" If yes, it can stay in your earnings estimate. If no, strip it. On Screener.in, "Other Income" appears as a separate line in the P&L — click the "+" to expand it if available.

2

Exceptional Items — Always Strip, No Exceptions

Exceptional Items are specifically defined in Indian GAAP and Ind AS as items that are material and outside the ordinary activities of the business. They must be disclosed separately. The critical insight: whether the exceptional item is a gain (profit from selling a factory) or a loss (impairment of goodwill), it should always be stripped out when calculating "normalised" or "clean" PAT. A company that artificially boosts reported PAT by selling assets every year to hide operational weakness is using exceptional items dishonestly.

3

How to Calculate Clean Core PAT — The Formula

Clean Core PAT = Reported PAT − After-tax Other Income (non-recurring portion) − After-tax Exceptional Items. The "after-tax" part is important: these items get taxed too, so you subtract only the after-tax amount. If the company's tax rate is 30%, and it had ₹100 Cr exceptional gain, the after-tax impact on PAT is ₹70 Cr (100 × 70%). On Screener.in, look for "Exceptional Items" in the P&L — it appears below EBIT and before PBT in most company disclosures.

4

The Warning Sign — "Other Income" Growing Faster Than Revenue

A clear red flag: a company's revenue grows 10% but Other Income grows 80%. This often signals operational weakness being masked by investment gains or asset sales. The business isn't growing — it's monetising its balance sheet to appear profitable. Track "Other Income as % of Total Income" over 10 years on Screener.in. Healthy businesses: Other Income stays below 5–10% of total income. Troubled businesses: Other Income climbs to 30–50%+ of reported PAT, propping up earnings while core operations stagnate.

05 — Earnings Quality Red Flags

5 Cases of Misleading or Manipulated Other Income

These examples show how companies use Other Income and Exceptional Items to either mask operational weakness or, in the worst cases, actively mislead investors about real earning power.

🏗️
Jaypee Associates — Asset Sales Masking Operating Losses
Infrastructure · Historical · Other Income from asset monetisation hiding core losses
🚨 Other Income > Operating Profit 🚨 Annual exceptional "gains" 🚨 Core business deeply loss-making
🚨 The most dangerous pattern: using asset sales as "Other Income" to pretend the business is profitable while the core operations are loss-making. Jaypee repeatedly sold cement plants, hydropower assets, and land parcels to generate "profits." In several years, the profit on asset sales exceeded the operating profit — meaning if you removed the asset sales, the company was actually losing money. This is exactly the trap of not stripping Other Income. Investors who looked only at "Net Profit" saw profitability. Investors who stripped asset sale gains saw the truth: operational insolvency, mounting interest costs, and a business that was systematically selling itself to survive.
💻
Satyam Computer — Fictitious Other Income (Interest on Non-existent Cash)
IT Services · Historical · Fabricated bank balances generating fake interest income
🚨 Interest income from fake FDs 🚨 ₹7,000 Cr bank balance didn't exist 🚨 10-year fraud finally exposed in 2009
🚨 Satyam is the extreme case — fabricating Other Income to support a fictitious cash balance. B Ramalinga Raju (founder) inflated cash and bank balance by ₹7,136 Cr. This fake cash was "invested" in fake fixed deposits, generating fake interest income which appeared in Other Income. The cross-check that could have caught it: CFO (Cash from Operations) was genuinely healthy (real software business), but the "interest received" in the cash flow statement didn't match actual bank credits. When Interest Income in P&L doesn't show up as actual cash received in the Cash Flow Statement — it doesn't exist. Always cross-check Other Income against the Cash Flow Statement.
📱

Telecom · NSE: IDEA · Impairments & restructuring charges appearing annually

🚨 The "recurring exceptional item" paradox — when exceptional losses appear every year, they are no longer exceptional. Vi reported exceptional losses in every single year since the merger — goodwill impairment, network asset write-offs, spectrum derecognition, AGR provision. If a company has exceptional items for 5+ consecutive years, they're structurally embedded losses, not genuine one-time events. The correct approach: include all these losses in your "true" cost of running the business. This makes Vi's economic losses even larger than the already catastrophic reported numbers suggest.
🏦
IL&FS Group — Circular Other Income (Fees from Own Subsidiaries)
Infrastructure Finance · Historical · Fee income from subsidiaries masking group losses
🚨 Other Income from related parties 🚨 Circular fee structures 🚨 Consolidated losses hidden by sub-group
🚨 IL&FS used a complex web of 348 subsidiaries to create circular "Other Income" flows. The parent company charged management fees to subsidiaries; subsidiaries charged project fees back; holding companies earned "income" from sub-holding companies. When you consolidated the group, these transactions cancelled out — but at the individual entity level, each entity looked profitable. This is why always checking consolidated accounts (not standalone) is essential. If a company's "Other Income" is largely from related parties (subsidiaries, group companies), always check whether these are genuine arm's-length transactions or circular flows that disappear on consolidation.

⚡ 4 Bonus Quick Tricks — 10 Seconds Each
A
The "10-Year Other Income Trend" Test
Go to Screener.in → P&L tab → set to 10-year view. Look at the "Other Income" bar vs "Net Profit" bar for each year. If Other Income is consistently 8–12% of PAT and stable = excellent quality. If Other Income jumps to 40%+ in any year = strip that year's exceptional gains and recalculate the real PAT. One year's distortion is recoverable; five years of rising Other Income % = structural concern.
B
The "Exceptional Every Year" Red Flag
Exceptional items are supposed to be rare — by definition. If a company has exceptional losses in 4 out of 5 years, they are not exceptional — they are ordinary costs of running that business that management is choosing to classify as one-time. The test: count how many of the last 10 years had exceptional items. More than 3 = these are structural, not exceptional. Include them in your earnings model.
C
The Standalone vs Consolidated Check
Always use CONSOLIDATED P&L for earnings quality analysis, not standalone. In standalone, holding companies earn "Other Income" from subsidiaries (dividends, management fees) which disappears on consolidation. Comparing Other Income in standalone vs consolidated immediately reveals how much is circular group-level income vs genuinely external income. If Other Income is 40% lower in consolidated = largely circular flows, not real.
D
The "Related Party Other Income" Warning
Check the Annual Report → Related Party Transactions section. If a significant portion of Other Income comes from related parties (subsidiaries, promoter entities, group companies), verify it's arm's-length. Management fees charged to subsidiaries that are struggling, or "royalties" from weak affiliates, can be circular or fictitious. Cross-check: does the related party paying this income appear healthy enough to afford it in its own accounts?

CFO/PAT RatioCash from Operations divided by Profit After Tax. Measures how much of accounting profits actually show up as cash. Above 0.85× = real, cash-backed profits. Below 0.5× for 3+ years = investigate — profits may be inflated through accruals or aggressive revenue recognition.Validate PAT
Kitchen Sink QuarterWhen new management books all possible write-offs, provisions, and impairments in one large exceptional loss to "clean the slate" — creating a depressed base for future "growth" comparisons. Always check if exceptional losses in Year 1 of new management are followed by suspiciously clean books in subsequent years.🚨 Watch
Prior Period ItemsIncome or expenses relating to previous years that were omitted or misstated — now being corrected in current P&L. Should be disclosed separately. One-off corrections are normal and acceptable; repeated prior period items suggest poor accounting practices or restatements.Investigate
Recurring vs Non-RecurringThe fundamental distinction in earnings quality analysis. Recurring income will appear again in future years (interest on cash, annual dividends). Non-recurring income was a one-time event (sale of land, insurance claim). Only recurring items should be used in forward earnings estimates for valuation.Core concept
Related Party IncomeOther Income from subsidiaries, group companies, or promoter-linked entities. May be legitimate (genuine dividends, arms-length management fees) or circular (fees charged to struggling subsidiaries that merely appear in both companies' books). Always check related party disclosures in the Annual Report.Cross-check
Ind AS 116 AdjustmentAn accounting standard change (adopted by Indian companies from FY20) that brought lease obligations onto the balance sheet. This increased depreciation and interest costs, reducing reported EBIT and PAT. When comparing pre and post Ind AS 116 figures, adjustments are needed to make numbers comparable.Adjust pre-FY20
