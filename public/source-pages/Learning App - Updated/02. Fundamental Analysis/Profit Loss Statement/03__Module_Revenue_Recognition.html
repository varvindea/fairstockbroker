02 — The Foundation

What Is Revenue Recognition — Explained Simply

Revenue recognition answers one question: "At what point has the company truly earned its money?" The answer isn't always obvious — and companies exploit that ambiguity to inflate near-term earnings.

📦
Step 1 — The Rule
Ind AS 115 — 5-Step Model
India follows Ind AS 115 (aligned with IFRS 15). Revenue is booked only when the performance obligation — delivering goods or services to the customer's satisfaction — is met. Not when a contract is signed, not when goods are merely shipped.
⚠️
Step 2 — The Problem
How Companies Manipulate
Companies push revenue early: shipping unsold goods (channel stuffing), booking multi-year contracts entirely upfront, recognizing revenue before milestones, or using related-party transactions to inflate sales. P&L looks great — cash flow doesn't.
🔎
Step 3 — The Solution
3 Screener.in Signals
Check: (1) Revenue growth vs Trade Receivables growth — receivables growing much faster = cash not flowing in. (2) CFO vs Net Profit — if operating cash is far below net profit, earnings quality is poor. (3) Debtor days trend — rising every year = serious warning.

🔍 Every rupee of revenue must pass this test: Did the customer receive what they paid for? Did cash actually arrive? If not, the revenue exists only on paper — and the CFO ratio will reveal it.

📋 Ind AS 115 — The 5-Step Revenue Recognition Model
Every Indian listed company must follow this. Understanding each step helps you spot where companies bend the rules to inflate reported earnings.
1
Identify the Contract with Customer
A valid, enforceable contract must exist — written, oral, or implied. No valid contract = no revenue. Companies sometimes book revenue on unconfirmed orders or verbal agreements that collapse next quarter, forcing embarrassing reversals.
📌 Ask: Is this contract legally binding and approved by both parties?
2
Identify Distinct Performance Obligations
A software company selling a license + 3 years support has TWO obligations. Revenue must be split between them. Many companies ignore this and front-load recognition of bundled deals — making Year 1 revenue look artificially high while Year 2–3 look weak.
🚨 Risk: Multi-year bundled contracts recognized entirely upfront in Year 1
3
Determine the Transaction Price
The price must exclude expected returns, discounts, and trade allowances. Channel stuffers give huge incentives to dealers to accept excess inventory — but those incentives must reduce the recognized revenue. They often don't, inflating reported sales.
🚨 Risk: Returns, discounts, and trade rebates not properly deducted
4
Allocate Price to Each Obligation
When multiple obligations exist, the total price must be allocated proportionally. Companies sometimes allocate more value to items delivered first, boosting near-term revenue while pushing cost-heavy obligations (like ongoing service) to future periods.
⚠️ Watch for uneven price allocation in bundled product-service deals
5
Recognize When Obligation is Satisfied
The critical step. Revenue is recognized at a point in time (delivery) or over time (subscriptions, long-term contracts). The test: has the customer obtained "control" of the goods or service? Channel stuffing fails here — the dealer never truly accepted the goods.
✅ Green flag: Revenue recognized only after confirmed delivery and acceptance

A company with ₹500 Cr net profit but only ₹80 Cr CFO is generating mostly paper profits, not real shareholder wealth.

📋

Check 2: Debtor Days Rising — The Channel Stuffing Fingerprint

Debtor Days = (Trade Receivables ÷ Revenue) × 365. Find Trade Receivables on Screener.in Balance Sheet. If this number is growing from 30d → 45d → 75d → 100d over 4 years while revenue also grows, customers are taking longer to pay. Either the company is extending more credit to boost sales artificially, or channel stuffing is occurring — pushing inventory to dealers who haven't committed to buying. In FMCG (where goods should sell in 2–4 weeks), debtor days above 90+ is a serious red flag.

⚠️

Check 3: Revenue vs Receivables CAGR — The Mismatch Test

Compare 5-year Revenue CAGR vs 5-year Trade Receivables CAGR. If Revenue grew 15% CAGR but Receivables grew 40% CAGR, that 25% gap is revenue that wasn't collected in cash. If Receivables CAGR ÷ Revenue CAGR is above 1.5×, investigate. Above 2.0× is a serious red flag. This works for any sector — FMCG, pharma, auto ancillaries, specialty chemicals. Growing receivables faster than revenue is one of the most reliable early warning signals in equity analysis.

Debtor Days(Trade Receivables ÷ Revenue) × 365. How many days customers take to pay. Stable or declining = healthy. Rising year after year = possible channel stuffing or credit deterioration.Watch
Performance ObligationUnder Ind AS 115, a distinct promise to deliver a good or service. Revenue is recognized only when this obligation is fulfilled. Ignoring obligations = aggressive recognition.Technical
Deferred RevenueMoney received from customers for services not yet delivered — a liability on the Balance Sheet. Growing deferred revenue = strong future pipeline. Declining = future revenue consumed today.Watch Trend
Aggressive RecognitionRecording revenue earlier than it should be — e.g., booking a 3-year contract entirely in Year 1. May not violate rules technically, but inflates near-term earnings at expense of future periods.🚨 High
Trade ReceivablesMoney owed TO the company by customers. A healthy business has low and stable trade receivables. Surging trade receivables relative to revenue = revenue booked but cash not collected.Watch
Earnings QualityHow reliably reported profits reflect actual economic reality and cash generation. High quality = sustainable, repeatable, backed by cash. Low quality = one-time items, accounting adjustments, paper profits.Core Concept
Related Party RevenueRevenue from promoter-owned companies, subsidiaries, or associates. Small amounts are normal. More than 15–20% of total revenue warrants scrutiny — prices may not be arm's-length.🚨 Above 20%
Ind AS 115India's revenue recognition standard, effective April 2018, aligned with IFRS 15. Requires the 5-step model. Replaced older, more flexible standards. Companies still find ways to be aggressive within the framework.Regulatory
