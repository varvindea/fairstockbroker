Your Core Takeaway
Operating Expenses are all the costs a company incurs to run its business and generate revenue. They fall into three major categories: COGS (Cost of Goods Sold — what it costs to actually make the product), SG&A (Selling, General & Administrative — all overhead and marketing), and R&D (Research & Development — investment in future competitive advantage). The TREND of each as a percentage of revenue is the key signal.
Imagine running a restaurant. Your ingredient cost (COGS) should grow in line with meals served — if it grows faster, you're becoming less efficient. Your rent and manager's salary (SG&A) should ideally stay fixed even as revenue grows — this is operating leverage. If you invest in a new kitchen (R&D), it costs money today but builds future capacity. For any listed company on Screener.in, tracking COGS%, SG&A%, and R&D% over 10 years tells you whether management is disciplined, whether the business model has structural efficiency, and whether the company is building or eroding its competitive advantage.

02 — The Three Cost Categories

COGS · SG&A · R&D — Decoded Simply

Every rupee a company spends falls into one of these three buckets. Understanding what each represents — and why its trend matters far more than its absolute value — is the foundation of cost structure analysis.

🏭 COGS — Cost of Goods Sold
Raw Materials
Steel, cotton, wheat, crude oil, chemicals — whatever the product is physically made from. Rises directly with production volume.
Direct Labour
Factory workers, machine operators, assembly line staff — people who physically make the product. Not office staff.
Manufacturing Overhead
Factory electricity, machine maintenance, production equipment depreciation, quality control costs.
Packaging & Inbound Freight
Cost to package finished goods and bring raw materials to the factory. Rises with production volume.
🏢 SG&A — Selling, General & Administrative
Sales & Marketing
Salesperson salaries, advertising, promotions, trade discounts, distribution network — the cost of getting products to customers.
General & Administrative
CEO/CFO salaries, legal fees, accounting, IT systems, office rent and utilities — the cost of running the organization.
Brand Investment
TV ads, digital marketing, sponsorships — builds long-term demand but is expensed immediately in the current P&L.
Distribution Costs
Outbound freight, warehouse management, dealer margins, logistics — the cost of the sales supply chain.
🔬 R&D — Research & Development
Basic Research
Pure science and discovery — pharmaceutical drug trials, semiconductor material research. Long-term payoff, often 5–15 years away.
Product Development
Engineering new products, writing software, designing new features — turning research into market-ready offerings.
Process Innovation
Improving manufacturing efficiency, reducing COGS over time, automating production steps — drives future margin expansion.
Regulatory & Clinical
Drug approvals, product certification, compliance testing — mandatory for pharma/biotech before products can be sold.
COGS + SG&A + R&D = Total Operating Expenses
Revenue − Total Operating Expenses = Operating Profit (EBITDA)  ·  The lower these costs as % of revenue, the higher the OPM%

🏭 Every rupee of revenue flows through three cost filters before becoming profit. Track each as a % of revenue over 10 years on Screener.in. Falling percentages = improving efficiency. Rising percentages = cost discipline slipping.

C

COGS — What It Costs to Actually Make the Product

COGS (also called "Cost of Revenue") covers everything directly tied to production: raw materials, direct factory labour, factory overhead, and packaging. Gross Margin = (Revenue − COGS) ÷ Revenue. A rising COGS% over years means raw material inflation is eating margins or production is becoming less efficient. A falling COGS% means the company is gaining pricing power or improving manufacturing efficiency. On Screener.in, expand "Expenses +" in the P&L to find "Raw Material Cost" and "Power & Fuel" — these form the COGS equivalent.

S

SG&A — The Overhead That Should Scale Slowly

SG&A is everything beyond production: salesperson salaries, ad budgets, CEO pay, legal fees, office rent, IT systems. The crucial insight is that SG&A should ideally grow much slower than revenue — this creates operating leverage. If SG&A% is stable or declining as revenue grows, the business is becoming more profitable per rupee of revenue. If SG&A% is rising rapidly, management is not controlling overhead — or is investing heavily in sales force expansion (which needs to show up in revenue growth to justify itself).

R

R&D — Today's Cost That Builds Tomorrow's Revenue

R&D is the investment a company makes in its own future: new drugs, better products, improved processes, new technologies. Indian pharma companies spend 6–10% of revenue on R&D. IT service companies spend 1–3%. A company cutting R&D to boost short-term PAT is mortgaging its competitive future — it may look good for 1–2 years before competitive pressure shows up. Stable or growing R&D% during difficult revenue periods shows that management is protecting long-term strategic advantage.

🏭

COGS Discipline — Stable Raw Material % Despite Commodity Inflation

Nestlé's raw material cost percentage stayed in a narrow 40–44% band across FY20–FY25, even though global commodity prices (wheat, cocoa, palm oil, milk) went through significant inflation in FY22–FY23. How? Brand pricing power: Nestlé raised Maggi, KitKat, and Munch prices 10–15% in FY22–FY23, passing on cost increases to consumers without losing significant volume. This is the hallmark of a company with genuine pricing power — COGS inflation doesn't reach the bottom line because revenue grows proportionally.

🏢

SG&A Leverage — Revenue Growing Faster Than Overhead

Employee costs grew from ₹1,215 Cr (FY20) to ₹1,832 Cr (FY25) — a 51% increase. But revenue grew 47%. Employee costs as % of revenue stayed at 10%. This tells you Nestlé is adding staff at roughly the same rate as revenue — not faster. The A&P (advertising) spend of 8% of revenue is a deliberate choice: keeping brands visible requires consistent investment. Notice that A&P grew from ₹1,012 Cr to ₹1,488 Cr — Nestlé didn't cut brand investment when margins were under pressure in FY22–FY23. That discipline protects the long-term moat.

📊

OPM% Trend — The Final Cost Structure Report Card

Nestlé's OPM expanded from 22.2% in FY20 to 27.1% in FY24 — a significant 5-percentage-point improvement showing costs growing slower than revenue (genuine operating leverage). The slight compression to 23.8% in FY25 reflects Nestlé investing in new capacity and product categories. This is a temporary, investment-driven compression — not structural deterioration. For any FMCG company, sustained OPM above 20% is excellent. When Nestlé's OPM touched 27%, it was generating ₹27 of operating profit for every ₹100 of revenue — extraordinary for a consumer goods company selling everyday food products at grocery store prices.

The OPM% (Operating Profit Margin) row is already calculated on Screener.in — no math needed. Just look at the 10-year trend. Is it expanding (improving year over year), stable (holding but not improving), or compressing (deteriorating)? This single number summarises the combined impact of all three cost lines — it's your final verdict on cost structure quality.
OPM % = Revenue − COGS − SG&A − R&D Revenue × 100
Expanding OPM% = cost discipline + operating leverage ✅ Stable OPM% = costs tracking revenue (no leverage) ⚠️ Compressing OPM% = costs growing faster than revenue ❌
✅OPM expanding 1–2% per year over 10 years = costs being extremely well managed. Profits compounding faster than revenue. Best business quality signal.
🚨OPM compressing 2–3% per year = costs out of control. In 5 years this company may have razor-thin or negative margins despite growing revenue.
⚡ 4 Bonus Quick Tricks — Spot Cost Problems in 10 Seconds Each
A
The Gross Margin Collapse Test
Look at the OPM% row on Screener.in. Then look at the "Expenses / Revenue" ratio. If OPM dropped 5+ percentage points in 2 years but revenue grew — COGS exploded. Check Raw Material Cost% specifically. A 3% COGS% rise on ₹10,000 Cr revenue = ₹300 Cr less profit. That's massive.
B
The Employee Cost Ratio Trick
Divide Employee Cost by Revenue. Compare FY20 vs FY25. If Employee Cost% is declining as revenue scales: operating leverage from a semi-fixed payroll. If Employee Cost% is rising every year: either over-hiring or wage inflation not matched by revenue. For IT services (employee-heavy), stable at 52–55% is healthy.
C
The Revenue vs Operating Profit Multiplier
Revenue 5-year growth: X%. Operating Profit 5-year growth: Y%. If Y > X: operating leverage exists — OPM is expanding. If Y = X: costs tracking revenue perfectly — neutral. If Y < X: costs growing faster than revenue — OPM is compressing. This comparison takes 15 seconds and tells you everything about cost discipline.
D
The Fixed Cost Trap Early Warning
If revenue is falling but total expenses are barely changing — the company has high fixed costs it cannot reduce. OPM will collapse fast. Calculate: when revenue fell 10%, did OPM fall more than 10%? If OPM fell 20–30% on a 10% revenue drop = dangerously high fixed cost base. MTNL, Vi — both showed this pattern years before the crisis.

07 — Reference Glossary

Cost Structure Glossary

Every term you need to analyse operating expenses — plain language definitions with context on what each means for investors.

Term What It Means in Simple Words Investor Signal
COGSCost of Goods Sold — the direct cost of making the product: raw materials, direct labour, factory overhead. Gross Margin = (Revenue − COGS) ÷ Revenue. Falling COGS% = efficiency improving.Watch %
SG&ASelling, General & Administrative costs — all overhead beyond production: sales team, marketing, executive salaries, office, IT systems. Should ideally grow slower than revenue to create operating leverage.Trend key
R&DResearch & Development — investment in future products, processes, and competitive advantage. Expensed immediately in P&L even though benefits come years later. Cutting R&D for short-term profit = dangerous.Long-term
Gross Margin(Revenue − COGS) ÷ Revenue. Shows how much is left after direct production costs. High gross margin = pricing power or manufacturing efficiency. Low gross margin = thin product economics.Core metric
OPM %Operating Profit Margin = (Revenue − COGS − SG&A − R&D) ÷ Revenue. The bottom line of operational efficiency. OPM expanding over time = costs growing slower than revenue. OPM contracting = cost bloat or pricing pressure.Key ratio
Operating LeverageThe multiplier effect of fixed costs: when revenue grows faster than fixed costs, every extra rupee of revenue drops disproportionately to profit. Businesses with high fixed costs but growing revenue show rapidly expanding OPM.Very bullish
Fixed Cost TrapThe opposite of operating leverage: when a business has high fixed costs (that cannot be reduced) and falling revenue. Every rupee of lost revenue has a multiplied impact on losses. MTNL and Vi are classic examples.🚨 Danger
Cost BloatWhen SG&A or overhead grows significantly faster than revenue without generating proportionate additional revenue. Common in rapidly hiring companies, companies with weak management discipline, or businesses chasing growth without profitability.Red flag
EBITDAEarnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation & Amortisation — a proxy for operating cash generation. Revenue minus COGS minus SG&A minus R&D (but before depreciation). Used to compare operational efficiency across capital structures.Watch trend
% of Revenue AnalysisExpressing every cost line as a percentage of revenue and tracking that percentage over 10 years. The most powerful tool for cost structure analysis — it removes the distortion of absolute growth and shows whether costs are being controlled relative to revenue.Core method
