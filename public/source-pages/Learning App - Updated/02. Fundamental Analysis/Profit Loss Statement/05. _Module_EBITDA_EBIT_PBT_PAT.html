01 — Key Learning Outcome

Calculate and Interpret Profitability Layers

📊
Your Core Takeaway
A company's income statement contains four distinct profit numbers — EBITDA, EBIT, PBT, and PAT. Each one strips out a different type of cost from revenue. They exist because different stakeholders need different measures: an analyst comparing operational efficiency across industries ignores capital structure (uses EBITDA); a debt investor needs to see interest coverage (uses EBIT); a tax lawyer focuses on PBT; an equity shareholder ultimately cares about PAT.
Think of it like peeling an onion. Revenue is the whole onion. EBITDA peels off operating expenses. EBIT additionally peels off depreciation — the accounting cost of assets wearing out. PBT further peels off interest — the cost of borrowed money. PAT is the final layer: profit after paying the government's share of taxes. Each layer reveals something different about the business. A company with great EBITDA but terrible PAT is either heavily indebted (interest eating profits) or highly capital-intensive (depreciation eating profits) — and that matters enormously for your investment decision.

02 — The Profitability Stack

From Revenue to PAT — Every Deduction Explained

Revenue flows through four deductions to become PAT. Each deduction represents a real cost — operational, physical, financial, and governmental. Understanding each one tells you who is taking a cut before shareholders get their share.

EBITDA
Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation & Amortisation
Operating profit — before financing and accounting deductions
Measures pure operational efficiency. Two companies with identical products but different debt levels will have the same EBITDA but very different PATs. Best for comparing operational performance across capital structures.
Revenue − COGS − SG&A − R&D
EBIT
Earnings Before Interest & Tax
After subtracting depreciation & amortisation from EBITDA
Depreciation represents the annual accounting cost of assets wearing out (machines, buildings). EBIT shows profit after recognising this real economic cost. Capital-intensive companies (steel, power) have much lower EBIT vs EBITDA.
EBITDA − Depreciation − Amortisation
PBT
Profit Before Tax
After subtracting interest expense from EBIT
Interest is the cost of debt — paid to banks and bondholders regardless of business performance. A heavily indebted company can have excellent EBIT but near-zero or negative PBT. PBT reveals the burden of the capital structure on profitability.
EBIT − Net Interest Expense
PAT
Profit After Tax (Net Profit)
What's left for shareholders after paying government taxes
PAT is the "bottom line" — the profit that belongs entirely to equity shareholders. It can be paid out as dividends or retained to reinvest. PAT drives EPS (Earnings Per Share) and ultimately the intrinsic value of every share you own.
PBT − Income Tax Expense

E1
EBITDA Revenue − OpEx

Why It Ignores Debt and Depreciation — and Why That's Useful

EBITDA deliberately excludes interest (debt financing), depreciation (accounting for asset age), and taxes (government-set rates). This makes it ideal for comparing two companies with identical products but different capital structures. Company A borrowed heavily to build its factory — high interest, high depreciation, low PAT. Company B raised equity — zero interest, same depreciation, higher PAT. Their EBITDA would be identical — their PAT wildly different. On Screener.in this is the "Operating Profit" row.

E2

The True Earning Power After Acknowledging Asset Wear

Depreciation is the annual accounting charge for physical assets wearing out over time. A steel company with ₹10,000 Cr of fixed assets might depreciate ₹800 Cr annually — a real economic cost even if no cash leaves today. For asset-light businesses (software, FMCG brands), depreciation is tiny and EBIT ≈ EBITDA. For capital-intensive businesses (telecom, power, manufacturing), EBIT can be dramatically lower than EBITDA — revealing hidden asset replacement costs that EBITDA flatters away.

📍 Screener: Operating Profit − Depreciation ✅ Asset-light: EBIT ≈ EBITDA (tiny D&A) 🚨 Capital-intensive: EBIT far below EBITDA

Deducted next
Net Interest Expense — cost of borrowing, paid to banks, bonds & NCDs regardless of performance
PBT
PBT EBIT − Interest

Profit After Paying Your Lenders, Before Paying the Government

Interest is the price of borrowing — paid to banks, bondholders, and NCDs regardless of how the business is performing. A company with ₹300 Cr EBIT but ₹280 Cr interest expense has PBT of only ₹20 Cr — dangerously leveraged. The Interest Coverage Ratio (EBIT ÷ Interest) below 2× is a red flag. Above 5× = financially comfortable. Debt-free companies (CDSL, Nestlé, TCS) have EBIT = PBT — they skip this deduction entirely, giving them a massive competitive advantage during downturns.

PAT
PAT PBT − Tax ✅

The Final Number That Actually Belongs to You

PAT is the only profit number that truly belongs to equity shareholders. It drives EPS (Earnings Per Share = PAT ÷ Total Shares), which drives the P/E ratio used for valuation. Net Profit Margin = PAT ÷ Revenue. A 20%+ net margin for 10 consecutive years is exceptional — businesses like this compound wealth reliably. On Screener.in, PAT is listed as "Net Profit" in the P&L. Watch the PAT CAGR over 10 years — it's the single most reliable predictor of long-term stock price performance.

📊

EBITDA vs EBIT — Why TCS Shows the Asset-Light Advantage

TCS's EBITDA margin (26%) is 6–7 percentage points above its EBIT margin (19–20%). This gap is the D&A — ₹9,500–15,700 Cr annually for servers, software, office equipment, and right-of-use asset depreciation (leased office spaces under Ind AS 116). For a pure software company with no physical factories, this D&A is real but manageable. Compare this to a steel company where D&A can consume 10–15% of revenue — making EBIT dramatically lower than EBITDA. When someone quotes "26% EBITDA margin" for TCS but the stock P/E uses PAT-based EPS, you need both numbers to understand the full picture.

🏦

PBT > EBIT — The Magical Effect of Being Debt-Free with Surplus Cash

TCS's PBT exceeds EBIT by ₹2,000–5,000 Cr annually — because TCS has no debt and instead holds massive cash reserves (₹50,000+ Cr in investments) that earn interest income. Net Interest = Interest Income − Interest Expense. For TCS, Interest Income is substantial (earning returns on its cash pile) while Interest Expense is near-zero (no borrowings). This is the opposite of a leveraged company where Interest Expense devastates PBT. Debt-free, cash-rich companies effectively have a free "financial business" running alongside their operating business — the cash earns money while the core business earns money.

💰

PAT CAGR — The Single Most Important Trend for Equity Investors

TCS's PAT grew from ₹33,388 Cr (FY21) to ₹49,454 Cr (FY25) — a 48% increase in 5 years, or approximately 10% CAGR. This PAT growth directly drives EPS growth, which drives P/E-based valuation, which determines whether the stock is cheap or expensive. If PAT CAGR exceeds 15% for 10+ years, the company is compounding wealth. If PAT CAGR is below revenue CAGR, costs are growing faster than revenue — a warning sign. The 10-year PAT CAGR is one of the most predictive indicators of long-term stock performance available on Screener.in — it requires no complex analysis, just discipline to read the trend.

1
screener.in → P&L tab → "Operating Profit" row = EBITDA ✅
"Operating Profit" on Screener.in is your EBITDA — pre-calculated, no math needed. Read the OPM % column next to it (also pre-calculated). Track it for 5–10 years. Is it expanding, stable, or compressing? This one number tells you whether the core business is getting more or less profitable over time.
EBITDA= Revenue − All Operating Expenses (excl. D&A, Interest, Tax)
OPM above 20% = strong operating business ✅ OPM 10–20% = moderate, sector-dependent ⚠️ OPM below 5% = thin margins, high risk ❌ OPM negative = operating loss, avoid 🚨
📋 You now have EBITDA and OPM% for 10 years. Every step below will reduce this number further. The goal is to see how much survives each layer.
2
P&L tab → "Depreciation" row → EBIT = Operating Profit − Depreciation
Find "Depreciation" (exactly this name on Screener). Calculate D&A ÷ Revenue. This percentage tells you the capital intensity of the business — how much of each revenue rupee goes to covering asset wear-out. The larger this gap between EBITDA and EBIT, the more capital-hungry the business.
EBIT= EBITDA − Depreciation & Amortisation
D&A below 3% of revenue = asset-light model ✅ D&A 3–8% = moderate capital intensity ⚠️ D&A above 10% = heavy capex, EBIT far below EBITDA 🚨
💡 No-calculator trick
Just look at the Depreciation number vs the Operating Profit number. If Depreciation is less than 15% of Operating Profit — it's not eating much. If it's above 50% of Operating Profit — EBIT is far below EBITDA. For Vodafone Idea, D&A exceeds EBITDA entirely — EBIT goes negative immediately after this step.
3
P&L tab → "Interest" row → ICR = EBIT ÷ Interest
Find the "Interest" row. Calculate Interest Coverage Ratio (ICR) = EBIT ÷ Interest. This is the single most important solvency check for any non-financial company. If there is no Interest row — the company is debt-free (PBT ≈ EBIT or higher from interest income).

🚨 ICR below 2× = the company struggles to pay interest from operations. PBT and PAT will be dramatically lower than EBIT.

Calculate PAT ÷ EBITDA. This tells you how many paisa of every EBITDA rupee actually reaches shareholders. A high ratio = asset-light + debt-free business. A low ratio = capital-intensive or heavily leveraged. Below 20% is a warning sign even if EBITDA looks strong.

⚡ 4 Bonus Quick Tricks — Read Any Profit Layer in 10 Seconds Each
A
The EBITDA–PAT Gap Test
Subtract PAT Margin from EBITDA Margin. Gap above 15 percentage points = something big is eating between those layers. Ask: Is it D&A (capital-intensive)? Or interest (over-leveraged)? Or abnormal taxes? Each point of gap = real shareholder money not arriving. TCS gap: ~9%. Vi gap: +80% (EBITDA positive, PAT massively negative).
B
The Effective Tax Rate Check
Calculate Tax Expense ÷ PBT. Normal Indian company: 22–30%. Below 15% = tax incentives or deferred tax assets (check if sustainable). Above 40% = disallowances or additional levies. A sudden drop in effective tax rate makes PAT look better without operational improvement — always verify it's repeatable.
C
The PAT vs CFO Cross-Check
After reading PAT, jump to the Cash Flow tab → "Cash from Operating Activities" (CFO). Calculate CFO ÷ PAT. Above 0.8× = profits are backed by real cash. Far below PAT for years = paper profits from accruals or aggressive revenue recognition. This cross-check validates whether all four layers are telling a consistent story.
D
The "Adjusted EBITDA" Red Flag
If a company's presentations say "Adjusted EBITDA" — always find Screener.in's "Operating Profit" instead. Companies adjust out ESOP costs, customer acquisition costs, restructuring charges. If these recur every year, they're not "one-time" — they're permanent costs being hidden. GAAP Operating Profit on Screener.in cannot be adjusted away.

07 — Reference Glossary

Profitability Layers Glossary

Every term in the EBITDA → PAT profitability chain — plain language, precise definition, and investor relevance.

Term What It Means in Simple Words Investor Signal
EBITDAEarnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation & Amortisation. Operating profit before financing and accounting deductions. Best for comparing operational efficiency across companies with different debt levels or asset intensities.Compare ops
EBITEarnings Before Interest & Tax. EBITDA minus depreciation. Recognises the real economic cost of assets wearing out. For asset-light companies (IT, FMCG), EBIT ≈ EBITDA. For capital-intensive companies (steel, telecom), EBIT is dramatically lower.Solvency test
PBTProfit Before Tax. EBIT minus net interest expense. Reveals the cost of debt financing on profitability. Debt-free companies have PBT = EBIT (or higher if earning interest income). Heavily indebted companies can have EBIT positive but PBT negative.Debt impact
PATProfit After Tax. Net Profit. The final number that belongs entirely to equity shareholders. Drives EPS, dividends, and intrinsic value. This is the only profit number you should use for PE ratio calculation and long-term investment decisions.Core metric
OPM %Operating Profit Margin = EBITDA ÷ Revenue × 100. Shows the percentage of revenue that becomes operating profit before financing costs. Available directly on Screener.in as "OPM %".Key ratio
Interest CoverageEBIT ÷ Interest Expense. Shows how many times EBIT covers interest payments. Below 2× = high risk; company barely covers interest from operations. Above 5× = very safe. The most important solvency ratio for non-financial companies.Watch carefully
DepreciationAnnual accounting charge for the wearing out of physical assets (machines, buildings) over their useful life. Not a cash outflow today, but represents the real economic cost of eventually replacing assets. Added back in EBITDA — subtracted in EBIT.% of rev
EPSEarnings Per Share = PAT ÷ Total Shares Outstanding. The per-share amount of net profit. P/E Ratio = Share Price ÷ EPS. Growing EPS over 10 years is the most direct predictor of share price appreciation. Dilution (new share issuance) reduces EPS even if PAT grows.Valuation key
Effective Tax RateActual Income Tax Paid ÷ PBT × 100. Normal for Indian companies: 22–30%. Below 15% = deferred tax benefits or tax incentives (check sustainability). Above 35% = additional charges. A sudden drop in effective tax rate can inflate PAT without operational improvement.Watch
Adjusted EBITDAA non-GAAP metric defined by management, not auditors. Can exclude any "one-time" costs the company chooses. Common exclusions: ESOP cost, customer acquisition cost, restructuring. Always compare to Screener.in's Operating Profit (GAAP EBITDA) — if they differ significantly, investigate what's being excluded and why.🚨 Verify
