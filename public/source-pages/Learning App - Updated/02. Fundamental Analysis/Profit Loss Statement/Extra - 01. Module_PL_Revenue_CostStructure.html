01 — Key Learning Outcome

The P&L Is a Movie, Not a Photograph

📊
Your Core Takeaway
Unlike the Balance Sheet (a snapshot at one date), the P&L Income Statement shows what happened over an entire year — how much the company earned from sales, what it cost to generate those sales, and what survived as profit after all expenses, interest, depreciation, and taxes.
Imagine a cable manufacturer — Polycab — buying copper wire (raw material), passing it through machines to make electrical cables, then selling those cables to electricians and contractors across India. The money collected from sales is Revenue. The cost of the copper, the factory workers' wages, and the electricity to run the machines is COGS. The cost of the sales team, the advertising, and the head office is Operating Expenses. What's left after these two deductions is EBITDA. Then subtract depreciation on the machines, interest on any loans, and income tax — what remains is Net Profit (PAT). The P&L is read from top to bottom, with revenue at the very top (hence "Top Line") and net profit at the very bottom ("Bottom Line"). The journey from top to bottom reveals everything about a company's efficiency, pricing power, and competitive advantage.
📈 Revenue = Top Line 💸 COGS = Direct Costs ✅ EBITDA = Operating Proxy ⚠️ PAT ≠ Cash Flow
02 — The P&L Waterfall

💰
Revenue / Net Sales
Top Line · Primary Business Income
= Gross Sales − Returns − Discounts − GST
Revenue is the total money a company earns from selling its products or services during a year — before any expenses are deducted. For Polycab, it's the value of all cables, wires, and FMEG products sold. For Maruti, it's the selling price of every car delivered. It's shown "Net" — meaning GST collected is excluded (GST goes to the government, not the company), and trade discounts and returns are already deducted.

The critical distinction every analyst must make: Operating Revenue vs Other Income. Operating revenue is what the company earned from its core business — cables, cars, medicines. Other income (dividends, interest on FDs, forex gains) is non-recurring and should be excluded when measuring true business performance. A company with ₹2,000 Cr operating revenue but ₹800 Cr "other income" propping up its PAT is not as healthy as it appears.

Double-digit revenue growth (10%+) consistently over 5 years is a strong positive signal — it means the company is gaining market share, expanding into new geographies, or benefiting from sector tailwinds. Revenue growing below GDP growth (~6–7%) for a consumer company = losing ground in real terms.
Metric
Revenue Growth = (Current Year Revenue − Previous Year Revenue) ÷ Previous Year Revenue × 100
✅ Target: 12–20% CAGR revenue growth for 5 consecutive years = excellent. Consistent organic growth (not acquisition-driven) is the gold standard.
🔩
Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
Direct Costs · Raw Materials · Labour · Manufacturing Overhead
Raw materials + Direct wages + Factory overhead
COGS represents the direct costs of producing what was sold. For a cable manufacturer like Polycab, this includes copper (the primary raw material, 60–65% of COGS), PVC insulation, steel armour, direct factory worker wages, and manufacturing overhead (electricity, factory maintenance). For a pharma company like Sun Pharma, COGS is Active Pharmaceutical Ingredients (APIs), packaging, and direct manufacturing labour.

Gross Profit = Revenue − COGS. The Gross Margin (Gross Profit ÷ Revenue) is the first and most fundamental measure of pricing power. A company with 60% gross margin (like Asian Paints) keeps ₹60 from every ₹100 of sales before paying any operating expenses. A company with 20% gross margin (like a pure commodity steel producer) keeps only ₹20 — and must run very lean operations to be profitable.

When raw material costs spike (copper price surge, oil price shock, pharma API inflation), COGS rises faster than revenue — compressing gross margins. The best businesses either: (a) pass on input cost increases to customers (pricing power), (b) have long-term supply contracts that cap input costs, or (c) have diversified raw material sourcing that reduces exposure to single-commodity spikes. Watch gross margin trends across 5 years — sustained compression signals structural loss of pricing power.
Primary Formula
Gross Profit = Revenue − COGS  |  Gross Margin % = (Gross Profit ÷ Revenue) × 100
🚨 Red Flag: COGS growing faster than revenue for 2+ years = margin compression from commodity inflation or competitive pricing pressure. Gross margin below 10% for a non-commodity manufacturer = near-zero buffer for any operating inefficiency.
🏢
Operating Expenses (OpEx)
SG&A · R&D · Marketing · Employee Costs · Indirect overhead
Indirect costs not tied to single unit of production
Operating expenses are costs incurred to run the business that are not directly tied to a single unit of production. They include: Selling expenses — distributor commissions, salesman salaries, advertising, trade promotions. General & Administrative (G&A) — CEO and management salaries, legal fees, audit fees, head office rent. R&D expenses — critical for pharma (drug discovery) and technology companies. Employee costs — for service-heavy businesses, often the largest OpEx item.

EBITDA = Gross Profit − Operating Expenses. Rising OpEx as a percentage of revenue without a corresponding rise in revenue is the clearest sign of operational inefficiency — the company is spending more to generate the same revenue. Conversely, if OpEx grows at 5% while revenue grows at 20%, the company is demonstrating operating leverage — the ability to scale revenue faster than costs, dramatically improving profitability.

For manufacturing companies, the ideal is for employee costs and SG&A to grow at half the rate of revenue growth over time — this operating leverage is what drives margin expansion and justifies premium valuations.
Key Metric
EBITDA = Revenue − COGS − Operating Expenses  |  EBITDA Margin = EBITDA ÷ Revenue × 100
⚠️ Watch: If employee costs + SG&A are growing faster than revenue for 3+ years, the company is losing operating leverage. Ask: is the extra spending on R&D/new capacity that will pay off later (justified) or is it structural overhead creep (serious concern)?
🎯
EBITDA — The Operating Heartbeat
Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation & Amortisation
Most widely-used valuation & comparison metric
EBITDA is the single most widely-used metric in professional M&A, credit analysis, and company valuation. It answers: how much cash does the core business operations generate before financing decisions (interest), accounting conventions (depreciation), and tax policy (income tax) are applied?

By excluding depreciation and amortisation, EBITDA makes it easier to compare companies across different asset ages (a new factory vs a fully-depreciated one) and different capital structures (low-debt vs high-debt). This is why EV/EBITDA is the preferred valuation multiple for capital-intensive industries — it neutralises the effect of leverage on profitability comparisons.

Critical limitation: EBITDA ignores capex. A steel plant requires ₹5,000 Cr of annual maintenance capex just to keep existing capacity running. EBITDA says ₹3,000 Cr profit. But after spending ₹5,000 Cr on replacing ageing equipment, the true free cash flow is deeply negative. This is why capital-light businesses (Nestle, Asian Paints, IT companies) with identical EBITDA margins are far more valuable than capital-heavy ones (steel, cement, power) — their EBITDA actually converts to free cash flow.
Valuation Use
EV/EBITDA = Enterprise Value ÷ EBITDA  |  EBITDA Margin target: >15% for most manufacturers
✅ EBITDA above 15% margin for a manufacturer = good. Above 20% = excellent pricing power or operating leverage. Below 8% = very thin buffer — any revenue decline can wipe out profitability entirely.
💎
Net Profit (PAT)
Profit After Tax · Bottom Line · Basis for EPS
= EBITDA − Depreciation − Interest − Income Tax
Net Profit (also called PAT — Profit After Tax) is the ultimate bottom line: what's left of revenue after every single expense, interest payment, depreciation charge, and income tax has been deducted. This is the number that gets divided by shares outstanding to calculate Earnings Per Share (EPS) — the basis for most retail investor decisions.

However, PAT can be manipulated by accounting choices in ways that EBITDA and gross profit cannot. Capitalising operating expenses (showing opex as capex to avoid hitting the P&L), aggressive revenue recognition, understating provisions, or reversing earlier provisions can all inflate PAT in a given year without corresponding cash.

The most important quality check: Operating Cash Flow vs PAT. If a company reports ₹1,000 Cr PAT for 3 consecutive years but Operating Cash Flow (from the cash flow statement) averages only ₹400 Cr — something is wrong. Either receivables are building up (customers not paying), inventory is piling up (goods not selling), or earnings are being recognised before cash is actually collected. Persistent PAT > OCF gap is the single clearest signal of potential earnings quality issues.
Quality Check
OCF/PAT Ratio: Target >0.8× (OCF should be at least 80% of PAT for quality earnings)
🚨 Red Flag: PAT consistently 2–3× higher than Operating Cash Flow = earnings may be accounting-driven rather than cash-backed. Always cross-check with the cash flow statement before trusting a P&L number.
💡
Other Income
Non-operating income · Dividends · Interest · Forex gains
Non-core, often non-recurring — separate from operating profit
Other Income includes income from sources outside the company's primary business: interest earned on surplus cash and fixed deposits, dividends received from subsidiaries or equity investments, profit on sale of investments or assets, and foreign exchange translation gains. For companies with large cash holdings (TCS has ₹55,000+ Cr in investments), other income can be substantial — TCS earns ₹3,000–4,000 Cr annually just from treasury investments.

The critical rule: always separate other income from operating profit when evaluating business quality. A manufacturer reporting ₹500 Cr PAT where ₹300 Cr comes from other income and only ₹200 Cr from core operations has very thin operating profitability. The core business might struggle while financial engineering masks the weakness.

High other income as % of total PAT is acceptable for cash-rich companies like TCS or Infosys (cash-generation is a strength). It's a red flag for capital-light companies that should be deploying cash more productively — or for companies where "other income" includes one-time asset sales that inflate a single year's PAT without recurring value.
Quality Check
Operating Profit = EBITDA (excludes other income)  |  If Other Income >25% of PAT → investigate source
⚠️ Watch: Other income propping up PAT can hide a weakening core business. Always calculate "Operating PAT" = Total PAT − Other Income × (1 − Tax Rate) to see the true health of core operations.
03 — What to Look For

Healthy P&L Signals vs Red Flags

These are the checks every fundamental analyst runs on any manufacturing company's P&L before forming an investment view.

✅ Healthy P&L Signals
📈

Revenue Growing 12–20% CAGR for 5+ Years

Consistent double-digit growth through multiple economic cycles (including one slowdown year) proves structural demand for the company's products — not just cyclical tailwind. Polycab's 5-year revenue CAGR of ~19% shows India's infrastructure and real estate boom creating durable demand for wires and cables.

💪

Expanding or Stable Gross Margin Despite Input Cost Inflation

When copper prices rise 20% but the cable company's gross margin holds at 25%, it signals that the company passed on costs to customers — pricing power in action. Companies with strong brands (Polycab, Asian Paints) can protect margins; commodity manufacturers cannot. Expanding gross margin over 5 years = genuine pricing power improvement.

⚡

EBITDA Growing Faster Than Revenue (Operating Leverage)

If revenue grows 20% but EBITDA grows 30%, operating leverage is at work — fixed costs are spreading across a larger revenue base, driving margin expansion. This is the ideal P&L pattern for a scaling manufacturer: margins improve as the business grows, creating an accelerating compounding effect on profits.

💰

Operating Cash Flow ≥ 80% of Net Profit

When OCF consistently equals or exceeds PAT, earnings are cash-backed and real — not accounting constructs. The best manufacturers (Nestle India, Asian Paints) routinely show OCF above 100% of PAT because their negative working capital model means cash comes in before products are delivered. This is the gold standard of earnings quality.

🚨 P&L Red Flags
📉

Revenue Flat or Declining Over 3+ Years

A manufacturing company with flat revenue over 3 years while the industry grows is losing market share — the most dangerous trend for long-term equity value. Revenue decline in an industry growing 15% means the company is falling behind by 15% per year. Check: is the decline from competition, product obsolescence, or management failure?

🗜️

Persistent Gross Margin Compression

Gross margin declining 2–3% annually for 3+ years signals a structural loss of pricing power — customers are switching to competitors or the company is discounting to retain sales. This is more dangerous than a single-year compression from raw material inflation, because recovery from structural pricing loss requires either major product innovation or market repositioning.

💸

Other Income >30% of Total PAT

When "other income" (interest, dividends, asset sales) contributes more than 30% of reported PAT, the core business is generating far less than the headline profit suggests. This is especially concerning for pure-play manufacturers — a steel company's profitability should come from selling steel, not from selling assets or earning bank interest on a cash hoard.

🔍

PAT Consistently Much Higher Than Operating Cash Flow

PAT/OCF ratio consistently above 1.5× (PAT is 50%+ more than cash generated) is the most serious earnings quality alarm. Check: are receivables building up (customers not paying)? Is inventory surging (products not selling)? Are large expenses being capitalised to avoid hitting the P&L? Each of these explains the PAT-OCF gap and worsens over time if uncorrected.

The Golden Rule of P&L Analysis
Track Margins Over 5 Years — Not Just Latest Year
The single biggest mistake in P&L analysis is judging a company by one year's results. Polycab's FY22 EBITDA margin (10.5%) looked weak — but it was a commodity spike year when copper costs surged. The 5-year trend showed consistent improvement from 11.8% to 13–14%. Rain Industries' FY22 EBITDA (15.2%) looked solid — but the 5-year trend showed a structurally cyclical business that can't sustain margins. Always open Screener.in, click Profit & Loss, and examine margins over 5–10 years. Expanding margins = quality business gaining leverage. Contracting margins = structural problems. Volatile margins = cyclical business requiring cycle-aware valuation, not PE-based valuation. The pattern tells you more than any single number.
