01 — Key Learning Outcome

The PAT-to-CFO Bridge: Every Step Explained

🌉
Your Core Takeaway
Operating Cash Flow (CFO) and Net Profit (PAT) start from the same number but diverge due to three types of adjustments: (1) non-cash charges added back, (2) working capital changes, and (3) other operating cash items. The gap between them is the most important quality signal in all of accounting.
Think of it this way: your P&L is like your salary slip — it shows what you earned. Your bank account is CFO — it shows what you actually received. If your salary slip shows ₹1 lakh but your bank received only ₹30,000 — something is wrong. Maybe your employer deducted loan repayments, or promised to pay part next month. The same logic applies to companies. Accountants use accrual basis — they record revenue when earned and expenses when incurred, not when cash actually changes hands. This is perfectly legal and standard practice. But it means PAT can diverge significantly from actual cash collected. The reconciliation from PAT to CFO — called the "indirect method" cash flow statement — shows you every single reason for that gap, item by item. Mastering this bridge lets you detect earnings manipulation, working capital deterioration, and quality problems that the P&L alone will never reveal.
📊 PAT = Accrual profit 💵 CFO = Real cash collected ⚙️ D&A = Non-cash add-back 📦 WC changes = Key driver
02 — The PAT to CFO Bridge

How Net Profit Becomes Operating Cash Flow

The indirect method starts with PAT and makes specific adjustments. Each adjustment has a clear economic meaning. Once you understand the logic, you can read any company's cash flow statement in minutes.

Starting from PAT (₹500 Cr), add back non-cash D&A (₹80 Cr), subtract working capital drains from inventory build (−₹120 Cr) and receivables growth (−₹90 Cr), add supplier credit received (+₹60 Cr), and subtract taxes actually paid (−₹80 Cr) to arrive at CFO of ₹350 Cr. The gap of ₹150 Cr is entirely explained by working capital changes.

Working Capital Components — What Each Means
📦
Inventory Change
↑ Rise = Cash OUTFLOW
When inventory increases, the company spent cash buying raw materials or produced goods that haven't been sold yet. This cash went out but didn't hit the P&L as an expense until goods are sold. Conversely, falling inventory (selling old stock) is a cash inflow not captured in PAT.
Inventory +₹200 Cr → CFO reduced by ₹200 Cr despite same PAT. Demand slowdown warning.
🧾
Trade Receivables Change
↑ Rise = Cash OUTFLOW
When receivables increase, the company booked more revenue (P&L looks good) but customers haven't paid yet. The cash is owed but not in the bank. Rising receivables relative to revenue = customers taking longer to pay, or aggressive revenue recognition. This is the most common source of CFO-PAT divergence.
Receivables +₹150 Cr with flat revenue → 60+ debtor days → serious collection risk.
🤝
Trade Payables Change
↑ Rise = Cash INFLOW
When payables increase, the company bought goods or services but hasn't paid suppliers yet. This delays cash going out, boosting CFO. It's not free money — it's deferred payment. Companies with strong brands and market power (Nestle, Asian Paints) routinely run high payables — their suppliers can't say no to extending credit terms.
Payables +₹80 Cr → CFO boosted by ₹80 Cr. Supplier power signal. Watch if payables grow faster than purchases — may signal financial stress.
🏗️
Depreciation & Amortisation
Always ADDED BACK
Depreciation is recorded as an expense in the P&L (reducing PAT) but absolutely no cash leaves the bank account for depreciation. The cash was spent when the asset was bought (shown in CFI). Adding back D&A in CFO corrects for this: the P&L charged it, but cash wasn't spent in this period. This is always the largest single add-back for manufacturing companies.
Capital-heavy steel/cement: D&A ₹2,000+ Cr added back annually. Makes CFO significantly higher than PAT in asset-heavy businesses.
💸
Taxes Paid (Cash)
Actual payment = OUTFLOW
The P&L shows a "tax expense" based on accounting rules — including deferred taxes. But CFO shows taxes actually paid in cash this period. These often differ because of advance tax payments, tax refunds, and timing of settlements. The difference creates another PAT-to-CFO gap. When CFO tax is much higher than P&L tax expense, the company may have paid advance taxes — temporary drag.
Advance tax payments in Q1–Q3 create temporary CFO suppression — normalises by year end. Always check annual data, not quarterly.
⚙️
Other Operating Adjustments
Various signs · Read each
Includes: interest received (added to CFO in some formats), unrealised forex gains/losses (reverse non-cash), provision for doubtful debts (non-cash expense, add back), employee ESOP expense (non-cash, add back), and other non-cash P&L items. Each must be evaluated individually. The rule: any P&L item that didn't involve actual cash movement gets reversed here.
ESOP expense ₹50 Cr in P&L → add back in CFO (no cash paid — shares issued). Forex loss ₹30 Cr unrealised → add back in CFO (no cash left).

✅ Nestle's CFO exceeds PAT every single year — the rarest and most valuable cash flow pattern. Why? Negative working capital: Nestle's supermarket retailers pay within 1–5 days of receiving products, but Nestle pays its dairy farmers and suppliers on 30–45 day terms. This means Nestle collects cash before it pays for inputs — making every unit sold generate cash before the cost is even incurred. The D&A add-back (₹308–496 Cr) further boosts CFO. Net WC is occasionally a small drag (when inventory rises temporarily) but the structural payables advantage always results in CFO > PAT. This 1.10–1.17× ratio is the gold standard of manufacturing cash quality.

✅ FY22's CFO/PAT of 0.83× was a one-time copper-driven event — not structural deterioration. Copper prices rose 35% in FY22 as global supply chains disrupted. Polycab, to protect itself from further rises, pre-purchased large quantities — inventory jumped ₹385 Cr beyond what was needed for immediate production. This was a working capital spike, not an earnings quality problem. By FY23, copper prices normalised and the inventory was consumed, CFO recovered to 0.98× PAT. This example teaches an important lesson: always differentiate between structural WC deterioration (receivables rising year after year = quality problem) and tactical WC changes (one-time inventory build from commodity hedging = fine). The trend across 5 years (average 0.95×) shows solid quality.

The result: more cash leaves for working capital each year than enters from operations. The PAT figure (₹172–216 Cr) is entirely an accounting construct — not a single rupee of it is available as real cash. This is the clearest possible illustration of why CFO/PAT is the single most important quality ratio in fundamental analysis.

The Master Insight
The PAT-to-CFO Bridge Tells You Where the Business Really Stands
Across these five examples, one pattern is consistent: high-quality businesses (Nestle, Maruti, Polycab, Sun Pharma) have CFO/PAT ratios consistently at 0.85× or above, with WC changes driven by predictable business dynamics — not structural deterioration. Low-quality situations (HFCL) show PAT that looks positive but CFO that is deeply negative, driven by receivables that never convert to cash. The most powerful investor habit you can build: for every company you research, calculate the 5-year average CFO/PAT ratio before looking at anything else. If it's below 0.75× without a clear one-time explanation, the reported profits are largely fictional — accounting constructs that will eventually be corrected by write-offs, lower future PAT, or outright collapse.
