01 — Key Learning Outcome

FCF: What You Really Own When You Buy a Stock

💎
Your Core Takeaway
Free Cash Flow (FCF) = Operating Cash Flow minus Capital Expenditure. It is the cash left over after a company has maintained and grown its business — money that could theoretically be distributed to shareholders immediately without harming future earnings power.
When you buy a share of a company, you are buying a proportional claim on its future Free Cash Flows — not its reported profits. PAT can be inflated by capitalising expenses, understating provisions, or booking revenue before cash arrives. FCF is far harder to manufacture: cash either enters or leaves the bank. Think of FCF like the "take-home pay" of a business. Gross salary is Revenue. Tax deductions and expenses are costs. What's left is PAT (salary credit on the slip). But if you've spent money on EMI for a machine this month (CapEx), that EMI comes out of your actual bank account. What remains after EMI is your real disposable income — that's FCF. Warren Buffett called it "owner's earnings" — the actual return an owner receives for holding the business. A company compounding FCF at 15% annually for 10 years doubles its intrinsic value approximately 4×. This compounding of FCF is what creates wealth in the stock market.
💎 FCF = Owner's Earnings 📊 Base for DCF Valuation 📈 FCF Yield = Real Return ⚠️ PAT can mislead; FCF doesn't
02 — How to Calculate FCF

Three Formulas, One Truth

FCF has multiple definitions depending on the context — equity valuation, debt analysis, or management benchmarking. Here are the three versions every fundamental analyst must know.

Basic FCF — Most Used
FCF = CFO − Capital Expenditure
The simplest and most widely-used definition. CFO from the Cash Flow Statement minus CapEx (Purchase of Fixed Assets). Used for FCF Yield calculation and FCF/PAT quality check. Available directly from Screener.in in 30 seconds.
Levered FCF — Equity Value
LFCF = CFO − CapEx − Interest Paid − Debt Repayment
The cash available specifically to equity shareholders after debt obligations are met. Used in equity DCF valuation. If LFCF is negative while FCF is positive, the debt burden is consuming all free cash generation — shareholder value is shrinking.
FCF Yield — The Valuation Check
FCF Yield = (FCF ÷ Market Cap) × 100
Compares FCF to market price. FCF Yield above 4–5% = reasonable for quality manufacturer. Above 7% = attractively priced. Below 2% = expensive — you're paying a large premium and need high FCF growth to justify. Compare to 10-yr bond yield (~7%) for context.

The factory generates ₹1,000 Cr operating cash, spends ₹300 Cr on machines and expansion (CapEx), and is left with ₹700 Cr FCF — the true owner's earnings. Management then decides: pay dividends, buy back shares, repay debt, or reinvest in growth.

Why FCF Matters — Four Critical Uses
🎯
DCF Valuation Foundation
Primary valuation input
The Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) model is the theoretical bedrock of equity valuation. It states that a company's intrinsic value equals the present value of all future Free Cash Flows, discounted at the company's Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC).

In practice: project FCF for 5–10 years using growth assumptions, add a terminal value (FCF in perpetuity at a long-run growth rate), then discount back to today. If the resulting intrinsic value exceeds the current market price — the stock may be undervalued. If market price exceeds intrinsic value significantly — potential overvaluation.

The key sensitivity: FCF growth rate assumption drives most of the value. A company growing FCF 5% vs 15% annually will have a dramatically different DCF output. This is why high-FCF-growth companies (Nestle, Asian Paints in good years) command 40–80× earnings — the market is paying for compounded future FCF growth, not just current year earnings.
Simple DCF: Company with FCF ₹500 Cr growing at 12% for 10 years, then 4% terminal, WACC 10%. Intrinsic value ≈ ₹10,000–12,000 Cr. If market cap is ₹8,000 Cr → potential 25–50% upside.
📊
FCF Yield — Quick Valuation Screen
FCF ÷ Market Cap × 100
FCF Yield is the fastest valuation shortcut: it tells you what percentage of a company's market price is returned each year as genuine free cash. Compare it to bond yields for context — if a 10-year government bond yields 7%, a quality stock offering 7%+ FCF yield is likely attractively priced.

FCF Yield bands for manufacturing companies:
Above 7%: Potentially undervalued — check why the market hasn't rerated (cyclical trough, temporary problem, sector headwind).
4–7%: Reasonably valued for quality business.
2–4%: Fairly priced with growth expectations embedded.
Below 2%: Richly valued — market is pricing in significant future FCF growth. Any growth disappointment causes sharp correction.
Polycab FCF ~₹1,800 Cr on Market Cap ~₹70,000 Cr = ~2.6% FCF Yield. Moderate, reflecting growth expectations as cable demand from infra boom continues. Tata Steel FCF volatile — yield swings from 8% (cycle peak) to negative (cycle trough).
📈
FCF Growth — The Compounding Engine
FCF CAGR > 15% = wealth machine
A company that compounds FCF at 15% annually doubles its intrinsic value every 5 years. Over 15 years, it creates approximately 8× its original value — entirely through organic cash generation without needing new equity or debt. This FCF compounding is the mathematical engine behind the great long-term wealth creators in Indian manufacturing.

FCF growth comes from three sources: (1) Revenue growth — more products sold = more operating cash. (2) Margin expansion — OpEx growing slower than revenue = more EBITDA → more CFO. (3) CapEx efficiency — the same CapEx produces more revenue over time as capacity utilises fully.

Nestle India's FCF grew from ₹2,120 Cr (FY20) to ₹3,632 Cr (FY25) — a 71% increase in 5 years (CAGR ~11.5%). Polycab's FCF grew from ₹188 Cr to ₹1,800 Cr — a 10× increase (CAGR ~57%). These are the compounding dynamics that reward long-term patient investors.
FCF CAGR calculation: (Ending FCF ÷ Starting FCF)^(1/n) − 1. Polycab: (1800÷188)^(1/5) − 1 = 57% CAGR. Outstanding — driven by revenue scaling 3× while CapEx intensity fell from 3.1% to 2.2% of revenue.
🔍
FCF/PAT — Earnings Quality Check
FCF ÷ PAT: target >0.7×
Dividing FCF by PAT reveals what fraction of reported profits actually converted to free cash. This is a powerful earnings quality check that catches problems the P&L hides.

If FCF/PAT is consistently above 0.8× — earnings are genuine, working capital is well-managed, and CapEx intensity is appropriate. If FCF/PAT is below 0.5× for multiple years — investigate: either CapEx is very high (capital-intensive cycle), working capital is ballooning (HFCL-type B2G trap), or earnings are being manufactured through accounting choices.

Note: this ratio can legitimately be low during a major CapEx cycle (building a new plant) — don't penalise companies in genuine productive expansion phases. The key is whether FCF/PAT recovers and improves once the plant comes online.
HFCL: FCF negative while PAT positive every year → FCF/PAT far below zero → pure earnings quality failure. Nestle: FCF consistently 90–110% of PAT → FCF/PAT ratio 0.90–1.10× → gold-standard quality. Use FCF/PAT as a first-pass quality filter for every company.
03 — FCF Quality Signals

Positive FCF Patterns vs FCF Red Flags

Reading FCF trends correctly separates genuine compounders from value traps. These are the four patterns that matter most in manufacturing sector analysis.

✅ High-Quality FCF Patterns
💎

FCF Positive and Growing for 5+ Years

A manufacturing company that generates positive, growing FCF across multiple economic cycles — including at least one industry downturn — has proven its business model works in all conditions. Nestle India, Polycab, Asian Paints, and Maruti all achieved this. This 5-year positive track record is the single most reliable FCF quality confirmation available.

📉

CapEx/Revenue Declining as Business Scales

When a company grows revenue 20% annually but CapEx grows only 10%, CapEx intensity (CapEx/Revenue) declines — meaning each unit of revenue requires less capital investment over time. This is operating leverage in its purest form and a direct driver of FCF expansion. Polycab's CapEx intensity dropped from 3.1% to 2.2% of revenue as factories built in 2020–23 reached full utilisation by 2025.

🔄

FCF Conversion Ratio (FCF/EBITDA) Above 50%

FCF/EBITDA measures what fraction of operating earnings converts to free cash after CapEx. Above 60% = asset-light, excellent. Nestle's ratio: ₹3,632 Cr FCF ÷ ₹5,186 Cr EBITDA = 70% — outstanding. Tata Steel at peak: 40% (heavy capex). At cycle trough: negative. The FCF/EBITDA ratio immediately reveals how much of "operating profit" is consumed by maintaining and growing the asset base.

📊

FCF Yield Expanding Over Time (Rerate Signal)

When FCF yield expands (FCF growing faster than market cap) — the stock is becoming cheaper in fundamental terms even as the share price may be flat or rising. This "natural rerate" happens when FCF compounding makes the current price seem increasingly reasonable relative to growing cash generation. Polycab's FCF growing 10× in 5 years created this dynamic for patient holders.

🚨 FCF Red Flags
📉

PAT Growing While FCF is Flat or Declining

This is the most dangerous FCF divergence: reported earnings rise every year but the actual cash generated doesn't follow. The widening gap is being filled by receivables buildup, inventory accumulation, or capitalised expenses — all of which will eventually reverse and force PAT lower. Companies showing this pattern for 2–3 years typically face earnings corrections 1–2 years later. HFCL showed this pattern consistently.

🏗️

CapEx Consistently Exceeding CFO (Negative FCF)

When CapEx is larger than CFO every year for 3+ years without visible new revenue-generating capacity coming online, capital is being misallocated or projects are delayed. Negative FCF that doesn't resolve within 2–3 years signals either a structurally broken business model or management that can't execute. The classic symptom: CWIP (Capital Work in Progress) on the balance sheet stays large for years without converting to productive PP&E.

💸

Negative FCF + Debt Rising + Dividends Paid

The most dangerous combination: the company generates negative FCF (CapEx exceeds operations), is raising new debt, AND paying dividends. This triple negative means the company is borrowing to fund both its investment and its shareholder distributions — a fundamentally unsustainable financial profile that ends either in dividend cuts, equity dilution, or debt restructuring.

📊

FCF Highly Volatile (Cyclical Trap)

A company whose FCF swings from ₹5,000 Cr positive to ₹2,000 Cr negative across a 3-year commodity cycle cannot be valued using a DCF model — because the "steady state" FCF is undefined. Rain Industries, Tata Steel in bad years: FCF volatility of ±₹10,000 Cr makes any valuation based on "normalised FCF" an educated guess. Always identify whether FCF volatility is cyclical (business dependent on commodity prices) or structural (business model breaking down).

The Master FCF Insight
FCF Is the Only Number That Cannot Be Faked Over Multiple Years
Management teams have dozens of accounting tools to smooth, boost, or delay PAT recognition across quarters. But cash in the bank account is binary — either the money is there or it isn't. Across our five examples, the single clearest differentiator between wealth-creating and wealth-destroying companies is sustained positive FCF. Nestle, Polycab, Asian Paints, and Maruti generate real cash that compounds for shareholders. HFCL reports profits that never arrive as cash — and has borrowed ₹2,000+ Cr over 5 years to fund the gap between its accounting profits and its actual cash reality. The FCF/PAT ratio over 5 years is the most reliable single-number quality filter in all of fundamental analysis. Calculate it for any company in 60 seconds on Screener.in. If it's consistently below 0.6×, the business is consuming more cash than it generates — regardless of what the P&L headline says.
