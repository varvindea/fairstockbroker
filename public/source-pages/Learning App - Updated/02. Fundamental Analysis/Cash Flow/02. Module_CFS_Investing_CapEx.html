01 — Key Learning Outcome

CapEx-Heavy vs Asset-Light: The Most Important Business Model Difference

🏗️
Your Core Takeaway
Investing Cash Flow (CFI) shows every rupee a company spends acquiring assets or receives from selling them. It is almost always negative for a healthy growing company — and that's perfectly fine. The critical skill is distinguishing between necessary, value-creating investment versus wasteful capital destruction.
Imagine two factories. Factory A (Steel Plant) must spend ₹5,000 Cr on new blast furnaces every 10 years just to keep running — and another ₹3,000 Cr to expand. Its CFI is perpetually large and negative. Factory B (Nestle's Maggi plant) spends only ₹700 Cr total on upgrades over the same 10 years because the recipe and brand don't require expensive machinery replacement. Both factories earn ₹1,000 Cr annual profit. But Factory B generates far more Free Cash Flow because it keeps almost all of its profit — while Factory A must reinvest half or more just to maintain current output. This difference — CapEx intensity — is why consumer brands are valued at 40–60× earnings while steel companies trade at 6–8×. The CFI section of the cash flow statement reveals this fundamental difference in one look. Learn to read it and you will never overpay for a capital-hungry business.
🏭 CapEx-heavy = Low FCF ✅ Asset-light = High FCF 🤝 Acquisitions = Risk 💸 Divestments = Watch
02 — What Goes Into CFI

The Three Parts of Investing Cash Flow

Every number in the CFI section fits into one of three buckets. Each tells a different story about management's capital allocation choices and the long-term trajectory of the business.

CFI has four components: CapEx (buying assets), Acquisitions (buying companies), Divestments (selling assets/units), and Investment activity (FDs, securities). CapEx intensity — spending as % of revenue — is the single most important number for comparing business models.

CFI Components — What Each Means
🔧
Capital Expenditure (CapEx)
Always negative · Largest CFI item
CapEx is cash spent purchasing property, plant, and equipment — the machines, factories, and infrastructure that produce goods. It appears as "Purchase of Fixed Assets" in the cash flow statement. For a cable manufacturer like Polycab, CapEx funds new extrusion machines and factory expansions. For Nestle, it funds food processing equipment and packaging lines.

CapEx is not an expense in the P&L when incurred — instead it becomes an asset on the balance sheet, then gradually expensed as depreciation over its useful life. This is why CFI and the P&L diverge: a ₹500 Cr machine purchase hits CFI in full immediately, but only ₹50 Cr/year hits the P&L as depreciation for 10 years.
FCF = CFO − CapEx. A company with ₹1,000 Cr CFO and ₹800 Cr CapEx has only ₹200 Cr FCF — despite looking profitable. Maruti's ₹5,844 Cr CapEx in FY25 is high but funded from ₹16,112 Cr CFO — comfortable 2.8× coverage.
⚙️
Maintenance CapEx
Must-spend · Like rent · Non-negotiable
Maintenance CapEx is the portion of total CapEx that simply keeps existing capacity operational — replacing worn-out machines, refurbishing ageing factory infrastructure, upgrading compliance and safety systems. It creates no new revenue capacity; it merely prevents existing capacity from deteriorating.

The standard analyst estimate: Maintenance CapEx ≈ Annual Depreciation charge. If a company reports ₹300 Cr annual depreciation, it likely needs to spend ₹300 Cr just to stand still. Subtract this from total CapEx to find growth CapEx. For capital-heavy industries (steel, cement), maintenance CapEx alone can consume 80–90% of operating cash flow — leaving almost nothing for growth or shareholder returns.
Steel plant: Annual Dep ₹2,000 Cr → Maintenance CapEx ≈ ₹2,000 Cr. If total CapEx is ₹2,200 Cr, only ₹200 Cr is actually growing the business. Nestle: Dep ₹496 Cr, Total CapEx ₹848 Cr — ₹352 Cr is genuine expansion CapEx.
📈
Growth CapEx
Value-creating · Should earn >WACC
Growth CapEx is spending beyond maintenance — new factories, new production lines, new geographies. Unlike maintenance CapEx (which is obligatory), growth CapEx is a choice. Management must ensure this spending earns returns above the cost of capital (WACC — typically 10–14% for Indian manufacturers).

The key analyst test: compare growth CapEx to subsequent revenue growth. If a company spent ₹1,000 Cr in growth CapEx 2 years ago and revenue grew ₹2,000 Cr since — excellent 2× return. If revenue grew only ₹400 Cr — poor 0.4× return. Polycab's Kharkhoda and Halol plant expansions show excellent growth CapEx returns: every ₹1 invested generated ₹3–4 of incremental revenue within 2–3 years.
Growth CapEx signal: CapEx growing faster than depreciation + revenue accelerating = management investing wisely. Growth CapEx with flat revenue = capital misallocation — investigate immediately.
🤝
Acquisitions
High risk · Creates goodwill · Watch integration
Acquisitions appear in CFI as large, one-time outflows when a company purchases another business entirely. Unlike CapEx (buying specific assets), acquisitions buy the whole enterprise — including management teams, customer relationships, brand equity, and intellectual property — at a premium over book value, creating goodwill.

Acquisitions have a mixed track record globally: studies show 50–70% of acquisitions destroy value rather than create it. The premium paid is usually too high, integration costs are underestimated, and promised synergies fail to materialise. Warning signs: goodwill impairment charges in subsequent years (Suzlon's ₹2,864 Cr → ₹360 Cr goodwill collapse), revenue stagnation post-acquisition, and management distraction from the core business.
Watch: repeated acquisitions without FCF improvement. Sun Pharma's Ranbaxy acquisition (2014) and Taro acquisition are examples where pharma companies built global presence through acquisitions — some successful, some problematic. Always check goodwill as % of total assets post-acquisition.
💰
Divestments / Asset Sales
Makes CFI positive · Strategic OR distress
Divestments occur when a company sells a subsidiary, a piece of land, a factory, or any long-term asset. They create a positive cash inflow in CFI — and they appear in the P&L as a "profit on sale of assets" (one-time, non-recurring gain that inflates PAT).

Context is everything: Strategic divestment — Selling a non-core business to focus on strengths. Tata Group sold many businesses to fund its core operations and pay down debt strategically. Distress divestment — Selling productive assets to generate cash because CFO is insufficient. Jaiprakash Associates sold cement plants, power assets, and real estate year after year — not from strategy, but from desperation. If CFI is positive every year only because of divestments, the business cannot self-fund and is consuming its own asset base.
Red flag: CFI positive for 3+ consecutive years without clear strategic rationale. The company is liquidating assets, not growing. Calculate: do total assets grow despite divestments? If assets are shrinking → asset stripping → serious distress signal.
🏦
Financial Investments
Treasury activity · Large in cash-rich companies
Many large manufacturers invest surplus cash in financial instruments — fixed deposits, government bonds, mutual funds, and equity stakes in other companies. These appear in CFI as large outflows (when investing) or inflows (when liquidating). This can make CFI look very negative even for cash-rich companies like Maruti Suzuki.

Maruti's CFI in FY25 was −₹13,956 Cr — but only ₹5,844 Cr was actual CapEx. The remaining ~₹8,112 Cr was deploying surplus cash into short-term instruments (FDs, treasury bonds). This is NOT a sign of heavy capital intensity — it's actually the opposite: so much free cash that the company runs out of productive investment opportunities and parks money in safe instruments. Always separate CapEx from financial investments when analysing CFI.
Maruti's ₹40,000 Cr treasury generates ₹3,000–4,000 Cr "other income" annually — essentially a parallel finance business. Nestle, Asian Paints also hold large current investments for the same reason. Separation: True CapEx intensity = CapEx ÷ Revenue (not total CFI ÷ Revenue).

A steel company earns ₹1,000 Cr EBITDA but must spend ₹600 Cr on maintenance CapEx (replacing blast furnace linings, rolling mill equipment, pollution control upgrades) just to keep running at current capacity. Another ₹400 Cr in growth CapEx adds new rolling mill. FCF = ₹1,000 Cr − ₹1,000 Cr total CapEx = ₹0. The business earns well on paper but generates nothing after reinvestment.

These businesses are highly sensitive to commodity cycles, are difficult to price competitively, require deep domain expertise to manage, and offer little operational flexibility when demand falls because costs are largely fixed.
🚨 Valuation trap: A 6× P/E on a steel company sounds cheap — but if true FCF (after maintenance CapEx) is only 40% of reported earnings, the real P/FCF is 15× — not cheap at all. Always look at FCF, not PAT, for capital-heavy businesses.

The Master CFI Rule
CapEx/Revenue Ratio — The Single Most Revealing Number in CFI
Across these five examples, CapEx/Revenue is the single most powerful number in the Investing Cash Flow section. Nestle at 4.2% generates FCF nearly equal to EBITDA. Polycab at 2.2% (and declining!) shows a business that needs less capital as it scales. Maruti at 3.7% (true CapEx only) funds a massive SUV expansion from operational surplus. Tata Steel at 8.3% consumes a third of EBITDA just in maintenance. Jaiprakash's CapEx declining toward zero is the clearest sign that management has given up investing in the future. On Screener.in, find "Purchase of Fixed Assets" in the Cash Flow tab, divide by total revenue from the P&L tab, and compare across 5 years. A declining ratio with rising revenue = operating leverage in action. A rising ratio with flat revenue = capital misallocation. This 30-second check will screen out most value traps before you spend another hour on analysis.
