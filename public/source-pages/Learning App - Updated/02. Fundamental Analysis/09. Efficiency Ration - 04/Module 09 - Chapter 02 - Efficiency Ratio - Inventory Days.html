Inventory Days (also called Days Inventory Outstanding or DIO) measures exactly that — the average number of days inventory stays unsold. Every day on the shelf has a cost.

01 — Key Learning Outcome

What Will You Learn From This?

📦
Your Core Takeaway
Inventory Days tells you how many days on average it takes for a company's inventory — raw materials, work-in-progress, and finished goods — to be sold and converted into revenue. Lower days means faster stock movement and better cash efficiency.
Every rupee of inventory sitting on a warehouse shelf is cash that the company has spent but not yet recovered. If a company holds ₹120 Cr of inventory and makes ₹600 Cr of sales per year, it holds roughly 73 days of sales in stock at any time. This is Inventory Days. A fast-moving FMCG company like HUL might need only 30–40 days of inventory on hand. A heavy manufacturer making custom industrial equipment might hold 150+ days — because the production cycle simply takes longer. The key insight is that Inventory Days must always be judged against the specific business model and sector. A rising trend in Inventory Days (across 3–5 years) is a warning signal — it may mean slowing sales, production inefficiencies, or quality problems causing returns. 02 — The Formula Explained

How to Calculate Inventory Days

The formula divides the average inventory by the cost of goods sold, then multiplies by 365. This converts a ratio into days — making it intuitive and directly comparable across companies and time periods.

1

Why COGS, Not Revenue?

Inventory is valued at cost (what was spent to produce or purchase it), not at the selling price. So it must be compared to COGS — the cost-based measure of goods sold — not revenue (which includes profit margin). Using revenue would artificially inflate Inventory Days by comparing a cost figure to a selling price figure. COGS gives you a true apples-to-apples comparison.

2

What "Average Inventory" Means

Inventory can fluctuate significantly during the year — companies often stock up before festivals, then run down stock post-sale. Using year-end inventory alone can give a misleading picture. Average Inventory = (Opening Inventory + Closing Inventory) ÷ 2. This smooths seasonal spikes and gives a more representative view of how much stock the company typically holds throughout the year.

3

What "Inventory Turnover" Is — and How It Relates

Inventory Turnover = COGS ÷ Average Inventory. It's the inverse of the Days calculation: a company with Inventory Days of 73 has an Inventory Turnover of 365 ÷ 73 = 5.0×. This means it "turns over" (sells and replaces) its entire stock 5 times a year. Both metrics convey the same information — Days is more intuitive for most people; Turnover is used more in wholesale and retail analysis.

4

A Rising Inventory Days Trend Is a Warning

If a company's Inventory Days rises from 60 to 80 to 110 over three years, that's a significant efficiency deterioration. Possible reasons: sales slowing down while production continues at the same pace; quality problems causing returns; product lines becoming obsolete; or aggressive overproduction. Any of these deserve investigation — rising inventory is one of the earliest signals of business stress, often appearing 2–3 quarters before it shows in profit numbers.

03 — How to Interpret Inventory Days

What Each Range Tells You

Inventory Days is directly meaningful — it's measured in calendar days. Here's what each range signals about operational efficiency and cash management.

💚
< 30 days

Exceptional — Near-Instant Stock Turnover

Products sell almost as fast as they arrive. Typical of FMCG (soaps, biscuits, tea) and fresh food retail. Very low inventory means very low working capital tied up in stock — the company operates lean and generates cash rapidly. Risk: any supply chain disruption can cause stockouts and lost sales.

🟢
30–75 days

Healthy — Efficient Stock Management

Standard for consumer goods, retail, and light manufacturing. The company holds roughly 1–2.5 months of stock — enough buffer to handle demand fluctuations without excess. This range signals disciplined procurement, good demand forecasting, and lean operations. Most well-run Indian companies target this range.

🟡
75–120 days

Moderate — Acceptable for Complex Businesses

Expected for pharma, automobile components, and speciality chemicals where production cycles and regulatory requirements create naturally longer inventory holding. Acceptable if stable. If this range is reached by a company that was previously in the 30–75 range, investigate the cause.

🔴
120–180 days

High — Significant Working Capital Strain

3–6 months of production held as stock. Typical for heavy manufacturing (steel, cement), engineering goods, and infrastructure-linked businesses where custom orders take long to complete. For companies that were managing at lower days before, this range signals deteriorating demand or overproduction. Working capital requirements are substantial.

💀
> 180 days

Danger — Severe Buildup or Structural Issue

6+ months of stock unsold is a major red flag outside of heavy industry. It suggests products may be uncompetitive, pricing is out of market, there's been severe demand destruction, or management is hiding inventory write-down risks. Always check if inventory is valued appropriately — in distress situations, book value can far exceed market realisable value.

Soaps, shampoos, biscuits, tea — products that consumers buy every week. HUL, Dabur, Nestlé hold minimal stock because demand is predictable and constant. Short shelf lives (especially food) mean fast turnover is essential. A 30-day inventory in FMCG is a sign of exceptional execution.

Retail
Ideal:30–90 days
Retailers hold a mix of fast and slow-moving SKUs. DMart holds ~35 days — its every-day-low-price model drives constant high volumes. Fashion retailers hold more because seasonal items can take time. Watch for retailers with rising inventory especially after major stock purchases.
🚗
Automobile
Ideal:30–75 days
Maruti, Tata Motors, and Bajaj Auto manage complex supply chains with hundreds of components. JIT (Just-in-Time) production helps keep inventory lean. The 30–75 day range is healthy for assembled vehicles. Watch for excess inventory at dealers — it signals demand weakness before it appears in company numbers.
💊
Pharma
Ideal:60–120 days
Pharmaceutical companies must hold safety stock due to regulatory requirements, long batch testing timelines, and the critical nature of supply continuity. API (Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient) production cycles can take weeks. Finished dosage forms must pass QC before release. 60–120 days is structurally normal and often mandated by good manufacturing practices.
🏭
Heavy Mfg
Ideal:90–180 days
Steel plants, engineering goods, industrial equipment — production cycles are long, raw materials (iron ore, coal) are bought in bulk for cost efficiency, and finished goods may be custom-ordered. L&T, BHEL, and Tata Steel hold months of stock by necessity. The question isn't whether days are high — it's whether they're rising unexpectedly.

Tata Steel's Inventory Days of 65–85 days (which fluctuates with the commodity cycle) is in the lower range for integrated steel manufacturers — and reflects the inherently complex nature of steelmaking. The production process starts with raw materials: iron ore, coking coal, and limestone must be purchased in bulk (often by the shipload) and stored before the blast furnace can begin. From raw material to finished hot-rolled coil or long products takes days of continuous processing through blast furnaces, basic oxygen furnaces, and rolling mills. Finished steel then needs to be shipped to customers, who may themselves have 30–60 day payment terms. Tata Steel's relatively efficient position versus the sector (180 days would not be unusual for some steel companies) reflects its scale advantages, integrated operations, and the fact that its Indian operations are more efficient than its legacy European operations (Tata Steel Europe historically ran at much higher inventory levels). For steel companies, the key is to watch Inventory Days through the commodity cycle — when steel prices are rising and demand is strong, companies reduce inventory days by selling quickly; when the cycle turns, inventory builds up as production continues but demand slows.

06 — How to Apply Inventory Days

3 Smart Ways to Use This Ratio

Inventory Days is most powerful as an early warning tool and efficiency comparator — used correctly, it reveals operational health that profit statements often obscure.

📈
Track the Trend — Rising Days = Early Warning
A single year's figure is less useful than its direction over 3–5 years. If a company's Inventory Days rises from 55 to 70 to 95, that's a significant trend that often precedes revenue disappointments. Companies build inventory in anticipation of sales — when those sales don't materialise, inventory rises before reported revenue falls. This makes Inventory Days one of the most useful leading indicators of business deterioration.
⚖️
Compare Within Sector Only
A pharma company's 90-day inventory is excellent. The same 90 days for an FMCG company is alarming. Always benchmark against 3–5 direct sector peers. The company with consistently the lowest Inventory Days in its sector usually has better demand, more efficient production, or superior supply chain management — any of which translates to lower working capital needs and higher returns on capital.
🔗
Combine with Debtor Days for the Full Picture
Inventory Days + Debtor Days − Creditor Days = Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC). The CCC tells you how long cash is tied up in operations before it returns. Companies with low Inventory Days AND low Debtor Days AND long Creditor Days effectively get interest-free financing from their supply chain — DMart is the classic Indian example. Monitoring all three together gives the complete working capital picture that no single ratio can provide alone.
⚠️
Important Caveat
Inventory Quality Matters as Much as Inventory Days
Two companies with identical Inventory Days can have very different risk profiles. One holds fresh, in-demand products easily converted to cash. The other holds aging, slow-moving stock that may require deep discounts or write-downs to clear. Always check: Are inventory values increasing faster than revenue? Are there unusual provisions against inventory in the notes? Is the company's main product in structural decline (obsolescence risk)? For example, a phone manufacturer with 90-day Inventory Days holding last year's model is far more risky than a steel company with 90-day Inventory Days holding standard-grade steel. The commodity has no obsolescence risk; the electronics product does. Read the inventory breakdown in annual report notes — finished goods vs raw materials vs WIP is important context.
