PEG Ratio P/E Adjusted for Growth Rate

Fundamental Analysis · Growth Valuation · P/E + Growth · Peter Lynch Method

A stock at 40× P/E can be cheaper than one at 15× P/E — if it's growing four times faster. P/E alone shows the price tag. PEG shows whether that price tag is justified by growth. It's the one ratio that turns "expensive" and "cheap" from feelings into mathematics.

01 — Key Learning Outcome

Why P/E Alone Can Fool You

🎯
Your Core Takeaway
The PEG Ratio fixes P/E's biggest flaw — it divides P/E by the earnings growth rate, so you can compare a fast-growing company fairly against a slow-growing one without being misled by their vastly different P/E ratios.
Imagine two used cars priced at ₹5 lakh and ₹2 lakh respectively. The cheaper car sounds like a bargain — until you find out the ₹5 lakh car can do 200 km/h and the ₹2 lakh car tops out at 40 km/h. Paying ₹5 lakh for speed might actually be the better deal. That's exactly what PEG does for stocks. A company at 40× P/E growing at 40% per year has a PEG of 1.0 — perfectly fair. A company at 15× P/E growing at 3% per year has a PEG of 5.0 — actually very expensive for its growth rate. Peter Lynch, one of the greatest investors ever, made PEG famous as his go-to screening tool: "Any company with a PEG under 1 is worth a hard look."

02 — The Formula

How to Calculate the PEG Ratio

The PEG formula is elegantly simple: take the P/E ratio and divide it by the expected annual EPS growth rate (expressed as a number, not a percentage). The result tells you whether you're paying a fair, cheap, or expensive price relative to how fast the company is growing.

Company A (P/E 40×, Growth 40%) has PEG = 1.0 — fair value. Company B (P/E 15×, Growth 3%) has PEG = 5.0 — very expensive for its growth. PEG reveals the truth P/E hides.

P/E

The Numerator — P/E Ratio

Use the standard Price-to-Earnings ratio (market price ÷ EPS). This is the starting point. A high P/E alone doesn't tell you if a stock is expensive — that depends entirely on how fast earnings are growing. A P/E of 50 for a company growing 50% is perfectly reasonable. A P/E of 12 for zero-growth is expensive.

G%

The Denominator — Earnings Growth Rate

The expected annual EPS growth rate expressed as a number (e.g., 20% growth = use 20, not 0.20). Analysts typically use 3–5 year forward consensus EPS growth estimates. The most common version is Forward PEG — using expected future growth, which better captures where the business is heading rather than where it's been.

÷

The Result — A Growth-Normalised Price

PEG tells you: "For every 1% of annual earnings growth, how many rupees of P/E premium are you paying?" A PEG of 1 means you pay 1× the growth rate — the benchmark of fair value. PEG of 0.5 means you're paying half the growth rate — potentially undervalued. PEG of 3 means you're paying 3× the growth rate — expensive for what you're getting.

03 — Interpreting the PEG Ratio

The PEG Zone Framework

Unlike P/E, which has no absolute benchmark, PEG has a natural anchor: 1.0 = fair value. Every value above or below that anchor carries a clear meaning. Peter Lynch made PEG ≈ 1 the most famous valuation benchmark in growth investing.

👴
The Father of PEG Investing
Peter Lynch's Famous PEG Rule
PEG ≈ 1 = Fairly Valued
Peter Lynch ran the Magellan Fund from 1977–1990, averaging 29.2% annual returns — the best 13-year mutual fund record in history. He popularised PEG as the primary tool for identifying growth stocks trading at reasonable prices. Lynch wrote: "The P/E ratio of any company that's fairly priced will equal its growth rate." That single sentence became the foundation of growth-at-a-reasonable-price (GARP) investing. His approach: find companies growing at 20–25% with PEG below 1 — you own the growth at a discount to its value. This framework has been used by generations of professional fund managers since.
🟢
< 1

PEG Below 1 — Potentially Undervalued, But Verify Growth Quality

A PEG below 1 means you're paying less than 1 rupee of P/E for every percent of earnings growth — theoretically attractive. However, the most common reason PEG is below 1 is that the market doesn't believe the growth estimates. If a company shows 40% expected growth but a PEG of 0.6, ask: why is the market sceptical? Is it because the growth is from one-time gains, an unsustainable acquisition, or a temporarily favourable product cycle? Verify: Is the EPS growth organic and recurring? Is it backed by improving operating cash flow? Only if the growth is genuine does PEG below 1 represent real opportunity.

🔵
≈ 1

PEG Around 1 — Fair Value by Peter Lynch's Standard

PEG of 1.0 means you're paying exactly the growth rate in P/E premium — the theoretical fair value point. For consistently growing companies with strong balance sheets and durable competitive advantages, PEG around 1 is generally an attractive entry point. The logic: if a company grows earnings at 20% annually for the next decade, and you buy at PEG 1, your investment compounds at approximately the earnings growth rate — which for 20% growers means 6× your money in a decade. That's why Lynch focused so intensely on finding stocks in the PEG 0.5–1.0 range.

🟡
> 2

PEG Above 2 — Expensive Relative to Growth, Requires Exceptional Quality

PEG above 2 means you're paying more than twice the growth rate in P/E premium. This is only justified in rare cases: companies with near-monopoly positions (pricing power that compounds), businesses with recurring revenue and extremely predictable growth visibility, or companies where current EPS dramatically understates true earning power. Some exceptional FMCG companies trade at PEG 3–5 and have rewarded patient investors — because their growth is extraordinarily predictable and the probability of disappointment is very low. But for most companies, PEG above 2 demands a very specific thesis about what makes this business worth the premium over growth.

This is the most important PEG lesson — and the most counter-intuitive one for new investors. A stock trading at 12× P/E looks obviously cheap. P/E investors say "buy it!" But PEG exposes the truth: if this company grows earnings at only 3% annually (barely above inflation), the PEG is 12 ÷ 3 = 4.0 — extremely expensive relative to growth. 06 — What to Look For

Strong PEG Signals vs Red Flags

PEG is most dangerous when used mechanically. The ratio is only as reliable as the growth estimate it's dividing by. Always interrogate the quality of the earnings growth before trusting the PEG number.

✅ Strong PEG Characteristics
📊

PEG Near or Below 1.5 with Quality Growth

The combination of reasonable PEG AND high-quality earnings growth (organic revenue expansion, improving margins, strong cash conversion) is the GARP investor's ideal. Not just any PEG below 1.5 — but one backed by a business genuinely earning that growth, not manufacturing it through accounting adjustments.

💰

Strong ROE and ROCE

Companies with high PEG but genuinely high ROE (above 20%) and ROCE (above 15%) have demonstrated they earn well on capital — the foundation of sustainable growth. ROE above cost of equity means every rupee reinvested creates value. This combination — low PEG + high ROE — is the most reliable indicator of long-term wealth creation.

📈

Consistent Earnings Growth Over 5+ Years

Five consecutive years of above-average earnings growth (15%+ annually) through multiple economic cycles — including at least one slowdown — proves the growth is structural, not cyclical. This consistency is exactly what PEG is designed to reward. A proven 5-year growth track record makes the denominator of PEG far more trustworthy.

⚡

Positive Free Cash Flow Supporting Earnings

When operating cash flow closely tracks reported EPS growth year after year, the growth is real — not accounting-driven. Free cash flow growing at or above the EPS growth rate confirms the business is genuinely compounding in value. PEG below 1.5 + strong FCF growth = Lynch's dream scenario.

🚨 PEG Red Flags — Avoid
📉

High P/E + Slowing Growth = PEG Expansion Trap

The most painful investment outcome: a company at 50× P/E where the earnings growth expectation was 30% but actual growth comes in at 8%. PEG was 1.7×, appeared reasonable — but actual PEG (using real growth) was 6.25×. Stock falls 40–60% in a "derating." Never pay high P/E without very high confidence in the growth estimate.

🎭

Aggressive Earnings Assumptions

Wall Street and Dalal Street analysts are chronically optimistic about earnings forecasts. When growth estimates are based on "management guidance" alone without independent verification, treat them with scepticism. Check the 3-year history of how accurate analyst estimates have been for this company. If estimates have consistently been revised downward — the PEG based on those estimates is meaningless.

💸

Weak Free Cash Flow Despite EPS Growth

EPS can be engineered through accounting choices (capitalising expenses, aggressive revenue recognition, changing depreciation methods). If EPS is growing but operating cash flow is flat or declining, the PEG calculation is built on a fiction. Always verify: is EPS growth translating into actual cash in the bank? EBIDTA-to-cash conversion ratio below 0.7 is a serious warning sign.

🔄

Growth Driven Only by Acquisitions

Acquisition-driven EPS growth can make PEG look attractive — but it's borrowed growth, not organic. Each acquisition requires capital, creates integration risk, and the synergies rarely materialise fully. Serial acquirers often destroy long-term value while appearing to grow EPS. Insist on organic revenue growth as the primary driver before trusting a low PEG for an acquisition-heavy company.

07 — How to Apply PEG in Practice

3 Smart Ways to Use PEG

PEG is most powerful as a screening and comparative tool — it narrows the investable universe but should never make the final investment decision alone.

🔍
Step 1: Screen for PEG Below 1.5
Start with a universe of all profitable, growing companies (earnings growth >15%). Filter for PEG below 1.5. This immediately eliminates companies where you're overpaying for growth relative to the price. The resulting shortlist is your hunting ground — companies where the market may be undervaluing the growth opportunity. Screener.in allows custom PEG filters. Most major broking platforms show both forward and trailing PEG in stock screeners.
🔬
Step 2: Verify Growth Quality
For every stock with attractive PEG, verify the growth is real. Check: (1) Is operating cash flow growing at least as fast as EPS? (2) Is the growth organic or acquisition-driven? (3) Are gross margins stable or expanding? (4) Has the company consistently delivered within 10% of analyst earnings estimates for 3+ years? Only companies passing all four checks deserve the low PEG premium — the others are PEG traps.
📈
Step 3: Use Forward PEG, Not Trailing
Trailing PEG (using past growth) is backward-looking. Forward PEG (using consensus analyst estimates for next 1–3 years) captures where the business is heading. For growing companies, forward PEG is almost always lower than trailing PEG — but only if the growth estimates are credible. Use the average of next 2–3 year EPS growth estimates, not just next year (which can be distorted by one-time items or base effects). Cross-reference with management guidance and sectoral tailwinds.
⚠️
Key Limitations
When PEG Fails — Four Situations Where It Should Not Be Used
1. Cyclical companies: At earnings peaks, cyclicals show high growth rates — making PEG falsely low and the valuation appear attractive right when it's most dangerous. JSW Steel or ONGC at peak commodity prices with PEG 0.5 is a value trap, not a bargain. 2. Loss-making firms: No earnings = no P/E = no PEG. Use P/S or EV/Revenue instead. 3. Volatile/inconsistent growers: If EPS swings from +50% to −20% every few years, there is no stable growth rate to put in the denominator. PEG becomes meaningless. 4. Very small or early-stage companies: Growth estimates for pre-scale companies are notoriously unreliable — analysts routinely overestimate by 50–100%. PEG based on these estimates creates false confidence. Always treat PEG as the starting question, never the final answer.
