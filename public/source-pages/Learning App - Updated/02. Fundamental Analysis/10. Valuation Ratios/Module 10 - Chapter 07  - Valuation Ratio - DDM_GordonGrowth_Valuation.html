01 — Key Learning Outcome

A Stock Is Worth the Sum of Its Future Dividends

💰
Your Core Takeaway
The DDM (Gordon Growth Model) calculates a stock's intrinsic value by treating it as the present value of all future dividends growing at a constant rate — giving you a mathematically derived "fair price" to compare against the market price.
Imagine you own a mango orchard. Every year the orchard pays you ₹1,000 in mangoes. Next year ₹1,060 (6% growth). Then ₹1,124. Then ₹1,191… forever, growing 6% each year. How much is that stream of mango payments worth to you today? If you want a 12% annual return on your investment, the Gordon Growth Model says: the orchard is worth ₹1,060 ÷ (12%−6%) = ₹17,667. That's the DDM in action. Replace "mangoes" with "dividends" and "orchard" with "company" — and you have one of the most fundamental valuation tools in finance. The key insight: a dividend-paying company is essentially a perpetuity — a machine that produces growing cash flows. DDM tells you what that machine is worth based on your return expectations and the company's growth rate.

02 — The Gordon Growth Formula

Breaking Down D₁ ÷ (r − g)

The Gordon Growth Model has just three inputs — but each one requires careful estimation. Small errors in any input, especially the growth rate g, can dramatically shift the intrinsic value. Understanding what each symbol means is the foundation of using DDM correctly.

The company (mango orchard) pays D₁ next year, then D₂, D₃… forever. The investor uses D₁ ÷ (r−g) to find the present value of this infinite stream. r must always exceed g.

D₁

Expected Next-Year Dividend — The Numerator

D₁ = D₀ × (1 + g). If a company paid ₹10 this year (D₀) and grows dividends at 6% annually, D₁ = ₹10.60. This is the first of an infinite stream of growing dividends. The whole valuation rests on the assumption that this growth continues forever at a constant rate — which is only realistic for truly stable, mature businesses.

r−g

The Denominator — Why (r − g) Is So Sensitive

The denominator r − g is the most critical part of the formula. A tiny change here causes enormous swings in value. If r = 12% and g = 6%, the denominator is 6% = 0.06. If g rises to 8%, denominator falls to 4% = 0.04 and the value jumps from ₹177 to ₹265 — a 50% increase from a 2% change in g. This sensitivity is why careful, conservative growth estimation is essential.

03 — Sensitivity & Growth Estimation

Why Small Changes in Growth Rate Change Everything

The DDM's greatest strength is also its greatest vulnerability. Because growth rate g is in the denominator as (r−g), even a 1–2% shift in g dramatically changes the output. This is why disciplined, conservative growth estimation is the most important skill in DDM analysis.

📐 How to Estimate Sustainable Growth Rate
g = ROE × Retention Ratio
Rather than guessing g, finance theory gives us a formula. The sustainable growth rate is what a company can grow its dividends at indefinitely, funded by its own earnings — no additional debt or equity issuance. ROE (Return on Equity) measures how efficiently the company earns on shareholders' money. Retention Ratio (= 1 − Dividend Payout Ratio) measures how much of earnings is reinvested back into the business to fuel future growth. The product of these two tells you the maximum sustainable growth rate grounded in financial reality.
ROE18%
Dividend Payout Ratio40%
Retention Ratio (1 − 40%)60%
Sustainable g = 18% × 60% = 10.8%
⚠️ Always use a long-run sustainable g, not recent peak growth. If the last 2 years were exceptional due to a commodity boom or one-time contract, those rates won't be sustainable forever. Use 5-year average ROE and a normalised payout ratio for a more defensible estimate.
r

How to Set the Required Return (r)

r represents what you demand as a minimum annual return for investing in this stock instead of a risk-free alternative. Common approaches: CAPM (Risk-Free Rate + Beta × Market Risk Premium), historical equity return expectations (~12–14% for Indian equities), or cost of equity from the WACC framework. For dividend-income investors, r is often set at 10–14% depending on the risk level of the company. Higher risk company = higher r = lower DDM value.

04 — When DDM Works (and When It Doesn't)

The Right Companies for DDM Valuation

DDM is one of the most elegant valuation models — but it has very specific requirements. Applying it to the wrong kind of company produces meaningless results. Understanding when to use it is as important as knowing how to calculate it.

✅ Excellent DDM Candidates
⚡

Utility Companies (Power, Water, Gas)

Regulated monopolies with captive customer bases. Revenue is predictable, earnings are stable, and dividend payout ratios are high and consistent. The dividend stream is as close to perpetuity as any real business can be. NTPC, Power Grid, GAIL — classic DDM candidates with predictable, growing dividends backed by long-term government contracts.

🧴

Established FMCG Companies

Companies like HUL, Nestlé India, and Britannia have paid and grown dividends consistently for decades. Their brand moats protect earnings from competitive disruption. Dividend growth is supported by steady revenue growth and high-quality cash conversion. DDM works well here because the "constant growth forever" assumption is the most realistic it can be.

🏛️

Blue-Chip Banks with Stable Payout Policies

Private sector banks with strong capital adequacy, consistent ROE above 15%, and transparent dividend policies are good DDM candidates. HDFC Bank has grown its dividend steadily for over 20 years. The key is stable earnings — a bank with volatile NPA cycles makes dividend forecasting unreliable. High-quality private sector banks with conservative lending culture fit well.

💊

Mature Pharma / Healthcare Companies

Established generic pharma companies with branded product portfolios and international licensing revenue have predictable cash flows. Companies like Abbott India or Pfizer India — mature, profitable, consistent dividend payers — fit the DDM framework reasonably well when their dividend payout ratios are stable over multiple years.

⚠️ Poor DDM Candidates
🚀

Tech Startups and New-Age Companies

Zomato, Paytm, Nykaa pay zero dividends. Their value comes entirely from future growth and eventual profitability — not current cash distributions. Applying DDM gives a value of zero (or undefined) — which is mathematically correct but commercially useless. Use P/S or EV/Revenue instead for these companies.

🔩

Cyclical Industries (Metals, Commodities)

Steel, aluminium, and commodity companies pay large dividends at earnings peaks and cut them sharply during downturns. The "constant growth forever" assumption is violated every 3–5 years when the commodity cycle turns. Plugging peak dividends into DDM produces wildly optimistic values right before the crash.

🌱

High-Reinvestment Growth Businesses

Companies that reinvest almost all earnings for growth (like D-Mart, Page Industries in growth phases) pay negligible dividends not because they can't afford them but because they've found better uses for capital internally. DDM undervalues these companies dramatically — it would have told you D-Mart was worth almost nothing when it was actually compounding at 30%+ annually.

📉

Loss-Making or Irregular Dividend Payers

Companies with inconsistent or no dividend history — including most PSU companies that pay special dividends only when government needs fiscal revenue — cannot be reliably valued with DDM. Irregular dividends mean the growth rate assumption is meaningless. Use P/B (for PSU banks) or EV/EBITDA (for infrastructure PSUs) instead.

❌ DDM Not Applicable — No Dividends, Model Returns ₹0 or Undefined
Zomato is the most instructive anti-example for DDM. Zomato has never paid a dividend — D₀ = ₹0. Therefore D₁ = ₹0 × (1+g) = ₹0. And ₹0 ÷ (r−g) = ₹0. DDM says Zomato is worth zero. But Zomato's market cap is tens of thousands of crores. This apparent contradiction teaches the fundamental lesson about DDM's scope: the model values the cash flow that goes to shareholders today and in the near future. Zomato's entire value is speculative — locked in future profits that haven't arrived yet, funded by current cash burn. This isn't wrong; it's simply outside what DDM is designed to measure. The correct valuation models for Zomato are EV/Revenue (revenue-based relative valuation), DCF with terminal value (projecting eventual profitability), and TAM-based analysis (what share of the food delivery market it can capture and what margins that implies). The broader lesson: model selection is itself a critical analytical skill. Using DDM for Zomato doesn't make DDM wrong — it makes using DDM for Zomato wrong. Always match the valuation model to the company's financial stage and dividend policy.

06 — Strengths and Limitations

When DDM Shines and When It Misleads

No valuation model is universally correct. DDM has specific strengths that make it irreplaceable for dividend-income investors — and specific weaknesses that make it dangerous when misapplied.

✅
Strength 1: Focuses on Actual Cash Returned to Shareholders

Unlike P/E (which uses accounting earnings) or EV/EBITDA (which uses operating profit), DDM focuses exclusively on money that actually transfers from the company to shareholders — dividends. You can't fake a cash payment the way you can adjust accounting earnings. For income-oriented investors, this focus on real cash is DDM's most important advantage.

✅
Strength 2: Simple, Transparent, and Theoretically Sound

The Gordon Growth Model has three inputs and one output. Every assumption is explicit and examinable. Compare this to DCF, which requires 10-year cash flow projections and a terminal value calculation that can be manipulated almost arbitrarily. DDM's simplicity makes it easier to identify when inputs are unrealistic — a key advantage for retail investors who lack access to detailed management forecasts.

⚠️
Limitation 1: Extreme Sensitivity to Growth Rate Assumption

As the sensitivity analysis shows, a 2% change in g can change the intrinsic value by 50% or more. This means DDM valuations should never be presented as a single "correct" number — always as a range based on conservative, base, and optimistic growth scenarios. An analyst saying "DDM gives ₹350" without showing the sensitivity range is providing false precision.

⚠️
Limitation 2: Assumes Constant Growth Forever — Rarely True

The "g is constant forever" assumption is mathematically necessary but commercially unrealistic for most businesses. Companies go through cycles — periods of faster and slower growth. The single-stage GGM works best for utility companies and mature FMCG businesses where earnings and dividend growth genuinely are close to a constant low rate. For all others, multi-stage DDM (high growth phase → stable growth phase) is more appropriate but requires more assumptions.

❌
Limitation 3: Inapplicable to Most Indian Listed Companies

Of the 5,000+ NSE/BSE listed companies, only a small fraction pay consistent, growing dividends suitable for DDM analysis. Most Indian companies — particularly PSUs that pay irregular dividends based on government fiscal needs, growth companies that reinvest all earnings, cyclicals with volatile payouts, and new-age companies that pay nothing — cannot be valued using DDM. This limits DDM's practical utility as a primary valuation tool to a specific subset of large-cap, mature, dividend-paying businesses.

07 — Practical Application Framework

3 Steps to Apply DDM Correctly

DDM is powerful when applied disciplinarily — with conservative inputs, sensitivity analysis, and cross-verification against other valuation methods.

🔍
Step 1: Qualify the Company
Before applying DDM, check: (1) Does the company have 5+ consecutive years of dividend payments? (2) Has the dividend grown at a stable rate over this period? (3) Is the payout ratio consistent (not wildly fluctuating)? (4) Is the business model stable enough for the "constant growth forever" assumption to be reasonable? Only proceed with DDM if all four questions are answered yes. If a company skipped dividends even once in the past 5 years, treat DDM results with high scepticism.
📐
Step 2: Use Sustainable g, Not Recent Growth
Calculate g using ROE × Retention Ratio — not the most recent dividend growth rate, which may have been artificially high. Use 5-year average ROE for stability. Check whether the retention ratio is sustainable (a company paying out 95% of earnings has almost nothing left to reinvest — its g must be very low). Then run sensitivity: calculate DDM value at g−2%, g, and g+2%. The range this produces is your honest valuation band, not a single point estimate.
⚖️
Step 3: Cross-Verify with P/E and P/B
DDM gives the best signal when it aligns with other valuation methods. If DDM says ₹250, P/E-based value says ₹270, and P/B says ₹230 — the convergence around ₹250 provides high confidence. If DDM says ₹250 but P/E says ₹400 — investigate why: either the dividend is extremely conservative (company paying out very little despite high earnings) or the growth expectations priced by the market are much higher than what you've used in g. Multi-method convergence is far more reliable than any single model.
💡
Key Investor Insight
High Dividend Yield ≠ Good DDM Stock. Low Dividend Yield ≠ Bad DDM Stock.
A company paying 8% dividend yield with zero dividend growth has a DDM value = D₁ ÷ (r − 0) = D₁ ÷ r — meaning you value it purely on current income with no growth premium. If r = 12%, the stock is overvalued unless the dividend yield exceeds 12%. Conversely, a company paying 1% yield but growing dividends at 15% annually will compound magnificently over 20 years — DDM assigns it a high intrinsic value despite the modest current yield. The DDM insight that great dividend-growth investing is not about today's yield but about the trajectory of future payments is one of the most powerful concepts in income investing. A stock paying ₹5 today growing at 15% will pay ₹82 in 20 years — your dividend yield on original cost becomes extraordinary even if the initial yield appeared unimpressive.
