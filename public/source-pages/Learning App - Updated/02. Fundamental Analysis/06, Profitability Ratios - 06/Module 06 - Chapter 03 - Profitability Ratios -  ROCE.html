02 — ROCE vs ROE

Why ROCE Is Better Than ROE for Debt-Heavy Companies

Both metrics measure profitability. But they ask different questions. Understanding the difference helps you choose the right lens for the right company.

📊 ROE — Return on Equity
Measures profit on shareholder money only
ROE asks: "How much profit did we earn on the shareholders' portion of funding?" It ignores all borrowed money completely.
Denominator: Shareholders' equity only
Limitation: High debt artificially inflates ROE — making a heavily borrowed company look great
Best for: Asset-light businesses — IT, FMCG, pharma
Numerator: Net Profit (after interest & tax)
VS
🏭 ROCE — Return on Capital Employed
Measures profit on ALL capital — equity + debt
ROCE asks: "How much operating profit did we earn on the total capital deployed — both owned and borrowed?" This is the complete picture.
Denominator: Equity + Long-term debt (Total Capital Employed)
Strength: Debt cannot inflate ROCE — it goes into the denominator, making the ratio more honest
Best for: Capital-intensive — steel, cement, power, telecom
Numerator: EBIT (Earnings Before Interest & Tax)
💡
The Key Insight
A company with lots of debt can show a high ROE — but a low ROCE reveals the truth.
Imagine a company borrows ₹90 Cr and adds only ₹10 Cr of its own equity. Even a small profit on ₹100 Cr of assets looks like a huge ROE on just ₹10 Cr of equity. But ROCE will reflect the true reality — it considers all ₹100 Cr. This is why analysts always use ROCE when evaluating steel mills, infrastructure companies, banks, and energy firms.
03 — Understanding ROCE

What Exactly Is ROCE and How Is It Calculated?

Think of ROCE like measuring how well an entire factory complex — the buildings, machines, land, and all the loans taken to build it — is being used to generate earnings. Every rupee deployed, tracked and measured.

Capital Employed = Shareholder equity + Bank debt. The entire factory runs on both. ROCE measures how much EBIT that total capital generates.

1

EBIT — Earnings Before Interest & Tax

This is the true operating profit — before paying lenders (interest) or the government (tax). It shows what the business earned purely from operations, regardless of how it was funded.

2

Capital Employed — All the Money Running the Business

Equity from shareholders + long-term debt from banks and bondholders. This is the total capital the management is responsible for — both owned and borrowed money deployed in operations.

3

Why EBIT and Not Net Profit?

Net profit is after paying interest on debt. Since we're measuring returns on ALL capital including debt, we must use profits before interest charges — otherwise the debt component is counted twice. EBIT solves this cleanly.

4

The Golden Rule: ROCE Must Beat Cost of Capital

If a company borrows at 10% and earns ROCE of 8%, it is actually destroying wealth — paying more for capital than it earns. ROCE must exceed the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) to confirm the business is truly adding value.

05 — Why Capital-Intensive?

Why ROCE Matters Most in Heavy Industries

ROCE becomes the dominant metric when a business needs massive upfront capital — factories, mines, power plants, pipelines — where billions are deployed before a single rupee of revenue flows in. Here's why.

🏗️
Infrastructure & Steel
Steel plants, bridges, and roads require thousands of crores of upfront capital. ROE can be misleading here because debt is structural. ROCE shows if all that capital — owned and borrowed — is being put to productive use. A steel company with 18% ROCE is genuinely strong.
ROCE > ROE here
⚡
Power & Energy
Power plants, solar farms, and oil refineries take years to build and billions to finance. The debt-to-equity ratios are high by design. Analysing these companies purely on ROE misses the full picture. ROCE tells you whether the entire capital stack — including project loans — is generating adequate returns.
ROCE is essential
📡
Telecom & Highways
Spectrum auctions and highway concessions require companies to borrow heavily at the start. For years, ROE looks misleading. ROCE reveals whether the deployed capital — all of it — is working hard enough to justify the investment. Rising ROCE over 5 years in a telecom company is a very bullish sign.
5-yr trend is key
Simple Analogy

Think of Two Construction Contractors

Both build a factory worth ₹1 crore. One uses only their own money. One borrows 80% of it from a bank. ROE will be vastly different between them. ROCE cuts through that — it measures the same thing: how much profit the ₹1 crore factory generates, regardless of who funded it.

🏗️

Same Factory, Same Earnings — but ROE Looks Very Different

Both contractors built the same factory with ₹1 crore and earned ₹18 lakh. ROCE is identical at 18% — because the factory's productivity is the same. But ROE is 90% for the borrowed-up contractor versus 18% for the self-funded one. ROE here is misleading. ROCE is honest.

⚖️

Debt Is in the Denominator — It Cannot Inflate ROCE

When a company borrows more, its capital employed goes up. That makes the ROCE denominator bigger — which keeps ROCE honest even as ROE balloons. This structural property makes ROCE the go-to metric for any sector where leverage is commonplace.

📈

Improving ROCE Over 5 Years Is the Real Signal

A single year's ROCE can be affected by a one-time write-off or a big capital addition. The truly powerful signal is a consistent upward trend in ROCE over 5 years — it means management is getting better and better at deploying capital efficiently as the business matures.

🎯

Use ROCE and ROE Together for a Complete View

Neither metric is better in isolation. When ROE and ROCE are both high and similar, the company runs on low debt — a great sign. When ROE is much higher than ROCE, that gap reveals debt-driven inflation. The spread between the two tells the leverage story instantly.

07 — How to Use ROCE

3 Smart Ways to Apply ROCE as an Investor

ROCE is most powerful when used contextually — in the right sectors, with trend analysis, and always benchmarked against cost of capital. Here's the practical playbook.

📊
Always Compare ROCE to WACC
Find the company's approximate WACC (usually disclosed in annual reports or estimated at 9–12% for Indian companies). If ROCE comfortably exceeds WACC, the company is creating real value. If ROCE ≤ WACC, the business is operating below minimum viability — regardless of how the stock looks on other metrics.
Rule #1 for ROCE
📅
Track 5-Year ROCE Trend
One year is noise. For capital-intensive companies, track ROCE over 5 full years. If ROCE is consistently rising, the business is getting more efficient as it matures — a powerful compounding signal. If ROCE is declining despite growing revenues, the company is diluting capital efficiency by overexpanding.
Trend > Point-in-time
⚖️
Use ROCE + ROE Together
When ROCE and ROE are close to each other, the company runs on minimal debt — and both numbers are trustworthy. When ROE is significantly higher than ROCE, the gap is debt inflating equity returns. The larger the ROCE–ROE gap, the more leverage is at play. Ideal: high ROCE, moderate leverage, improving trend.
The Complete Picture
⚠️
Important Caveat
Never Compare ROCE Across Different Industries
A software company's ROCE of 40% and a steel plant's ROCE of 14% say nothing meaningful about each other — they operate in completely different capital structures. Always benchmark ROCE within the same industry. A 14% ROCE in steel may be excellent; the same number in an FMCG company would be cause for serious concern. Context and sector benchmarks are everything when reading ROCE.
🧠 Memory Tricks

Remember ROCE Instantly

Six mnemonics to lock in the ROCE formula, the WACC hurdle rate, why ROCE beats ROE for capital-intensive sectors, the EBIT vs PAT choice, and how ROCE identifies genuine value creators.

📊
Formula Trick · ROCE Definition
ROCE = EBIT ÷ Capital Employed × 100 — uses ALL capital, pre-interest
"ROCE = profit from the ENTIRE capital base, before paying the debt providers. Total capital, total efficiency."
E— EBIT (Earnings Before Interest & Tax): Used instead of Net Profit because EBIT is pre-interest, making ROCE independent of financing decisions. Whether a company uses equity or debt doesn’t affect the EBIT (and thus ROCE), making it truly comparable across different capital structures.
C— Capital Employed: Total Assets − Current Liabilities = Equity + Long-term Debt + Other Long-term Liabilities. Represents the long-term capital committed to the business. Excludes short-term payables (suppliers, salaries) which are operational, not investment capital.
📉Quick example: L&T FY24: EBIT ₹15,000 crore ÷ Capital Employed ₹1,20,000 crore = ROCE ~12.5%. Asian Paints: EBIT ₹4,500 crore ÷ Capital Employed ₹12,000 crore = ROCE ~37.5%. Asian Paints extracts 3× more return per rupee of long-term capital deployed.
💡 Capital Employed formula check: Two equivalent formulas: (1) Total Assets − Current Liabilities. (2) Shareholders’ Equity + Long-term Borrowings + Deferred Tax + Minority Interest. 💸
WACC Trick · The 12% Hurdle
ROCE > WACC = value creation · ROCE < WACC = value destruction
"WACC is the minimum. ROCE > WACC = creating value. ROCE < WACC = destroying value. 12% is India’s typical WACC hurdle."
WWACC (Weighted Average Cost of Capital): The blended rate a company pays for its capital (equity cost + debt cost, weighted by proportion). India typical: equity cost 12–15%, debt cost 8–10% (post-tax), blended WACC ~10–14%. Companies must earn above WACC to justify their capital structure.
🟢ROCE > WACC (value creators): Company earns more from its capital than the capital costs. Creates economic profit. Asian Paints ROCE 37% − WACC 12% = economic spread +25%. This spread, sustained over years, drives massive wealth creation for shareholders.
🔴ROCE < WACC (value destroyers): Company earns less than its capital costs. Economic value is destroyed even if accounting profits are reported. A company with 8% ROCE and 12% WACC is destroying ₹4 of economic value for every ₹100 of capital deployed, year after year. Steel/airline sectors frequently fall here.
💡 The spread is what compounds wealth: ROCE − WACC = Economic Spread. Asian Paints spread: 37% − 12% = +25%. Reinvesting ₹100 of earnings back into a 37% ROCE business at 12% cost of capital = ₹25 of economic value creation per ₹100. Compounded over 20 years: this spread of +25% explains why Asian Paints stock has been a 100× wealth creator since 2003. The spread is the investment engine.
🏗
Capital-Intensive Trick · Why ROCE > ROE for Heavy Industries
ROCE removes debt distortion for steel, cement, power, infrastructure analysis
"ROE is distorted by leverage. ROCE isn’t. For debt-heavy sectors: ALWAYS use ROCE first, ROE second."
🔩Steel sector leverage problem: Two steel companies, both have 15% ROE. Company A: D/E 0.3×, ROCE 14%. Company B: D/E 2.5×, ROCE 6%. Company B’s ROE is entirely leverage-driven — its 6% ROCE is below WACC. It’s destroying economic value despite reporting the same ROE as Company A. ROCE reveals this; ROE hides it.
🏛Infrastructure legitimately uses leverage: Ports, airports, highways have 25–35 year PPAs with government. Predictable cash flows justify high leverage (D/E 2–4×). The key check: ROCE > cost of debt (~8–9%). If infrastructure company ROCE is 10–12% and debt costs 8%, leverage is accretive. If ROCE is 6% and debt costs 8%, leverage is destructive.
💷Cement sector benchmark: India cement ROCE benchmarks: UltraTech 12–15%, Shree Cement 18–22% (premium), Ambuja/ACC 10–13%. Shree Cement consistently earns highest ROCE in cement = best capital allocation = premium P/E justified. ROCE spread between Shree and peers is a direct proxy for management quality in capital-intensive sectors.
💡 The capital-intensive sector rule: For any sector with D/E > 1× (steel, cement, power, airports, real estate), reject any company with ROCE < 10%. ROCE below 10% = company earns less than cost of capital even before equity returns. This is economic value destruction. 💸
EBIT Trick · Why Not PAT?
ROCE uses EBIT (not PAT) to make it capital-structure neutral
"EBIT is pre-interest = ROCE is the SAME regardless of whether a company uses debt or equity financing."
📉Why EBIT over PAT: PAT (Profit After Tax) is after interest payments. If Company A uses no debt and Company B uses heavy debt: both have identical EBIT (same business operations). But Company B has lower PAT (interest expense reduces PAT). Using EBIT ensures ROCE measures underlying business efficiency, not financing choice.
🎯Numerical proof: Both companies earn ₹100 EBIT on ₹500 Capital Employed = ROCE 20%. Company A (no debt): PAT = ₹70 (no interest). Company B (50% debt at 10%): Interest ₹25, PAT = ₹52.5. ROE: Company A 28%, Company B 42% (leverage amplified). ROCE: Both 20% (identical, as intended).
⚠Tax rate caveat: Some analysts use EBIT × (1 − tax rate) = NOPAT (Net Operating Profit After Tax) in ROCE for a more precise comparison. If Company A has 30% tax and Company B has 20% (due to tax incentives), EBIT-based ROCE slightly favours B. NOPAT-ROCE accounts for this. For practical purposes, EBIT-ROCE is sufficient for most stock screening.
💡 Where to find EBIT: Annual Report P&L → Revenue − Operating Expenses = EBIT (or Profit Before Interest and Tax). Alternatively: EBITDA − Depreciation = EBIT. 📚 Glossary

Key Terms Glossary

Every ROCE-related term — from Capital Employed to WACC to economic profit — defined with Indian capital-intensive sector examples.

🔍
ROCE (Return on Capital Employed)
Definition
ROCE measures how efficiently a company uses ALL its long-term capital — both equity and debt — to generate operating profit. Formula: ROCE = (EBIT ÷ Capital Employed) × 100. EBIT is used (not Net Profit) to make ROCE independent of financing decisions: a company using debt and one using equity will have the same ROCE if their operating performance is identical. Capital Employed = Shareholders' Equity + Long-term Debt + Other Long-term Capital. It's the definitive metric for capital-intensive sector analysis (steel, cement, power, infrastructure, real estate).
💡 ROCE in practice: Asian Paints FY24: EBIT ₹4,500 crore ÷ Capital Employed ₹12,000 crore = 37.5% ROCE. L&T FY24: EBIT ₹15,000 crore ÷ Capital Employed ₹1,20,000 crore = 12.5% ROCE. Both are well-managed companies. Asian Paints earns 3× more ROCE because it's asset-light (brand business) vs L&T (massive construction assets needed). For L&T's capital intensity, 12.5% ROCE is actually good — it exceeds WACC of ~10%. The absolute ROCE must always be judged against the sector norm and WACC.
Capital Employed
Calculation
The total long-term capital deployed in a business. Two equivalent calculation methods: (1) Capital Employed = Total Assets − Current Liabilities. Intuition: total assets minus short-term payables (suppliers, salaries — these are operational, not long-term investment). (2) Capital Employed = Shareholders' Equity + Long-term Borrowings + Deferred Tax Liabilities + Minority Interest. This is the "sources" version: all long-term capital from any provider. Capital Employed is the denominator in ROCE. Rising Capital Employed = company is reinvesting (either growing or adding capacity). Capital Employed stable with rising EBIT = improving ROCE (ideal).
💡 Capital Employed trend: JSW Steel FY24: Capital Employed ~₹1,40,000 crore (massive factory assets, long-term debt). L&T FY24: ~₹1,20,000 crore. TCS FY24: ~₹65,000 crore (much smaller despite being India's most valuable company — because it's capital-light). TCS Capital Employed = mostly working capital and computers, not factories. Capital-light = small denominator = high ROCE even with same profits.
EBIT (Earnings Before Interest & Tax)
Calculation
Operating profit before deducting interest expense or income tax. EBIT = Revenue − Operating Expenses (COGS + selling, general & administrative expenses + depreciation). EBIT represents the profit generated purely from business operations, independent of how the business is financed (debt or equity). This is why ROCE uses EBIT: it enables fair comparison between companies with different debt levels. A company with zero debt and 30% EBIT margin and another with high debt and 30% EBIT margin have identical ROCE — the financing choice doesn't affect operating efficiency.
💡 EBIT vs PAT in ROCE: Two cement companies with identical operations, ₹500 crore EBIT on ₹2,000 crore Capital Employed = ROCE 25% for both. Company A (no debt): PAT ₹350 crore, ROE 35%. Company B (₹800 crore debt at 9%): Interest ₹72 crore, PAT ₹304 crore, ROE 38% (leverage amplified ROE). ROCE: Both 25% — correctly showing identical operational efficiency. If we had used PAT-ROCE: Company B would show 22.8% vs Company A 25%, unfairly penalising the leveraged company for a financing choice, not an operational one.
WACC (Weighted Average Cost of Capital)
WACC & Hurdle
The blended rate of return that a company's capital providers (equity holders + debt holders) expect to receive. Weighted by the proportion of each capital type. WACC is the company's minimum required return — the hurdle rate. ROCE > WACC = value creation (company earns more than capital costs). ROCE < WACC = value destruction (company earns less than capital costs, destroying economic value even if accounting profits are positive). India typical WACC: 10–13% for most listed companies. Higher WACC for riskier businesses (startups, infrastructure pre-revenue). Lower WACC for regulated utilities with guaranteed returns.
💡 India WACC estimation: (1) Cost of equity: Risk-free rate (10Y G-Sec ~7%) + Equity Risk Premium (~5%) = ~12%. (2) Cost of debt post-tax: Borrowing rate (~10%) × (1-0.25 tax) = ~7.5%. (3) Weighted average (50% equity, 50% debt): 0.5 × 12% + 0.5 × 7.5% = ~9.75% ≈ 10%. Practical rule: ROCE > 12% = value-creating for most India companies. ROCE 10-12% = marginal value creation. ROCE < 10% = likely value destroying. This 12% hurdle is a useful starting point across sectors.
Economic Profit / Economic Spread
WACC & Hurdle
Economic Profit = (ROCE − WACC) × Capital Employed. The excess profit generated above the minimum required return on capital. Positive economic profit = true value creation (shareholders are better off than investing elsewhere at similar risk). Negative economic profit = value destruction (shareholders would earn more from alternative investments of similar risk). Economic profit is distinct from accounting profit: a company can report positive accounting profit but negative economic profit if ROCE < WACC. Equity markets price in the present value of expected future economic profits — which is why high-ROCE companies trade at premium P/E multiples.
💡 Economic spread proof: Asian Paints Capital Employed ₹12,000 crore, ROCE 37%, WACC 12%. Economic Profit = (37% − 12%) × ₹12,000 crore = 25% × ₹12,000 crore = ₹3,000 crore economic profit per year (above and beyond what investors require). This ₹3,000 crore annual economic value creation is what justifies Asian Paints' premium 50-60× P/E multiple. Compare steel: ROCE 8%, WACC 10%. Economic Profit = (8% − 10%) × ₹80,000 crore = −₹1,600 crore. Despite accounting profits, economic value is destroyed annually.
ROCE Benchmarks by Sector
Benchmarks
ROCE benchmarks vary by sector's asset intensity and competitive dynamics. India sector ROCE benchmarks: FMCG premium brands (Nestlé 55%+, Colgate 80%+, HUL 35%+), IT Services (TCS 45%+, Infosys 35%+), Specialty chemicals (Pidilite 28%+, SRF 18%+), Specialty paints (Asian Paints 38%+), Private banking (HDFC Bank: ROE-equivalent 18%, ROCE concept different for banks), Cement (Shree Cement 18-22% = best in sector, UltraTech 12-15% = good), Capital goods / EPC (L&T 12-14% = good for complexity), Steel (JSW Steel 8-12% = cyclical), Power utilities (NTPC 8-10%), Airlines (IndiGo 5-10% = structurally challenged).
💡 Shree Cement vs UltraTech ROCE gap: Shree Cement consistently earns 18-22% ROCE vs UltraTech 12-15% ROCE. Both make cement — same product, same market. Why the gap? Shree: lowest cost per tonne producer in India, minimal capacity underutilisation, superior logistics location. UltraTech: larger scale but higher fixed costs per tonne. This 6-8% ROCE gap over 10 years = compounding wealth creation advantage for Shree. Shree P/E consistently commands 30%+ premium to UltraTech. ROCE is the underlying reason for this persistent valuation gap.
NOPAT (Net Operating Profit After Tax)
Definition
NOPAT = EBIT × (1 − Effective Tax Rate). A refinement of EBIT that adjusts for taxes to enable more precise ROCE comparison when companies have different effective tax rates. Used in the EVA (Economic Value Added) framework and advanced ROCE calculations. Formula: ROCE (NOPAT version) = NOPAT ÷ Capital Employed. When two companies have different tax rates (e.g., one has tax holiday under SEZ, another pays full 25%), EBIT-based ROCE gives Company A an unfair advantage. NOPAT-ROCE equalises the comparison. For most stock screening purposes, EBIT-ROCE is sufficient; NOPAT-ROCE is used by financial analysts for precise capital efficiency comparison.
💡 NOPAT vs EBIT-ROCE: Company A: EBIT ₹100 crore, 25% tax → NOPAT ₹75 crore. Company B: EBIT ₹100 crore, 10% tax (SEZ benefit) → NOPAT ₹90 crore. On Capital Employed ₹500 crore: EBIT-ROCE = 20% for both (identical). NOPAT-ROCE: Company A 15%, Company B 18%. The SEZ tax benefit shows up correctly in NOPAT-ROCE. For comparing speciality chemical companies (many have SEZ benefits) vs non-SEZ companies, NOPAT-ROCE provides cleaner comparison. Screener.in uses EBIT-ROCE — add tax-rate context for SEZ companies manually.
ROCE in Capex Cycles
Benchmarks
Capital-intensive companies go through recurring capex cycles: investment phase (ROCE falls as new assets added, not yet productive) and harvest phase (ROCE rises as new assets generate full revenue). Investors who only look at current ROCE miss the cycle: a company with falling ROCE due to new plant investment may actually be a better buy than one with stable ROCE and no growth. Assessment framework: (1) Is ROCE falling due to new capacity investment (temporary)? (2) What ROCE will this capacity generate when fully operational? (3) Is the incremental return on new capital above WACC? If yes → capex cycle is value-creating, current low ROCE is temporary, patient buying opportunity.
💡 JSW Steel capex cycle (2019-2024): JSW Steel invested ₹50,000+ crore in capacity expansion 2019-2022. ROCE fell to 6-8% during heavy capex years. Plant reached full operational capacity 2023-2024 → ROCE recovered to 12%+. Investors who bought during the 6-8% ROCE trough (knowing the capex was growth-oriented and steel prices would recover) earned 2-3× returns when ROCE normalised. Capex-trough investing requires understanding: (1) Is this capex building productive future assets? (2) Is the sector fundamentally attractive? (3) Does the company have strong enough balance sheet to complete the cycle?
