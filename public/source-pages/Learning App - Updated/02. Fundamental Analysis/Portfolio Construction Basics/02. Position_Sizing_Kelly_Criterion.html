01 — Key Learning Outcome

Why How Much You Invest Matters as Much as What You Invest In

📐
Your Core Takeaway
Even if you pick the right stocks, poor position sizing can destroy your portfolio. An investor who puts 40% of their money in one stock — even if they're right about the company — is one bad quarter away from catastrophic loss. The goal of position sizing is to ensure that no single mistake kills your wealth, while no single correct decision is wasted because you sized it too small.
Imagine you are a cricket captain picking your batting order. You have 11 batsmen. Some are world-class (Virat Kohli — you send them to bat early and let them play a long innings). Some are good but unproven (you give them a reasonable opportunity). Some are tail-enders (you don't waste the most important overs on them). Position sizing in investing works exactly the same way. Your highest-conviction, most-researched ideas get the most capital — but with a hard cap so that even if they're wrong, you survive. Your exploratory positions get smaller allocations so that if they work, you benefit, and if they don't, you're not devastated. This is called conviction-based position sizing. The mathematical framework that supports this — the Kelly Criterion — tells you precisely what fraction of your capital to risk on any investment based on your estimated probability of being right and the expected reward. In practice, most investors use a modified, more conservative version. Understanding both Kelly and equal-weight sizing helps you build a portfolio that is both smart about conviction and safe against overconfidence.

02 — Two Sizing Methods

Kelly Criterion vs Equal Weight — Which One Should You Use?

There are two main approaches to position sizing. Equal weight is simple, safe, and surprisingly effective. The Kelly Criterion is mathematically optimal but requires accurate probability estimates. Understanding both lets you choose the right tool for each situation.

📐
Kelly Criterion
Mathematically optimal capital allocation based on conviction probability
The Kelly Criterion was invented by a Bell Labs physicist named John Kelly in 1956 to optimise bet sizing for information transmission — but investors quickly realised it worked perfectly for portfolio sizing too. The core idea: the fraction of your capital you should put into any investment is determined by how likely you are to be right and how much you gain when right versus how much you lose when wrong. Kelly gives you the mathematically optimal size that maximises long-term wealth growth. In practice, most professional investors use "Half-Kelly" — half the mathematically optimal position — because real-world probability estimates are uncertain and full Kelly is extremely aggressive. Even Half-Kelly requires honest self-assessment of your conviction level, which is its greatest discipline-building benefit.
Kelly Formula
f* = (b × p − q) ÷ b
Where: f* = fraction of capital to invest · p = probability of winning (your conviction %) · q = probability of losing (1 − p) · b = ratio of expected gain to expected loss

⚖️
Equal Weight
Divide capital equally across all positions — simple, robust, and surprisingly powerful
Equal weighting is exactly what it sounds like: if you own 15 stocks, each gets 6.67% of your portfolio. No stock is "bigger" than another. This approach is surprisingly effective because it removes the human bias of over-concentrating in your "favourites" — which are often the positions you are most emotionally attached to, not necessarily the ones with the best risk-reward going forward. Research consistently shows that equal-weight portfolios beat cap-weighted portfolios over long periods. For most retail investors — especially those with less than 5 years of investing experience — equal weight is the recommended starting approach. It forces you to be right on most of your stock picks, rather than betting heavily on just one or two. It also makes portfolio management simple: rebalance when any position grows beyond 15% of portfolio.
Equal Weight Formula
Position % = 100% ÷ Number of Stocks
Example: 20 stocks → 5% each · 15 stocks → 6.67% each · 10 stocks → 10% each · Rebalance when any position drifts beyond ±5% of target weight

03 — Kelly in Practice

How to Apply the Kelly Criterion — Step by Step with Real Numbers

The Kelly formula looks intimidating but becomes intuitive once you see it applied to a real investment decision. Let's walk through it using a specific Indian stock example so you can replicate this thinking for any investment you evaluate.

1
🎯
Estimate Your Win Probability (p)
How confident are you the stock will outperform over 2–3 years? After deep research on Asian Paints — studying moat, SWOT, management, financials — you estimate 70% probability of 2× return. So p = 0.70, q = 0.30. Be honest. Overconfidence is the #1 Kelly mistake.
2
📊
Estimate Win/Loss Ratio (b)
If right: you expect +100% gain (stock doubles). If wrong: you expect −35% loss (stock falls on negative surprise). Win/loss ratio b = 100% ÷ 35% = 2.86. This is the reward-to-risk ratio of your specific investment thesis.
3
🧮
Apply the Kelly Formula
f* = (b × p − q) ÷ b = (2.86 × 0.70 − 0.30) ÷ 2.86 = (2.002 − 0.30) ÷ 2.86 = 1.702 ÷ 2.86 = 0.595 = 59.5% of your portfolio. Full Kelly says invest 59.5% in Asian Paints. This is why Full Kelly is dangerous — overconfidence inflates p.
4
✂️
Apply Half-Kelly Safety Factor
Divide Kelly output by 2 for safety: 59.5% ÷ 2 = 29.75% ≈ 30%. Still too high? Most investors cap individual stocks at 10%. Apply your portfolio maximum: min(Half-Kelly, your max cap) = min(30%, 10%) = 10% final allocation.

🚨 Key Lesson — The 10% Rule is Non-Negotiable
No matter how confident you are, no single stock should exceed 10% of your portfolio. Your maximum individual stock loss is then capped at 10% × 100% = 10% of portfolio in a complete wipeout. Position sizing is not about limiting upside — it's about ensuring you survive to keep compounding. Manish's portfolio could have continued growing if his conviction was wrong at a 10% size. At 40%, his mistake set him back years.

Rebalancing also prevents the emotional "ride the winner forever" bias that makes portfolios dangerously concentrated over time.

The Professional Decision Framework
📋 Step 1 — Thesis document: Before any investment, write a 1-page investment thesis with explicit probability estimate (p), expected upside if right (b numerator), and expected downside if wrong (b denominator).
📐 Step 2 — Kelly calculation: Apply f* = (b×p−q)÷b. If output <5%, idea doesn't meet bar. If output ≥5%, apply Half-Kelly and cap at 12%. Position enters portfolio.
🔄 Step 3 — Quarterly review: Re-estimate probability each quarter after results. If thesis is playing out as expected, maintain position. If key assumption has changed, re-size or exit immediately.
💡 Key Lesson — The Process Matters More Than the Formula
The most valuable part of Vikram's approach is not the Kelly formula itself — it's the discipline of writing an explicit thesis with quantified probability estimates before every investment. This forces intellectual honesty. Many "high conviction" ideas fail the Kelly test because when you actually quantify p and b, the output is surprisingly modest. The formula acts as a reality check on overconfidence — which is the investor's biggest enemy in position sizing.
