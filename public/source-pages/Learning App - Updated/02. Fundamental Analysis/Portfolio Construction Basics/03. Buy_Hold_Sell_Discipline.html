Buy, Hold & Sell Define Entry & Exit Rules Based on Valuation

Portfolio Construction Basics · Investment Discipline · Decision Framework

The most successful investors don't make buy and sell decisions based on feelings, tips, or price movements. They have a clear framework — a written set of rules — that tells them exactly when to buy, when to hold patiently, and when to exit. This discipline is what separates wealth builders from wealth destroyers.

01 — Key Learning Outcome

Why Rules Matter More Than Intelligence in Investing

📋
Your Core Takeaway
An investor with a mediocre strategy but iron discipline will outperform a genius with no rules every single time. The market is designed to exploit your emotions — fear makes you sell at the bottom, greed makes you buy at the top. The only defence is a pre-written rulebook that you follow regardless of how you feel in the moment.
Think of a surgeon. Before every operation, a skilled surgeon follows a pre-written checklist — sterilisation, patient identification, instrument count. Not because the surgeon is forgetful or incompetent, but because high-stakes decisions made under pressure are subject to human error. Investing is exactly the same. In a market crash, your heart will tell you to sell everything and run. In a raging bull market, your heart will tell you to take loans and buy more. Neither is correct — and both will destroy wealth. A written investment rulebook — specifying exactly what valuation metrics trigger a buy, what conditions justify holding through pain, and what specific events require an exit — is the surgical checklist of investing. The investors who maintain this discipline over 10–20 years don't just avoid catastrophic losses. They create conditions where compounding can work uninterrupted — which is how ordinary investors build extraordinary wealth.

02 — The Three Decisions

Buy · Hold · Sell — Exactly When Each Decision is Correct

Every day you own a stock, you are actually making one of three active decisions: Buy more, Hold what you have, or Sell. There is no neutral. Sitting without a rule is itself a decision — and usually the wrong one. Here is the framework for making each decision correctly.

🟢 BUY
Add to Position When the Stock is Undervalued Relative to Its Business Quality
Price is below intrinsic value · Thesis is intact · Valuation is attractive
A BUY decision means you believe the current market price significantly underestimates what the business is actually worth. This can happen because of temporary bad news, sector-wide pessimism, market crashes, or simply that the market hasn't discovered the business yet. The key: the business fundamentals must be healthy — you are buying business quality at a discount, not a bad business that looks cheap.
📉
PE below historical average + peers
Example: Titan normally trades at 60–70× PE. If it falls to 40× during a market correction — and the business thesis is unchanged — that is a BUY signal, not a panic signal.
📊
Price-to-book below long-run average for quality banks
HDFC Bank historically trades at 3–4× book value. When it fell to 1.8× book post-merger concerns — business intact, credit quality excellent — that was a BUY opportunity.
💧
Earnings yield > long-term bond yield
If a stock's earnings yield (E/P) exceeds the 10-year government bond yield by 2%+, the market is offering you equity at bond-like prices — historically an excellent entry point.
🌧️
Sector-wide panic unrelated to specific business
When the entire IT sector falls 35% because of US macro fears — but you've verified that your specific company's client relationships, deal wins, and margins are intact — the sector panic is your entry opportunity.
🟣 HOLD
Do Nothing When the Business is Performing and the Thesis Remains Valid
Thesis intact · Valuation fair · Business growing as expected
HOLD is the most underappreciated decision in investing. The entire power of compounding requires that you do nothing — that you resist the urge to sell a great business just because the price has doubled, or to panic-sell during a temporary correction. Holding requires more courage and discipline than buying. It means sitting through painful drawdowns knowing that your thesis is intact, and resisting the temptation to book profits when a business is still in the early stages of its growth journey.
✅
Thesis intact after each quarterly result
Read the concall transcript. If management's answers still confirm the original thesis — moat, growth runway, capital allocation — hold, regardless of short-term price movement.
📈
Valuation is fair but not extreme
If a stock at 40× PE grows earnings at 25% annually, it reaches 32× PE in just one year without moving. "Expensive" stocks that grow fast often become "reasonably valued" before you realise it. Hold unless valuation is genuinely stretched beyond any reasonable scenario.
⏳
Management is executing on stated roadmap
Compare what management said they would do in the last 4 concalls versus what actually happened. If guidance is consistently being met or beaten, hold through market noise — the business is compounding for you.
🌊
Short-term correction in a long-term compounder
A 20–30% correction in a high-quality business with a 10-year growth runway is noise. Hold. Asian Paints corrected 40% in 2022 — the business grew 15% that year. Every correction was a hold (or add) opportunity.
🔴 SELL
Exit When the Thesis Breaks, Not When the Price Falls
Thesis broken · Valuation extreme · Better opportunity available
Selling is the most emotionally difficult decision — and the most misunderstood. Most retail investors sell for the wrong reasons: they panic when price falls, or they take profits too early when price rises. The correct sell trigger is almost never the price. A stock price falling is a reason to re-examine the thesis — not automatically to sell. A stock price rising is never a reason to sell. The only legitimate sell triggers are: the original thesis has broken, valuation has reached an extreme, management has fundamentally changed, or a genuinely better investment opportunity presents itself.
💔
Original thesis has broken irreversibly
You bought Zomato on "network effect moat" thesis. If ONDC genuinely disrupts that network effect and adoption proves sustained — not just a media story — the original thesis is broken. Sell.
📈
Valuation has reached unsustainable extreme
A business growing at 15% annually trading at 120× PE requires a 20-year perfect execution to justify the price. At some point, even great businesses become priced for perfection. Trim (don't fully exit) when valuation exceeds your most optimistic reasonable scenario.
👤
Management integrity or quality has changed
Any evidence of accounting manipulation, governance issues, large unexplained related-party transactions, or auditor resignation triggers an immediate exit. Management trust, once broken, rarely fully recovers in time to matter.
🔄
Better opportunity — capital efficiency
If your current holding is at fair value with 12% expected returns and you've found a high-conviction undervalued business with 25%+ expected returns — it may make sense to sell the former and buy the latter. Capital should always work hardest.
03 — The Valuation Zone Framework

Where Is the Stock in the Valuation Spectrum Right Now?

Every stock — no matter how good the business — has a price at which you should be buying, a range where you hold patiently, and a price at which you should be trimming. Understanding which zone you're in determines the correct action.

📐 The Buy–Hold–Sell Price Zone Map
Where the stock is in its valuation spectrum determines the correct investor action — not the news, not the price movement, not the market mood
🟢 BUY ZONE
Below intrinsic value
🟣 HOLD ZONE
Fair value range
🔴 SELL ZONE
Above intrinsic value
🟢 Buy Zone — What Does "Cheap" Actually Mean?
The stock is trading at a meaningful discount to your calculated intrinsic value. For PE-based investors: current PE is below the stock's 5-year average PE by 20%+. For dividend investors: yield is above historical average. For GARP investors: PEG ratio below 1.5. In this zone — if the thesis is intact — add aggressively and buy on every dip. This is when the risk-reward is in your favour.
🟣 Hold Zone — When "Expensive" Is Still Fine
The stock is trading within its normal valuation band — neither a screaming bargain nor stretched. Current PE is within ±15% of historical average. This is the most common state for quality stocks. The correct action is to hold patiently and collect compounding. Do not sell because "the stock has risen." Do not add unless the business is growing faster than expected.
🔴 Sell/Trim Zone — When Even Great Businesses Are Overpriced
The stock is trading 30%+ above your most optimistic intrinsic value estimate. At this price, even perfect execution may not deliver adequate returns. The correct action: trim to your core position size (don't fully exit a great business — you may not get to buy it back at reasonable prices for years). But reduce the weighting to manage the risk of a valuation correction.
😰 Emotional Investor — What They Actually Do
Driven by fear, greed, and tips rather than valuation
😱
Sells when price falls 20%
"I need to cut my losses" — but the business hasn't changed, only the price has. Sells exactly when they should be buying more.
🤑
Buys when price rises 40%
"This stock is doing great!" — chases momentum at peak valuations, which is exactly when risk is highest.
📱
Acts on WhatsApp tips
Buys based on a friend's recommendation without ever understanding the business or checking valuation. The tip-giver has usually already bought and is waiting to sell to you.
🎲
No pre-defined exit rule
Holds a failing business "hoping it will recover" because there's no written rule about when to exit a broken thesis. Hope is not a strategy.
😌 Disciplined Investor — What Rules-Based Looks Like
Driven by pre-written valuation rules, not market mood
📉
Checks thesis when price falls
When price falls 20%, re-reads the original investment thesis. If thesis intact → add more (it's now cheaper). If thesis broken → exit immediately.
📐
Trims when valuation is extreme
Has a pre-written rule: "Sell 25% of position when PE exceeds 2× my fair value estimate." Executes this regardless of market mood or short-term momentum.
📋
Written investment thesis for every holding
Maintains a 1-page thesis document for each stock: what needs to be true for the thesis to work, what would make it wrong, and what valuation level triggers an exit.
⏳
Holds great businesses through market noise
Holds Bajaj Finance through every RBI action, interest rate cycle, and market crash — because the original thesis (India credit underpenetration + data moat) has not changed in 7 years.

Why P/B Works for Banks
Why PE Doesn't Work for Banks — Use P/B Instead
Banks' earnings are highly variable — they can provision more (reducing profits) in bad years and less in good years. PE fluctuates wildly. Price-to-Book (P/B) measures what you're paying for the bank's net assets — its loan book minus liabilities. For a bank with strong credit quality, paying 3–4× book for 18–20% ROE is reasonable. Paying below 2.5× book for the same quality is a rare gift.
💡 Key Lesson — Different Sectors Need Different Valuation Metrics
PE works for consumer companies. P/B works for banks. EV/EBITDA works for capital-intensive businesses. Sales multiples work for early-stage companies. Using the wrong metric for the wrong sector leads to wrong buy/sell decisions. Always match your valuation tool to the sector's specific economics.

Example 04 · Terminal Industry + Trim Discipline · NSE: COALINDIA
Coal India
Coal India offers a fascinating buy/hold/sell discipline test: a genuinely powerful monopoly in a structurally declining industry. The correct discipline is time-horizon specific — it's a 5-year income trade, not a 20-year compounding investment.

Decision Rules — Time-Horizon Specific
BUY
PE below 6× AND dividend yield above 6.5% for income-focused investors
For an investor explicitly seeking income (not long-term compounding), Coal India at 5–6× PE with 6–7% dividend yield offers better risk-adjusted income than bonds. Short-term buy for income investors with 3–5 year horizon.
HOLD
PE 7–10× while dividend payments are consistent
Hold as long as dividends are paid as promised, coal demand for India's power sector remains strong (i.e., before 2030 energy transition acceleration), and government doesn't cut regulated prices below cost.
SELL
PE above 12× OR solar LCOE falls below coal LCOE consistently
At 12×+ PE, the market is pricing in long-term growth that doesn't exist in a terminal industry. Sell. Or when solar + storage becomes structurally cheaper than coal for power generation — the structural demand cliff arrives.
Why the Same Rules Don't Apply to Every Investment
🚨 Key Lesson — Buy/Hold/Sell Rules Must Match Investment Horizon
Coal India's buy/hold/sell rules are fundamentally different from Asian Paints' because the investment thesis is fundamentally different. Asian Paints is a long-term compounder — hold through all market cycles. Coal India is a time-limited income trade — harvest dividends while the cash flow lasts and have a hard sell rule linked to energy transition data. Applying compounding investor logic (hold forever) to a terminal business thesis (harvest then exit) is one of the most common portfolio mistakes.
🕐 5-year rule for Coal India: Any Coal India position should have a hard 5-year exit date written into the thesis — not because something will go wrong, but because the opportunity window for income extraction is finite and predictable. This is active portfolio management, not buy-and-forget.
Example 05 · Quarterly Thesis Review · The 3-Point Checklist
The Thesis Review Framework
Every investor needs a systematic quarterly review process for every holding — not a price check, a thesis check. This framework works for any stock in any sector and is the discipline that prevents the most common investing mistake: holding a broken thesis while "waiting to break even."
Quarterly review · 3 checksPrice-independent decisionPrevents averaging down mistakesWorks for any sector
The 3-Point Quarterly Thesis Review
CHECK 1
Is the original reason I bought this stock still true?
Write out in one sentence why you originally bought the stock. Then ask: is this sentence still factually accurate after this quarter's results and concall? If yes → continue. If no → the thesis has broken. Exit regardless of current profit/loss.
CHECK 2
Has management done what they said they would do?
Pull out what management said in the Q2 concall about Q3 expectations. Compare it with Q3 actuals. Consistent under-delivery on guidance over 3–4 quarters without explanation is a management quality red flag — even if the business looks okay on paper.
CHECK 3
Is the valuation still in the hold zone?
Calculate the stock's current valuation metric (PE, P/B, EV/EBITDA as appropriate for sector). Compare with your pre-defined buy zone, hold zone, and sell zone. If it has drifted into the sell zone — trim according to your pre-written rule, regardless of recent performance.
📋 Decision rule: If all 3 checks pass → HOLD. If Check 1 fails → EXIT immediately (thesis broken). If Checks 2–3 fail but 1 passes → reduce position and monitor for 1 more quarter before deciding.
The Most Dangerous Investor Mindset
🚨 "I'll sell once I'm back to break-even" — This is the single most wealth-destroying thought in investing. Your purchase price is irrelevant to whether you should hold or sell. The only question is: would you buy this stock today, at this price, if you didn't already own it? If the answer is no, sell — your cost price has nothing to do with it.
🚨 "The stock has fallen 50% so the downside is limited" — A stock that has fallen 50% can fall another 50% from there. A stock at ₹100 that is worth ₹50 is overpriced by 100%. Its past price has no bearing on future performance. Thesis + valuation determine the decision — not price history.
💡 Key Lesson — Never Make Decisions Based on Your Cost Price
The most disciplined investors treat every holding as if they had just bought it today. They ask: "If I received this stock as a gift with no cost price, would I keep it or sell it based on current valuation and thesis?" If the answer is sell, they sell — even at a loss. This prevents the twin disasters of averaging down into broken theses and holding losses while "waiting for recovery" that never comes.
