01 — Key Learning Outcome

Why Do Smart People Make Stupid Investment Decisions?

🧠
Your Core Takeaway
Behavioural biases are predictable, systematic errors in human thinking that cause investors to make decisions based on emotions, shortcuts, and mental habits — rather than facts and logic. The damage is not random; these biases follow identifiable patterns you can learn to catch.
Imagine a highly experienced factory manager who bought a machine for ₹50 lakh five years ago. Today, a better machine exists for ₹30 lakh and the old one is worth ₹10 lakh as scrap. Should he sell the old machine and upgrade? Of course — but he refuses. "I paid ₹50 lakh for this," he says. That ₹50 lakh is gone. It's a sunk cost. His brain is anchored to the original price and cannot make the rational decision.

You do the same thing with stocks. You bought Vodafone Idea at ₹15. It's now ₹8. You don't sell because "it'll come back to ₹15 — I just need to break even." That ₹15 is your anchor. It has nothing to do with whether Vodafone Idea will actually recover.

Daniel Kahneman (Nobel Prize winner) showed that human brains use mental shortcuts called "heuristics" that were useful for survival in the prehistoric era — but are systematically wrong in financial markets. Markets are specifically designed to exploit these shortcuts. Understanding them is not optional for anyone who wants to compound wealth over time. This chapter teaches you the 5 most damaging biases — exactly how they appear in Indian stock investing — and precise techniques to override them.

02 — The Five Biases That Cost Indian Investors the Most

How Your Brain Sabotages Your Portfolio

Each bias follows a recognisable pattern: a trigger (what the market does), a mental shortcut (what your brain does automatically), and a mistake (the bad decision that follows). Once you can name the pattern, you can interrupt it before it costs you money.

Five biases surround the investor simultaneously, each pulling decisions in the wrong direction. Anchoring to past prices, extrapolating recent trends, filtering out bad news, refusing to realise losses, and overestimating skill — all five feed each other and compound the damage. Naming the bias is the first step to breaking the cycle.

⚓
Bias #1 — Most Expensive
Anchoring Bias
Your brain gets fixated on an arbitrary "reference price" — usually what you paid or what the stock once traded at — and uses it as the benchmark for all future decisions, even when it's completely irrelevant to current intrinsic value.
🔴 High Damage · Affects every investor who tracks their cost price
Think of a shopkeeper who bought 100 kg of tomatoes at ₹40/kg. Three days later, they're worth ₹20/kg (they're rotting). He refuses to sell at ₹20 because "I paid ₹40." The next day they're ₹5. He still won't sell. The day after that they're worthless. The ₹40 was never coming back — but his brain couldn't let go of it.

In stock markets, anchoring works identically. You bought Yes Bank at ₹300. It falls to ₹100. You don't sell because "I just need it to come back to ₹300." Meanwhile, the fundamental story has completely changed — asset quality problems, management changes, regulatory action — none of which cares about your cost price of ₹300. Your ₹300 anchor is irrelevant to what the business is now worth. A stock does not know what you paid for it. The market does not care.
🚫 How Anchoring Sounds
"I'll sell when it comes back to my buy price of ₹180" — even though the business has fundamentally deteriorated
"The stock was at ₹500 six months ago — at ₹350 it's cheap" — comparing to a previous high instead of intrinsic value
"My profit target is 20% from my cost price" — using your personal cost as the valuation benchmark instead of fair value
"I won't book a loss — I'll average down" — adding more capital to a deteriorating investment to lower the anchor point
✅ The Rational Question Instead
"Would I buy this stock TODAY at this price, knowing everything I know now?" If No — why are you still holding it?
Your cost price is completely irrelevant to what the business is worth today. The market doesn't know or care what you paid.
Compare current price to your estimate of intrinsic value — NOT to your cost price or the historical high
Define your sell trigger when you BUY — before the emotional anchor forms. "I will exit if quarterly CFO/PAT drops below 0.7× for 2 years."
🛡️ The Anchoring Antidote — "Fresh Eyes" Decision Test
1
Imagine you don't own this stock. You have the equivalent cash in your bank today. Would you buy this stock at the current price? If not — you have an anchoring problem.
2
Remove your cost price from your portfolio view. Many brokers allow you to hide the unrealised P&L column. Evaluate each holding purely on current price vs current intrinsic value.
3
Always evaluate a sell decision against the OPPORTUNITY COST: "What else could this capital do?" If ₹1 lakh stuck in a deteriorating company could earn 15%+ elsewhere — the anchor is destroying compounding opportunity.
📈
Bias #2 — Causes Market Cycles
Recency Bias
Your brain gives excessive weight to what happened recently and assumes it will continue — causing investors to buy at market peaks (after recent rises) and sell at bottoms (after recent falls). This is the single biggest driver of buying high and selling low.
🔴 Very High Damage · Creates market bubbles and panics
The stock market rose 60% between March 2020 and December 2020. AMFI data shows that retail SIP amounts increased 40% in January–March 2021. Investors poured money in after the rise — because recent performance felt like a permanent trend. Then the market corrected 15% in mid-2021. SIP cancellations spiked. Investors who joined at the top sold at the bottom — the exact opposite of rational behaviour.

In prehistoric times, recency bias kept us alive: "A tiger attacked near the river yesterday — avoid the river today." But in financial markets, the price that rose 40% last quarter is often MORE likely to mean-revert than to continue rising. Valuations matter. Momentum reverses. The recency bias causes investors to extrapolate the recent past indefinitely — making them the most bullish just before peaks and the most bearish just before recoveries.
🚫 How Recency Bias Sounds
"This sector has outperformed for 3 years — I should increase my allocation to it" — momentum extrapolation without valuation check
"Markets have been falling for 3 months — I should stop my SIP until things improve" — selling the recovery before it happens
"This stock has beaten the index every year for 5 years — it will definitely outperform next year too" — ignoring mean reversion
"Everyone is talking about [sector] — it must be a good investment now" — confusing popularity (recent visibility) with opportunity
✅ What Contrarians Do Instead
Compare current sectoral PE vs 10-year median. If above the median by 30%+, recent outperformance is likely already priced in — reduce, don't add
Continue SIPs through corrections. SIP returns are highest for investors who maintained during 2008, 2020, and 2022 drawdowns — recency sellers missed these recoveries
Study base rates: after 3 consecutive years of 20%+ returns, probability of a 4th year correction rises above 60% historically. Mean reversion exists.
Ask: "Why did this happen recently?" Identify the specific catalyst. If the catalyst is now priced in or reversing, the trend won't continue.
🛡️ The Recency Bias Antidote — The 10-Year Context Rule
1
Before any allocation decision, pull a 10-year chart. Where is current valuation relative to the 10-year range? Near the top = caution. Near the bottom = opportunity. Never make allocation decisions based on the last 6–12 months alone.
2
Pre-commit to your asset allocation in writing when markets are calm. "I will maintain 70% equity, 30% debt. I will rebalance annually. I will not change this based on short-term market direction." Written commitments survive emotional moments better than unwritten intentions.
3
When everyone around you is excited (news coverage peaks, IPO subscriptions at 100×, colleagues discussing stocks) — tighten your entry criteria, not loosen them. Peak excitement = peak recency bias = dangerous entry point.
🔍
Bias #3 — Makes Research Useless
Confirmation Bias
After forming an initial opinion about a stock (usually when you first hear about it), your brain selectively seeks, interprets, and remembers information that confirms what you already believe — and dismisses or underweights contradictory evidence.
🟠 High Damage · Turns research into rationalisation
You read a bullish article about HFCL at ₹80 — new 5G contracts, government BharatNet project, huge optique fibre demand. You get excited. Then you start "researching." You read 5 more bullish articles (avoiding the bearish ones). You check the stock's recent price (up 40%). You ask friends who own it — they're all bullish. You join a Telegram group called "HFCL Multibagger." You have now done "research" — but you've only collected confirming evidence and built an echo chamber.

The bearish evidence existed all along: negative CFO for 4 years, debtor days at 300+, B2G payment delays. But your brain filtered it out because it contradicted what you already believed. Professional fund managers are specifically trained to seek out the most compelling bear case for every stock they own. This discipline — deliberately trying to prove yourself wrong — is exactly what confirmation bias prevents you from doing naturally.
🚫 How Confirmation Bias Sounds
"I read 10 analyst reports and they all agree the stock will rise" — but you only read reports from analysts who are bullish
"The bear arguments don't make sense to me" — but you haven't seriously engaged with the strongest version of the bear case
Joining WhatsApp/Telegram groups for stocks you own and enjoying the group's optimism without questioning it
"The company's management addressed all concerns in the concall" — after listening only for positives, not for management evasions
✅ The Pre-Mortem Approach
Before buying, deliberately find the 3 strongest reasons NOT to buy. Write them down. If you can't write compelling bear cases, you have a confirmation bias problem.
Specifically seek out negative analyst reports, short-seller research, and company-critical journalism. Force-read material you're initially inclined to dismiss.
Use the "Steel Man" technique: state the bear case as persuasively as possible — more persuasively than any bear actually argues it. Then rebut it. If you can't rebut it, it might be right.
After every quarterly result, ask: "Were any of my bear cases validated this quarter?" Be honest. If yes, investigate further — don't rationalise the bearish data away.
🛡️ The Confirmation Bias Antidote — Required Bear Case Writing
1
Make it a rule: for every stock you buy, write a minimum 3-point bear case before purchasing. If you can't identify 3 genuine risks, you haven't done enough research — or your confirmation bias is so strong that you can't see risks that exist.
2
Find someone who actively disagrees with your thesis (a friend, an analyst, an online commentator) and genuinely engage with their argument. Don't argue back — listen. Ask what evidence would change their mind. Then check if that evidence exists.
3
Leave WhatsApp and Telegram stock tip groups for stocks you already own. These echo chambers destroy the ability to evaluate contradictory evidence objectively. Your research environment determines the quality of your decisions.

Bias #4 — Destroys Compounding
Loss Aversion
Psychologists have proven that losing ₹100 feels roughly twice as bad as gaining ₹100 feels good. This asymmetry causes investors to hold losing stocks too long (avoiding the emotional pain of realising the loss) and sell winning stocks too early (locking in the pleasure of a gain before it disappears).
🟠 High Damage · Cuts winners, holds losers — exact opposite of optimal
Kahneman's research shows that the pain of a ₹1,000 loss feels like the equivalent of losing ₹2,000 in emotional intensity. This is why investors do the irrational thing: they sell stocks that are up 30% (removing the risk of that gain disappearing) while holding stocks that are down 40% (avoiding the painful act of confirming the loss by selling).

This creates the classic portfolio pattern: a collection of small winners that were sold too early and large losers that are still being held "waiting to recover." The mathematical consequence is devastating for long-term compounding. The winners (sold at +30%) could have become +500% over 7 years. The losers (held through -40%) may never recover. Loss aversion literally inverts the ideal strategy — where the advice should be "let winners run and cut losers quickly."
🚫 How Loss Aversion Sounds
"I'll sell when I at least break even" — refusing to exit a deteriorating business to avoid confirming the loss emotionally
"I should book some profits" — selling Nestle at +40% "before it falls" while holding Vodafone Idea at -65% "waiting to recover"
"I'm not really losing money if I don't sell" — the unrealised loss is exactly as real as the realised loss; the money is gone either way
"I'll average down — it must bounce" — doubling down on a fundamentally weakening stock because averaging down feels better than accepting the loss
✅ The Loss-Aversion Antidote
Evaluate winners and losers using identical criteria: "Would I buy this TODAY at this price?" If yes for a loser — hold. If no — sell, regardless of your cost basis.
Think in terms of total portfolio value, not individual stock P&L. Whether a rupee came from a winner or a loser — it's still a rupee. Redeploy capital to the best available opportunity regardless of tax/emotional history.
Set maximum drawdown rules in advance: "I will exit any position that falls 30% from my buy price AND where the fundamental thesis has also changed." Stick to this mechanically.
"Cut losses and let winners run" is the mathematically optimal strategy for a concentrated stock portfolio. The data supports this — but every emotional instinct resists it.
🛡️ The Loss Aversion Antidote — The Opportunity Cost Reframe
1
Never look at individual stock P&L in isolation. Always ask: "What is the opportunity cost of keeping this capital here vs my next-best idea?" A 40% paper loss in a stock you won't buy today is ₹X tied up that could compound at 15%/yr elsewhere.
2
Pre-define your exit criteria when you buy. "I will exit if: (a) quarterly result shows CFO/PAT below 0.6× for 2 years, OR (b) management guidance missed 3 quarters in a row." Written criteria remove the emotional moment-of-decision pain.
3
Tax-loss harvesting reframe: in taxable accounts, realising a loss generates a tax benefit that partially offsets the emotional pain. A ₹10,000 realised loss saves ₹1,500+ in short-term capital gains tax elsewhere. The loss is never purely negative.

Bias #5 — Causes Concentration Risk
Overconfidence Bias
After a few successful stock picks, your brain dramatically overestimates your edge over the market. You start concentrating positions, trading more frequently, ignoring risk, and believing your analytical ability is systematically superior — until a concentrated loss resets the lesson.
🟡 Medium-High Damage · Leads to concentration, excessive trading, ignoring risk
The most ironic aspect of overconfidence: it peaks immediately after a bull market where even mediocre stock selection produced gains. If Sensex rose 40% and your portfolio rose 50%, you conclude you have 10% alpha — that your skill added 10%. In reality, most of the 50% was market beta (the broad rally), and your stock selection may have been entirely due to luck and sector tilts rather than genuine analytical insight.

Overconfidence leads directly to under-diversification: "I know this company really well so I'll put 20% of my portfolio into it." The problem is that your confidence is calibrated by how much research you did — not by how accurately your research predicts outcomes. You can do 100 hours of research and still be completely wrong about a stock because you have information disadvantage vs institutional investors, and the market is pricing in information you don't have access to.
🚫 How Overconfidence Sounds
"I've done 200 hours of research on this stock — I know it better than any analyst" — research hours don't create information advantage
"I predicted 4 stocks correctly last year — I know how to pick stocks" — 4 correct predictions in a bull market prove almost nothing about skill
"I can time the market — I feel when it's about to correct" — multiple academic studies show active market timing destroys returns over 10-year periods
Holding 30–40% of portfolio in one or two stocks "because I'm very confident in them" — concentration that eliminates any margin for being wrong
✅ Calibrated Confidence
Separate skill from luck: attribute all market-matched returns to the index (luck/beta). Count only the alpha — returns above index. Was your alpha consistent over 3+ years across different market cycles?
Track prediction accuracy: write down every stock prediction with reasoning. At year end, score yourself. Most investors find their actual accuracy is far lower than their felt accuracy.
Maximum single-stock concentration: even high-conviction picks should be capped at 8–10% for most retail investors. No single analytical error should be able to destroy more than 10% of your portfolio.
Compete with yourself, not the market: did your process improve this year? Did you follow your own rules? Sustainable wealth comes from process discipline, not brilliant predictions.
🛡️ The Overconfidence Antidote — The 10% Position Limit Rule
1
Implement a hard position-size limit: no single stock above 10% of portfolio, no single sector above 30%. This structural rule eliminates overconfidence from harming you catastrophically, even when it's operating fully. You can be wrong on any single holding and still compound wealth.
2
Keep a prediction journal. Before each purchase, write: the expected outcome, the timeline, and the probability estimate. Review quarterly. After 2 years, your journal will show you exactly how accurate your confidence is — and calibrate it to reality.
3
Every quarter, ask: "What did I get wrong this quarter?" Not "what went right" — what went wrong and WHY. The investors who compound best are not those with the most correct predictions; they're those who learn fastest from their errors and have systems that limit the damage when they're wrong.

03 — Biases in Action

5 Real Stocks — Which Bias Trapped Investors?

These five examples show the exact thought pattern of biased investors — and the rational antidote that data-driven analysis would have provided. Each example is drawn from widely-held stocks in India between 2018 and 2025.

🏦
Yes Bank — Anchoring + Loss Aversion: The ₹300 Trap
Private Banking · NSE: YESBANK · Stock fell from ₹393 (2018) to ₹5 (2020) — 99% destruction
🚫 Anchoring to ₹393 🚫 Loss aversion held 99% destruction
⚓ The Bias Trap
Held from ₹393 → ₹5
Investors bought Yes Bank at ₹200–393 in 2017–18. When it fell to ₹150, they didn't sell ("it'll come back to my price"). At ₹80 — "I'll average down." At ₹30 — "Can't sell now, too much loss." At ₹5 — "It's too cheap to sell, must bounce." Anchoring + loss aversion worked together to hold through 99% destruction.
📊 What Data Said Early
NPAs: 3.2% → 18.9% in 2 years
BSE Annual Report filings in 2018–19 showed NPA ratios deteriorating sharply — from 3.2% to 5.1% to 8.9% to 18.9% over 4 quarters. CFO/PAT was negative in FY19. Screener data showed loan book growing despite rising NPAs. The data screamed exit — but anchoring to the ₹300 price prevented action.
✅ The Rational Exit
Exit trigger: NPA above 5%
A pre-defined exit rule — "I will exit any bank where NPA rises above 5% for 2 consecutive quarters" — would have triggered an exit around ₹180 (after Q2 FY19 results). Total loss would have been 10–30% instead of 99%. Pre-committed rules eliminate the cost price anchor by making the sell trigger based on fundamentals, not price.
🚨 The Lesson: The anchor of "I paid ₹300" caused investors to hold Yes Bank through every warning sign — rising NPAs, RBI inquiry, CFO resignation, governance concerns. Each time a rational investor would have exited, the anchor pulled them back. Loss aversion ensured they couldn't confirm the loss emotionally at ₹100, ₹50, ₹20. The combination destroyed portfolios that could have redeployed to Bajaj Finance (which rose 5× in the same period) if the bias-driven holding had been resolved at ₹150.

IT Sector 2021 — Recency Bias at the Exact Peak
IT Sector · Infosys / TCS / Wipro · Heavy buying at 40× PE after 3 years of 30%+ returns
🚫 Recency extrapolation IT PE hit 40× in Oct 2021 Fell 35–45% by 2022
📈 The Recency Trap
IT funds: ₹8,000 Cr inflows Oct 2021
Indian IT stocks (Infosys, TCS, Wipro, HCL) outperformed by 50%+ in FY21 due to COVID-accelerated digital transformation. Investors poured ₹8,000+ Cr into IT sector funds in October 2021, extrapolating the 3-year trend forward. "Digital transformation will continue forever — IT is the future." IT PE ratios hit 38–42× (vs 10-year median 22×). Classic recency extrapolation to a PE extreme.
📊 What History Said
IT at 40× PE = 80% above median
Screener.in historical PE data for Infosys showed: 10-year median PE = 22×. In October 2021, Infosys traded at 38×. FCF yield was 1.8% — below the 10-year bond yield of 6.3%. Simple mean-reversion math: 38× → 22× = a 42% compression even without any earnings slowdown. The data was available to anyone checking historical valuations.
✅ The Rational Approach
Reduce when PE > 130% of median
A valuation-based allocation rule: "When sector PE exceeds 130% of 10-year median, reduce allocation by 30% regardless of recent performance." This would have triggered IT reduction in August–September 2021. The 35–45% correction that followed in 2022 would have become a buying opportunity rather than a painful holding experience.
🎯 The Lesson: Three years of IT outperformance (2019–2021) created such a powerful recency bias that investors poured the most money in exactly at the peak — when valuations had made future returns statistically unlikely. The 10-year PE history — available free on Screener.in — would have shown the clear overvaluation months before the correction. Recency bias is cured by historical context, not by more research about recent trends.

Nestle India — Confirmation Bias Missed the Royalty Cost
Food Manufacturing · NSE: NESTLEIND · Buried annual report detail ignored by confirmation-biased bulls
🔍 Confirmation bias Royalty: 4.5% of revenue Fully transparent in filings
🔍 The Confirmation Trap
Only read the bull story
Nestle India is genuinely a high-quality business. But confirmation-biased investors read only the positive story: "Zero debt, negative WC, CFO/PAT >1.0×, brand dominance." They missed — or dismissed — one structural cost disclosed clearly in every Annual Report: 4.5% of revenue paid as royalty to Nestle S.A. Switzerland. At ₹19,000 Cr revenue, that's ₹855 Cr/year leaving India to the parent company — reducing economic profit available to Indian shareholders.
📊 What the Filing Said
Note 32: Royalty ₹855 Cr/yr
BSE Annual Report, Note 32 (Related Party Transactions): "Royalty paid to Société des Produits Nestlé S.A. — ₹854.7 Cr (FY24)." This represented 23% of reported PAT flowing to the Swiss parent. A bull investor who read only the headline PAT would miss that nearly a quarter of "profit" was predetermined to leave. This is a legitimate cost — but must be factored into valuation models.
✅ Reading What You Don't Want To
Always read Notes to Accounts
Force yourself to read the Related Party Transactions note in every Annual Report — not the headline numbers. For Nestle, knowing the royalty structure makes the business more understandable, not less. It explains why OPM is 23% rather than 27% it would otherwise be, and allows you to build a model that accounts for this permanent cost rather than being surprised by it.
⚠️ The Lesson: Confirmation bias doesn't just cause bad investment decisions — it also causes incomplete investment analysis in otherwise good companies. Nestle India is genuinely excellent. But investors who only read confirming information missed the royalty structure, mispriced the sustainable PAT margin, and overestimated intrinsic value slightly. Reading the bear case and the awkward details doesn't mean avoiding good investments — it means pricing them correctly.

HFCL — Loss Aversion Held While Fundamentals Collapsed
Telecom Manufacturing · NSE: HFCL · Loss aversion + confirmation bias kept investors through fundamental deterioration
🚫 Loss aversion held 🔍 Only reading 5G bull case CFO/PAT: −2.95× for 3 yrs
😰 The Loss Aversion Trap
Held at −60%, reading 5G news
Investors bought HFCL at ₹100–140 during the 5G excitement in 2021. When it fell to ₹80, loss aversion kicked in: "I'll wait for it to come back." Simultaneously, confirmation bias made them read only 5G contract news while ignoring the cash flow filings showing CFO/PAT deteriorating to −2.95×. The combination: can't sell (loss aversion) + can't see bad news (confirmation bias) = held through a structural cash quality collapse.
📊 What Cash Flow Said
Debtor days: 217 → 343 over 5 yrs
Screener.in Cash Flow tab showed HFCL CFO negative in 4 of 5 years. Debtor days escalating from 217 to 343 — government clients not paying. Cumulative cash consumed: ₹2,044 Cr over 5 years while reporting ₹932 Cr cumulative PAT. The data said: "The profits are fictional." But loss-averse investors read the 5G news (confirming) and ignored the cash flow (contradicting).
✅ The Rules-Based Exit
Exit trigger: CFO/PAT <0 for 2 years
A pre-committed exit rule — "I will exit any manufacturing company where CFO/PAT is negative for 2 consecutive years without a specific capacity-expansion explanation" — would have triggered exit well before the deepest losses. Pre-commitment removes the emotional loss aversion moment: the rule fires mechanically regardless of how painful the realised loss feels.
🚨 The Lesson: HFCL showcased two biases working simultaneously: loss aversion prevented selling ("I'll wait to break even") while confirmation bias fed the 5G narrative ("it will recover once 5G contracts pay"). The rational investor with pre-defined exit rules (CFO/PAT threshold) would have exited long before the most painful drawdown levels. The 5G opportunity is real — but HFCL's ability to benefit from it while collecting cash from government clients is the question that both biases prevented investors from asking seriously.

Polycab India — Overconfidence After 5× Gain Led to Over-Concentration
Wires & Cables · NSE: POLYCAB · Investors who got the thesis right overweighted dangerously in subsequent cycles
😎 Overconfidence post-5× 30%+ portfolio in one stock ✅ Right thesis, wrong sizing
😎 The Overconfidence Trap
25% portfolio in Polycab at ₹5,000
Investors who correctly identified Polycab in 2020 at ₹1,000–1,200 and rode it to ₹5,000+ developed severe overconfidence. "I understand this company better than anyone." Result: increased position to 25–30% of portfolio. This level of concentration means any single SEBI investigation, major customer loss, or commodity shock that permanently impairs the business could destroy 25%+ of total wealth in one stock.
📊 The Valuation Reality
PE at 38× vs median 44×: still fair
At ₹5,000 in 2025, Polycab trades at 38× PE — below its 5-year median of 44×. This is reasonable for its FCF growth trajectory. The business thesis is intact. The problem is not the business — it is the position size concentration. Even a correct thesis can cause portfolio damage if the sizing assumes zero probability of being wrong.
✅ The Position Limit Rule
Max 10% single stock, always
Even for high-conviction winners, trim back to 8–10% when a position grows beyond that due to price appreciation. This "trimming winners" rule forces some profit-taking at higher prices and ensures no single error can be catastrophic. The trimmed capital is redeployed to the next best idea — creating portfolio compounding through diversified compounders rather than single-stock concentration risk.
✅ The Lesson: Polycab is a genuinely excellent business. Getting the thesis right — and profiting 5× on it — is excellent investing. The overconfidence bias appears in the NEXT step: assuming that because you were right this time, you should concentrate 25%+ of your portfolio in it at a much higher price. Research accuracy and position-sizing discipline are two entirely different skills. The 10% maximum single-stock rule protects compounding even when your fundamental analysis is completely correct.
💡
The Master Antidote
Write It Down Before the Emotion Arrives — Process Beats Willpower Every Time
You cannot defeat cognitive biases through willpower in the moment of decision. By the time you're staring at a 30% loss, loss aversion has already taken control. By the time every analyst is bullish on an IT sector at 40× PE, recency bias has already coloured your judgment. Willpower fails under emotional pressure — every time.

The only reliable antidote is a written process that you commit to before the emotional moments arrive. Write your buy rationale (including bear cases). Write your sell trigger (specific, measurable, fundamental — not price-based). Write your maximum position size. Write your review schedule. Then follow the written rules mechanically when the market tests your conviction — which it will, consistently, in every investment cycle.

The investors who compound the most wealth over 10–20 years are not those with the best analysis. They are those with the most disciplined process — and that process exists precisely to override the predictable, systematic errors that human brains make in financial markets. The five biases in this chapter will attack you every quarter. Your written process is the only permanent defence.
