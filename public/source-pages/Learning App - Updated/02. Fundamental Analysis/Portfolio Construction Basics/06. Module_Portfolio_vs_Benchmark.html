01 — Key Learning Outcome

Why Compare to a Benchmark? Because Effort Must Justify Itself

📊
Your Core Takeaway
Alpha = Your Portfolio Return minus the Benchmark Return (Nifty 500) for the same period. If your portfolio returned 18% while Nifty 500 returned 22%, your alpha is −4%. All your research, analysis, and stock picking cost you 4% per year versus doing nothing and buying an index fund.
Imagine two factory workers. Worker A works 60 hours a week and produces 100 units. Worker B works 40 hours a week and also produces 100 units. Who is more efficient? Obviously Worker B — same output for less effort. Now imagine: Worker A produces 90 units and Worker B produces 100 units. Worker A is actually destroying value through their extra 20 hours of effort.

Active stock picking works exactly the same way. An index fund (Nifty 500) earns the market return with zero research effort. If your active portfolio — where you spend hundreds of hours researching — returns less than the index, you are Worker A. You are destroying value through effort.

This is not about ego. It is about honesty. The first step to improving your investing is measuring it accurately. Most retail investors have never calculated their true XIRR against a benchmark — they only remember their winning stocks. The stocks they sold at a loss are forgotten. The timing mistakes are invisible. Only an accurate benchmark comparison reveals the truth.

Nifty 500 (rather than Nifty 50) is the appropriate benchmark for most active stock pickers because Nifty 500 includes small and mid-cap stocks — the same universe where active stock pickers claim to find their alpha. Comparing against Nifty 50 is like a marathon runner claiming victory in a 5km race.

02 — Alpha, Beta & XIRR Explained

The Three Numbers Every Investor Must Know

Before you can measure your performance against a benchmark, you need to understand exactly what you're measuring — and calculate it correctly. These three concepts form the complete performance measurement toolkit.

Think of it as a race. Nifty 500 is the pacer — running at 16% per year without thinking. Portfolio A (your research-driven picks) finishes ahead at 22% — positive alpha of +6%, research justified. Portfolio B finishes behind at 12% — negative alpha of −4%, research actually cost you money versus doing nothing.

⚡
Alpha (α)
The Excess Return You Generate
α = Rp − Rb
Alpha measures the return your portfolio generates above what the benchmark (Nifty 500) would have returned for the same period. It is the purest measure of your stock-picking skill, stripped of the market's general rise or fall.

Positive alpha (+5%) means your stocks outperformed the market by 5% — your research, timing, and selection genuinely added value beyond what any index fund investor received.

Negative alpha (−3%) means despite all your effort, your portfolio underperformed the index by 3%. You would have been better off, richer, and less stressed simply buying a Nifty 500 index fund and doing nothing.

Alpha should be measured over a minimum 3-year rolling period. One year is too short — you could be lucky in a single year. Three years across at least one market correction proves the skill is real and repeatable, not a bull-market coincidence.
Example: Portfolio XIRR = 20% · Nifty 500 XIRR = 15% · Alpha = +5%. Your research added ₹5 for every ₹100 invested per year above what the index gave for free. Over 10 years on ₹10 lakh → difference is ₹8 lakh extra wealth.
📐
Beta (β)
Your Portfolio's Market Sensitivity
β = Cov(Rp, Rm) / Var(Rm)
Beta measures how much your portfolio moves relative to the overall market. Beta = 1.0 means your portfolio moves exactly with the market — if Nifty rises 10%, your portfolio also rises 10%. Beta above 1.0 means your portfolio is more volatile than the market.

Beta > 1.0: High-beta portfolio — rises more in bull markets but falls more in bear markets. Typical for portfolios heavy in small and mid-cap stocks, cyclical sectors (steel, chemicals). Higher risk, potentially higher reward.

Beta < 1.0: Low-beta portfolio — more defensive, falls less in corrections. Typical for consumer staples (Nestle), pharma (Sun Pharma), and large-cap stocks with predictable earnings. Lower risk, lower potential upside.

The key insight: High alpha with low beta is the holy grail. It means you're generating above-market returns with below-market risk — true skill. High alpha with high beta just means you took more risk and got lucky in a bull market — the same result a levered index fund would produce.
Nestle India beta ≈ 0.4 (defensive). Polycab beta ≈ 1.3 (growth stock). A portfolio of 60% Polycab-type + 40% Nestle-type = beta ≈ 1.0 — market-neutral risk with potential for positive alpha from stock selection.
🧮
XIRR
The Only Accurate Return Measure
=XIRR(values, dates)
XIRR (Extended Internal Rate of Return) is the only accurate way to measure returns when you're making multiple investments at different times. Simple return (End/Start − 1) fails completely when you've been making SIPs, adding lump sums, or making withdrawals — which is how every real portfolio works.

Why simple return lies: You invest ₹1 lakh in January and ₹9 lakh in December (total ₹10 lakh). The portfolio rises 20% in January-November and falls 5% in December. Simple return looks excellent (total portfolio up). But XIRR reveals most of your money went in just before the 5% fall — your true annualised return is much lower.

How to calculate XIRR: In Excel or Google Sheets, put your investment dates in column A (negative = money out, positive = current value). Put corresponding amounts in column B. Use =XIRR(B:B, A:A). Compare this number to Nifty 500's XIRR over the identical date range. The difference is your alpha.

For the benchmark XIRR, assume you invested the same amounts on the same dates in a Nifty 500 index fund. This gives a fair apple-to-apple comparison.
Tracking example: 5 SIP investments of ₹20,000 each over 18 months + current portfolio value. XIRR = 14.2%. Nifty 500 XIRR for same amounts on same dates = 17.8%. Alpha = −3.6%. Important truth revealed.

How to Track Your Portfolio vs Benchmark — Four Methods
📊
Monthly Snapshot Method
Simplest · Beginner-Friendly
Record your portfolio value on the first of every month alongside the Nifty 500 level. Calculate a simple return for each month and year. Compare your portfolio's cumulative return to Nifty 500's cumulative return starting from the same date. While not as precise as XIRR for multiple investments, this reveals the directional trend quickly — are you consistently ahead, behind, or tracking?
1
Record portfolio total value on 1st of each month in a spreadsheet
2
Record Nifty 500 index level on same date (from NSE website, free)
3
Calculate: (Current/Start − 1) × 100 for both. Subtract Nifty 500 from your portfolio = monthly alpha
🧮
Excel XIRR Method
Most Accurate · For SIP Investors
The gold standard for measuring returns when you invest at irregular intervals. Every time you buy or sell, record the date and amount. Your current portfolio value is the final "positive" cash flow. Apply XIRR to get the true annualised return. Then calculate the hypothetical XIRR if you had invested the same amounts on the same dates in a Nifty 500 index fund. The difference is your genuine alpha.
1
List all investments as negative numbers (money going out) with dates
2
Add current portfolio value as a positive number with today's date
3
Formula: =XIRR(values column, dates column) · Repeat for Nifty 500 hypothetical
📱
App-Based Tracking
Automated · Low Effort
Several apps calculate XIRR automatically by syncing with your broker. Smallcase, Kite (Zerodha), INDMoney, and Groww all show portfolio XIRR. Set Nifty 500 as your benchmark in the app settings — most platforms support this. The app recalculates daily. The limitation: apps calculate XIRR for holdings only, not for holdings you've already sold. For true historical alpha, the manual XIRR method remains more accurate.
1
Open broker app → Portfolio → Performance section
2
Set benchmark to Nifty 500 Total Return Index (not just price return)
3
View 1yr, 3yr, and since-inception XIRR vs benchmark — all three matter
📋
Quarterly Review Discipline
Process · Prevents Bias
Set a fixed date every quarter (March 31, June 30, September 30, December 31) to review portfolio alpha. Calculate rolling 1-year and 3-year alpha. If rolling 3-year alpha is positive — your process is working, continue. If rolling 3-year alpha is negative — evaluate whether the underperformance is systemic (wrong process) or temporary (tough sector). Three consecutive negative-alpha years = strong signal to switch to index funds.
1
Every quarter end: update XIRR, compare to Nifty 500, record the alpha
2
Ask: "Did I follow my process this quarter? Did any holding change its fundamental thesis?"
3
Decision rule: 3-yr alpha consistently negative → evaluate switching to index fund allocation

The Alpha Reality Check
Measure First, Then Decide: Active vs Index vs Hybrid
After calculating your rolling 3-year XIRR and comparing to Nifty 500, one of three outcomes is possible — and each has a clear implication:

Consistent positive alpha (>3% for 3+ years): Your fundamental analysis process is working. Continue active stock picking, but maintain discipline — the process that generated alpha, not the specific stocks, is what should be preserved. Most importantly: don't let overconfidence grow your position sizes beyond the risk limits that make your process survivable when you're wrong.

Alpha near zero (±1% for 3 years): You are tracking the index with extra work and extra risk. Consider shifting 50–70% of the portfolio to a low-cost Nifty 500 index fund and actively managing only the remaining 30–50% with your highest-conviction positions. The index component provides compounding foundation; the active component provides alpha opportunity.

Consistent negative alpha (negative for 3+ years): Your process is systematically adding costs and errors beyond what the market gives freely. The honest, wealth-maximising decision is to move the majority of the portfolio to a Nifty 500 index fund. This is not failure — 85% of professional fund managers also fail to generate consistent alpha. Recognising this and acting on it is an act of financial intelligence, not defeat.
