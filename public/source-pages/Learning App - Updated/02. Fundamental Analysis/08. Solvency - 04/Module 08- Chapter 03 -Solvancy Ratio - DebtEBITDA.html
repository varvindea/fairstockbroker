01 — Key Learning Outcome

What Will You Learn From This?

📅
Your Core Takeaway
The Debt/EBITDA ratio tells you exactly how many years it would take a company to fully repay its debt if it used 100% of its operating cash profit — making it the gold standard for credit and leverage analysis.
Think of it like a home loan repayment timeline. If you earn ₹10 lakh per year and owe ₹30 lakh, it would take 3 years to repay at full capacity — a Debt/Income ratio of 3. Companies work the same way. Debt/EBITDA of 3x means the company would need 3 full years of operating profit to clear all debt. The lower this number, the faster debt can be repaid and the safer the company financially. This ratio is the primary tool used by credit rating agencies (CRISIL, ICRA), banks setting lending limits, and private equity investors evaluating acquisitions. If you want to understand why one company gets an AAA rating while another struggles to raise funds, Debt/EBITDA is almost always part of the answer.

02 — The Formula

Breaking Down the Debt/EBITDA Formula

The formula divides what the company owes (Total Debt) by what it earns before the big deductions (EBITDA). The result tells you how many "earning years" are needed to theoretically wipe the debt slate clean.

3 calendar years of EBITDA (₹400 Cr each) stack up to repay ₹1,200 Cr of total debt. Debt/EBITDA = 3x — a healthy leverage level.

D

Total Debt — Everything Owed to Financial Creditors

All interest-bearing borrowings: bank term loans, working capital loans, debentures, bonds, NCDs, external commercial borrowings. Found in the Balance Sheet under "Borrowings." This is the numerator — the mountain that needs to be climbed.

E

EBITDA — Earnings Before Big Deductions

EBITDA = Revenue minus Cost of Goods and Operating Expenses, but before Interest, Tax, Depreciation, and Amortisation. It represents the raw operating cash-generating ability of the business — closest to actual cash flow from operations before financial and accounting deductions.

=

The Result — A Repayment Timeline in Years

The ratio literally tells you: "If this company put all its EBITDA towards repaying debt, how many years would it take?" Lower is always better. A ratio of 1x = excellent. 3x = healthy. 5x+ = risky. Banks use this directly to set maximum debt limits before lending.

🟢
< 1x

Excellent — Fortress of Financial Strength

Companies with Debt/EBITDA below 1x could technically repay all their debt within a single operating year. This level is rare in capital-intensive industries but common for IT, FMCG, and pharmaceutical businesses with high margins and minimal debt. Credit rating agencies treat this as their strongest category. Banks compete to lend to these companies, offering the best rates. The company has extraordinary flexibility — to acquire, return capital, or weather any storm.

🔵
1 – 3x

Healthy — The Banker's Sweet Spot

Most well-run Indian companies in FMCG, pharmaceuticals, and established manufacturing operate in the 1–3x range. This is where banks are comfortable lending without heavy covenants, and where rating agencies assign strong investment-grade ratings (AA or above). The company can service debt from operations, still invest in growth, and maintain dividend payments — all simultaneously. The worked example in this chapter (Debt/EBITDA = 3x) sits right at this boundary.

🟡
3 – 5x

Elevated — Requires Stable, Predictable Cash Flows

Infrastructure, power, and telecom companies regularly operate in this range because their capital-intensive nature requires heavy borrowing upfront. The key differentiator is revenue predictability: a toll road company with 30-year concession agreements has reliable EBITDA, making a 4x ratio manageable. A consumer goods company at 4x would raise red flags. Banks add restrictive loan covenants at this level, and rating agencies push harder before granting investment-grade status.

🔴
> 5x

High Risk — Default Risk Rises Sharply

Above 5x, the company is in genuinely dangerous territory. Even a modest 20% decline in EBITDA could mean it cannot service interest payments, let alone repay principal. Rating agencies typically assign sub-investment grade or speculative ratings. Many loan covenants have "trigger clauses" that call the entire loan immediately if Debt/EBITDA exceeds 4–5x. This level often precedes debt restructuring, asset sales under distress, or referral to the NCLT (insolvency tribunal) in India.

04 — The Simple Analogy

Think of Your Annual Income vs Your Loan

The clearest way to understand Debt/EBITDA is through a personal finance lens — comparing your total home loan to your annual take-home salary. The ratio tells you how many years of income it would take to repay the loan in full. Companies work identically.

📅

It Measures Repayment Time, Not Just Debt Size

A ₹70 lakh loan isn't scary to someone earning ₹35 lakh per year (Debt/Income = 2x). But it's terrifying to someone earning ₹5 lakh per year (Debt/Income = 14x). Debt/EBITDA captures this relative burden — how long would the current earnings take to repay the entire debt? It contextualises the absolute debt number within the company's earning power.

🏦

Banks Literally Use This to Set Loan Limits

When a company approaches a bank for a loan, the credit officer calculates Debt/EBITDA before anything else. Most banks have internal policies: "We don't lend if Debt/EBITDA exceeds 4x for manufacturing" or "Maximum 3x for consumer companies." This ratio directly determines how much credit a company can access and at what interest rate.

📉

The Buffer Against Earnings Decline

A company with Debt/EBITDA of 2x can see EBITDA fall 50% and still meet all debt obligations. A company at 5x can only absorb a 20% EBITDA drop before debt becomes unsustainable. This "buffer" concept explains why rating agencies use this ratio to stress-test companies against recession scenarios — they ask: "At what level of EBITDA decline does this company fail?"

⚡

Why EBITDA and Not Net Profit?

EBITDA is used instead of net profit because it's a better proxy for cash available to service debt. Depreciation and amortisation are non-cash charges — the company doesn't actually write a cheque for them. Interest and taxes are paid after EBITDA is generated. So EBITDA represents the raw cash pool before financial obligations — making it the most relevant "repayment capacity" measure for creditors.

06 — How to Apply Debt/EBITDA

3 Smart Ways to Use This Ratio

Debt/EBITDA is most powerful when tracked over time, benchmarked within sector, and used alongside the Interest Coverage Ratio for a complete solvency picture.

📉
Track the Trend Over 5 Years
A falling Debt/EBITDA over 5 years is one of the most positive signals in solvency analysis — the company is either growing EBITDA, repaying debt, or both. Conversely, a rising Debt/EBITDA (especially from 2x to 4x) is an early warning: the company is taking on debt faster than it can grow earnings. Always compare the current ratio to the 5-year trend, not just the current number in isolation.
⚖️
Compare Within Sector Always
A Debt/EBITDA of 4x for a regulated power utility is very different from 4x for a consumer electronics company. Power utilities have long-term, contracted revenue — their EBITDA is predictable and stable. Consumer electronics has volatile demand. The ratio must be benchmarked against the sector average and direct competitors. The company with the lowest Debt/EBITDA in its peer group almost always has the best credit profile and the most financial flexibility.
🔗
Pair with Interest Coverage Ratio
Debt/EBITDA tells you the total repayment burden. Interest Coverage (EBIT ÷ Interest) tells you whether the company can pay the annual interest bill from current earnings. Together they give the complete solvency picture. A company can have Debt/EBITDA of 4x but ICR of 8x (fine — earnings easily cover interest). Or Debt/EBITDA of 2x but ICR of 1.5x (concerning — even moderate debt is barely serviceable). Always use both.
⚠️
Important Caveat
EBITDA Is Not Cash Flow — It's an Approximation, and It Can Be Manipulated!
EBITDA is widely used because it's a practical approximation of operating cash generation — but it's not actual free cash flow. It ignores working capital changes (if customers pay late, cash flow is lower than EBITDA suggests), capital maintenance expenditure (the real cost of keeping aging assets running), and the actual cash tax paid. A company with EBITDA of ₹400 crore but massive working capital bloat and maintenance capex might have actual free cash flow of only ₹150 crore — making its effective "Debt/Free Cash Flow" ratio 3x worse than Debt/EBITDA suggests. Always check the Cash Flow Statement alongside Debt/EBITDA. Screener.in and Trendlyne both publish this ratio for listed Indian companies — use them as a starting point, then verify against the balance sheet directly.

Remember Debt/EBITDA Instantly

Formula Trick · Debt/EBITDA = Repayment Years
Debt/EBITDA = Total Debt ÷ EBITDA — how many years of operating cash earnings to repay all debt
"Debt/EBITDA answers: if the company paid all its earnings toward debt, how many years until it’s debt-free?"
D— Total Debt: Long-term borrowings + Short-term borrowings + Current maturities of LTD. Same as used in D/E ratio. Does NOT include trade payables.
E— EBITDA: Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation & Amortisation. Proxy for operating cash generation. EBITDA = EBIT + Depreciation. Or from P&L: PAT + Tax + Interest + Depreciation.
📉Loan analogy: You earn ₹10 lakh/year. You owe ₹30 lakh on a loan. Debt/EBITDA = 30/10 = 3×. It would take 3 years of your full income to repay the loan. A company with Debt/EBITDA 3× is in the same position relative to its earnings.
💡 Finding on Screener.in: Not always directly shown. Calculate manually: Balance Sheet → Borrowings (sum of non-current + current borrowings) = Total Debt. P&L → Net Profit + Tax + Finance Costs + Depreciation = EBITDA. Divide. Or: search for “Debt to EBITDA” on the company page — some company pages show it in the Ratios section.

Benchmark Trick · The 1–3–5 Repayment Zones
Below 1× = debt-free in under a year. 1–3× = safe. 3–5× = watch. Above 5× = high risk for most sectors.
"Under 1 = excellent. 1–3 = safe. 3–5 = monitor. Over 5 = concern for non-infra companies."
⭐Debt/EBITDA < 1×: Company can repay ALL debt in under 1 year from EBITDA alone. TCS, Nestlé, Asian Paints, Colgate — debt-free or near-zero. No solvency risk. Preferred by rating agencies for AAA rating potential.
🟢1–3×: Conservative leverage. Maruti (~0.2×), HUL (~0.3×), quality mid-caps. Lenders comfortable lending at prime rates. CRISIL/ICRA AA+ territory.
🟡3–5×: Moderate–high leverage. Capital goods, cement, infrastructure companies. Banks lend but watch closely. Covenant trigger zone in many project finance loans. Requires strong ICR alongside.
🚫> 5× (non-infra): High leverage. Rating agency concern territory. Debt covenants may trigger. For infrastructure (roads, ports, power): 5–8× may be acceptable with long-term PPAs. For manufacturing/FMCG: >4× = investigate urgently.
💡 Rating agency rule of thumb: Moody’s, S&P, CRISIL consider Debt/EBITDA < 2× as investment grade comfortable for most manufacturing companies. Above 4.5×: typically sub-investment grade (BB or below). Above 6×: junk territory for non-infrastructure. Banks in India use an internal “Debt/EBITDA covenant” of typically 3.5–4.5× in project finance term sheets.
🔌
EBITDA Trick · Why EBITDA, Not Net Profit?
EBITDA is used instead of net profit because it is a closer proxy to operating cash flow and excludes financing decisions
"EBITDA adds back depreciation (non-cash) and interest+tax (financing/government). It shows raw operating earnings before capital structure choices."
DDepreciation added back: Depreciation is non-cash. A factory worth ₹100 crore depreciating at ₹10 crore/year doesn’t cost ₹10 crore in cash this year. EBITDA adds this back to show actual cash generated. Crucial for capital-intensive companies (Tata Steel, L&T) with very high depreciation.
IInterest excluded: Debt/EBITDA measures whether debt can be repaid from operating earnings — BEFORE paying interest. This makes it comparable across companies with different capital structures (some use more equity, some more debt).
TTax excluded: Tax rates differ by company (MAT, surcharges, exemptions, loss carry-forwards). Excluding tax makes the denominator comparable across companies with different tax situations.
💡 EBITDA limitation: EBITDA is NOT the same as free cash flow. It ignores working capital changes (receivables/inventory build-up can consume EBITDA) and actual capex. A company with EBITDA ₹500 crore but ₹400 crore annual capex has only ₹100 crore of real free cash flow to repay debt. For capex-heavy companies, “Debt/EBITDA–Capex” or “Debt/FCF” is a stricter measure.

India Sector Trick · Debt/EBITDA Ranges by Industry
IT/FMCG: 0–0.5×. Pharma/Auto: 0–1.5×. Cement/Capital goods: 1–3×. Infrastructure: 3–7×. Telecom: 3–6×.
"Debt/EBITDA benchmarks reflect how much debt a sector’s cash flows can safely support. PPAs and long contracts allow higher leverage."
IIT/Software: TCS, Infosys: 0–0.1× (near-zero debt, high EBITDA). Debt/EBITDA near zero = ideal. Any IT company above 1.5× = investigate why debt is needed.
CCement: UltraTech 1–2.5×, Shree Cement 0.3–1.5× (consistently lower than peers = higher quality). At cycle trough, cement Debt/EBITDA can spike to 3.5–4× temporarily.

SSteel: JSW Steel 1.5–4× cyclically. At commodity peak: 1.5×. At trough: 4×+. Invest at trough when Debt/EBITDA is rising but EBITDA recovering. Exit at peak when Debt/EBITDA is low but has bottomed.

Credit Rating Trick · How Rating Agencies Use Debt/EBITDA
CRISIL, ICRA, Moody’s use Debt/EBITDA as the #1 leverage metric in credit analysis. Know their thresholds.
"Banks lend to companies. Rating agencies grade companies. Both use Debt/EBITDA as the primary leverage question before saying yes."
AAAA rating (CRISIL): Debt/EBITDA typically < 1.5× for manufacturing companies. Combined with strong ICR (>8×) and D/E < 0.5. Tata Consultancy Services (0.0×), Asian Paints (<0.2×) historically in this territory.
AAA rating (CRISIL/ICRA): Debt/EBITDA 1.5–2.5× typical for manufacturing. Still investment grade. Most quality mid-large cap companies. Bajaj Finance, L&T, Kotak Mahindra: various sub-categories of AA.
BBB and below (Sub-investment grade): Debt/EBITDA above 4.5× for manufacturing. Higher yield required by lenders. Covenant triggers activate. Companies in this zone face higher borrowing costs that further compress EBITDA — a negative spiral.
💡 Bond covenant example: A company issues an NCD (Non-Convertible Debenture). Term sheet states: “Debt/EBITDA covenant: must not exceed 3.5× at any year-end. Breach triggers early redemption demand.” If EBITDA declines in a downturn and Debt/EBITDA crosses 3.5: lenders can demand full repayment immediately — even if the company was paying interest. This covenant makes Debt/EBITDA critically important for any company with outstanding NCDs or rated term loans.
🌟
Master Trick · Debt/EBITDA + D/E + ICR Triple Screen
Three solvency ratios together give a complete credit picture: stock of debt + repayment capacity + flow coverage
"D/E = how much debt. Debt/EBITDA = how long to repay. ICR = can you pay interest now. All three together = full solvency picture."
DD/E: Balance sheet leverage — how many rupees borrowed per rupee of equity. Stock measure. Sensitive to equity base size. Ideal: below sector median and declining.
EDebt/EBITDA: Repayment capacity measure — years of EBITDA needed to repay all debt. Normalises for asset-intensity. Ideal: below 3× for manufacturing; declining over 5 years.
IICR: Immediate interest payment capacity — how many times EBIT covers current interest bill. Flow measure. Ideal: above 5× and stable/improving. ICR below 1.5 = distress regardless of other ratios.
💡 Triple screen Screener.in filter: D/E < 1 AND ICR > 4 AND Debt/EBITDA < 2 AND all three improving over 5 years. This combination identifies companies with: conservative balance sheet leverage (D/E), strong repayment capacity (Debt/EBITDA), and comfortable interest coverage (ICR). Returns: TCS, Asian Paints, Nestlé, Pidilite, Titan — India’s solvency-fortress compounders.

📚 Glossary

Key Terms Glossary

Every Debt/EBITDA term — from EBITDA components to covenant triggers to credit rating thresholds — defined with Indian company examples.

🔍
Debt/EBITDA Ratio
Definition
Debt/EBITDA = Total Debt ÷ EBITDA. Answers the question: "How many years of operating cash earnings would it take to repay all the company's debt?" A Debt/EBITDA of 3× means the company would need 3 full years of EBITDA — applied entirely to debt repayment — to clear its obligations. Lower is better (faster repayment). This metric is used extensively by banks (before lending), rating agencies (credit scoring), and private equity (acquisition leverage assessment). Unlike D/E (which compares debt to equity book value), Debt/EBITDA compares debt to actual earnings capacity — making it more economically meaningful for assessing repayment ability.
💡 Interpretation examples: TCS Debt/EBITDA ~0.0× (debt-free): could theoretically repay all debt in 0 years. JSW Steel FY24 Debt/EBITDA ~2.5× (manageable for steel cycle). Adani Ports ~5× (infrastructure — acceptable with port concessions). Vodafone Idea ~12×+ (distressed — debt vastly exceeds any realistic repayment capacity from current EBITDA). The ratio immediately reveals whether a company is financially comfortable or stretched beyond repair.
EBITDA (Denominator)
Components
EBITDA = Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation and Amortisation. Proxy for operating cash generation capacity. Calculated from India P&L as: Net Profit + Income Tax Expense + Finance Costs + Depreciation & Amortisation = EBITDA. Alternatively: EBIT + Depreciation = EBITDA. EBITDA is used because it: removes non-cash charges (depreciation) making it comparable across capital-intensive vs. asset-light businesses; removes interest and tax making it capital-structure neutral; and approximates the cash earnings available to service debt. EBITDA margin = EBITDA ÷ Revenue — commonly reported by managements in quarterly earnings calls.
💡 EBITDA calculation: Shree Cement FY24: Net Profit ₹3,600 crore + Tax ₹1,200 crore + Finance Costs ₹400 crore + Depreciation ₹1,800 crore = EBITDA ₹7,000 crore. Total Debt ₹4,200 crore. Debt/EBITDA = 4,200 ÷ 7,000 = 0.6×. Outstanding: Shree can repay ALL its debt in 7 months of EBITDA. This exceptional ratio vs peers (UltraTech at 1.8×) directly explains Shree's commanding P/E premium in the cement sector.
EBITDA vs Free Cash Flow
Components
EBITDA overstates actual cash available for debt repayment in two key ways: (1) Ignores capex: a steel plant spending ₹2,000 crore annually on maintenance capex and expansion has ₹2,000 crore less real cash than EBITDA suggests. (2) Ignores working capital: if receivables grow ₹500 crore and inventory grows ₹300 crore in a year, ₹800 crore of EBITDA is absorbed by working capital rather than being available as cash. For capex-heavy companies (steel, cement, telecom, power), "Debt/EBITDA–Capex" or "Debt/FCF" (Free Cash Flow = EBITDA – Capex – Working Capital change) gives a truer picture of repayment capacity.
💡 EBITDA vs FCF divergence: Company EBITDA ₹5,000 crore (Debt/EBITDA looks safe at 2.5×). But: Annual maintenance capex ₹2,000 crore (maintaining aging plants), Working capital increase ₹500 crore (building inventory). Actual FCF = 5,000 − 2,000 − 500 = ₹2,500 crore. Real Debt/FCF = 12,500 ÷ 2,500 = 5× (concerning). Rating agencies adjust for this: "Adjusted Debt/EBITDA" from agency reports is always more conservative than the simple calculation, typically adding back maintenance capex and working capital requirements.
Debt/EBITDA Sector Benchmarks
Benchmarks
Debt/EBITDA varies significantly by sector based on cash flow predictability and asset intensity. India benchmarks: IT Services (TCS, Infosys): 0–0.1× (debt-free). Branded FMCG: 0–0.5×. Pharma: 0–1.5×. Auto OEMs: 0–1×. Cement: 1–3× (Shree 0.5–1×, UltraTech 1–2.5×). Capital goods/EPC: 1.5–3×. Steel (cyclical): 1.5–4×. Telecom: 3–6×. Infrastructure/Power: 4–8×. Real estate: 2–6× (varies by project mix and execution stage). Banks/NBFCs: not applicable (different framework). Always compare to sector median — not a universal 3× threshold.
💡 Debt/EBITDA cycle investing in steel: JSW Steel FY20 (COVID trough): Debt ₹48,000 crore, EBITDA ₹12,000 crore, Debt/EBITDA = 4×. Stock near lows. FY22 (commodity peak): Debt ₹37,000 crore (repaid debt), EBITDA ₹27,000 crore, Debt/EBITDA = 1.4×. Stock near highs. The Debt/EBITDA cycle (4× → 1.4×) mirrors the stock price cycle. Buying at 4× (improving trajectory + EBITDA recovering) and selling at 1.4× (peak, EBITDA about to normalise) is the fundamental steel investment timing framework.
Debt Covenant (Debt/EBITDA Trigger)
Signals
A debt covenant is a contractual obligation in loan or bond agreements that restricts borrower behaviour to protect lenders. Debt/EBITDA-linked covenants are among the most common: "Debt/EBITDA shall not exceed [threshold]×." If breached, lenders can: demand immediate full repayment of the loan (acceleration clause), increase the interest rate (step-up provision), restrict dividends and capex, or convert debt to equity. In India, project finance term loans (infrastructure, large manufacturing) typically include Debt/EBITDA covenants at 3.5–5×. NCD (Non-Convertible Debenture) prospectuses in India often disclose these covenants — searchable on NSE/BSE under "Offer Documents."
💡 Covenant breach example: A mid-cap manufacturer issues NCDs with covenant: Debt/EBITDA ≤ 3.5×. FY22 (demand slump + raw material spike): EBITDA fell 40%, Debt/EBITDA crossed 4×. NCD holders sent acceleration notice demanding early repayment of ₹500 crore. Company couldn't refinance in tight credit market → defaulted on NCD repayment → CRISIL downgraded to D (Default) → stock fell 70%. The Debt/EBITDA covenant was visible in the NCD prospectus on BSE. Tracking Debt/EBITDA vs stated covenant level is critical for any company with outstanding rated bonds.
Credit Rating vs Debt/EBITDA
Benchmarks
Rating agencies (CRISIL, ICRA, Moody's, S&P) use Debt/EBITDA as a primary quantitative input in credit rating analysis. Approximate correlations for Indian manufacturing companies: AAA: Debt/EBITDA < 1.5× + ICR > 8× + D/E < 0.5. AA+/AA: Debt/EBITDA 1.5–2.5× + ICR 5–8×. AA-/A+: Debt/EBITDA 2.5–3.5× + ICR 3–5×. A/BBB+: Debt/EBITDA 3.5–4.5× + ICR 2–4×. BB and below (sub-investment): Debt/EBITDA > 4.5× for manufacturing. D (Default): Debt/EBITDA becomes economically impossible (>10×) with negative FCF. These thresholds shift by sector — infrastructure companies can carry AAA with Debt/EBITDA 5× if they have PPAs.
💡 Rating watch: When a CRISIL AA-rated company's Debt/EBITDA rises from 2.5× to 3.8× due to a commodity shock, CRISIL places the rating on "Watch Negative." If it crosses 4.5× in the next year: downgrade to A+. Bond yields immediately rise (spread widens) as the downgrade risk is priced in — even before the official downgrade. Tracking Debt/EBITDA relative to implied rating thresholds gives 1–2 quarter advance warning of rating action, before the agency publishes it.
Debt/EBITDA Declining Trend (Deleveraging)
Signals
A consistently declining Debt/EBITDA ratio over 3+ years signals two positive dynamics: (1) Debt is being repaid (numerator falling) from strong free cash flow, and/or (2) EBITDA is growing (denominator rising) from business expansion or margin improvement. Both causes of declining Debt/EBITDA are positive quality signals. For cyclical companies, declining Debt/EBITDA from trough toward peak often coincides with the best stock return periods. For compounders, consistently low and declining Debt/EBITDA is a long-term quality signature. A declining Debt/EBITDA combined with rising ROCE = the highest-quality solvency trajectory for any Indian company.
💡 UltraTech deleveraging story: UltraTech acquired Century Cement (2019) and Binani Cement, causing Debt/EBITDA to spike to 3.8× (FY20). Over FY21–FY24: strong EBITDA growth + acquisitions absorbed + debt repayment → Debt/EBITDA fell to 1.5× (FY24). This 4-year deleveraging trajectory (3.8× → 1.5×) was the primary driver of UltraTech's stock re-rating and P/E expansion during this period. Investors who bought when Debt/EBITDA peaked at 3.8× (highest apparent leverage) caught the full re-rating as leverage normalized to 1.5×.
Debt/EBITDA vs D/E — Which is Better?
Definition
Both measure leverage but answer different questions. D/E (Debt-to-Equity): compares debt to the accounting book value of equity. Book value can be distorted by large asset write-downs, different depreciation policies, or thin equity bases from high dividend payout. Debt/EBITDA: compares debt to actual economic earnings capacity — independent of accounting book value. Debt/EBITDA is preferred by: Credit analysts (banks, rating agencies) — earnings capacity is what repays debt. Private equity (leveraged buyout analysis) — EBITDA multiples determine deal pricing. Lenders setting covenants. D/E is preferred by: Equity investors (DuPont ROE analysis), Companies with negligible earnings but significant asset values (real estate holding cos), Balance sheet analysts focused on net worth.
💡 When they diverge: Heavily depreciated manufacturer: D/E = 3× (small equity from large write-downs) but Debt/EBITDA = 1.5× (strong EBITDA despite depleted assets). D/E looks alarming; Debt/EBITDA shows the company can repay debt in 1.5 years. Credit analyst says: safe. Equity analyst focusing on D/E says: risky. The credit analyst is right for solvency assessment; the equity analyst raises a valid accounting concern. Using both together gives the complete picture: check if EBITDA-based repayment capacity aligns with the equity cushion in the balance sheet.
