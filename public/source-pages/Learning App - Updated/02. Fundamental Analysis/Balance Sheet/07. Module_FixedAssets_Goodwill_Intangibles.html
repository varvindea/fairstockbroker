Your Core Takeaway — Not All Assets Are Equal
A factory that makes real products is a real asset. Goodwill paid in a bad acquisition is an invisible liability waiting to be written off. The balance sheet treats both identically — but they are completely different in reality.

02 — Three Types of Long-Term Assets

Fixed Assets, Goodwill & Intangibles — Explained

The Non-Current Assets section of every balance sheet contains three very different types of assets. Understanding each one — how it is created, how it is measured, and what its presence signals about management quality — is essential for evaluating any company.

🏭
Type 1 — Tangible, Real
Fixed Assets (PPE)
Property, Plant and Equipment (PPE) are the physical assets a company uses to produce goods or services — factories, machines, land, buildings, vehicles, computers, and equipment. These are real, depreciable, productive assets that generate revenue. They appear at historical cost minus accumulated depreciation (Net Block on Screener.in). The Gross Block shows original cost; Net Block shows current book value after depreciation.
Net Block = Gross Block − Accumulated Depreciation
Capex Intensity = Capex ÷ Revenue. Asset Turnover = Revenue ÷ Net Block. Both are visible on Screener.in Ratios tab.
High asset turnover: >2× (efficient) Moderate: 0.5–2× Low: <0.5× (capital-intensive)
💡 Real analogy: Your home loan buys a house (fixed asset) that you live in and generate rental income from. Every year the house depreciates on the books (even if the actual market value rises). The Net Block of a factory = what the factory is worth on the books today. A factory bought for ₹100 Cr in 2005, depreciated ₹60 Cr by now, shows as ₹40 Cr Net Block — but is producing ₹500 Cr of revenue. Excellent asset turnover!
😱
Type 2 — Riskiest, Watch Carefully
Goodwill
Goodwill arises only when a company acquires another company at a price above its book value. The premium paid over book value is called goodwill and sits on the balance sheet indefinitely — unless an impairment test shows it is overvalued, at which point it gets written down, hitting profits directly. Goodwill is never depreciated (unlike fixed assets), which means it stays on the balance sheet at original cost until an impairment is forced. High goodwill = past acquisitions, not current operations.
Goodwill = Acquisition Price − Net Assets of Acquired Company
Example: Paid ₹5,000 Cr for a company whose books show ₹3,000 Cr of net assets. Goodwill = ₹2,000 Cr on your balance sheet. If the acquired company struggles, this ₹2,000 Cr gets impaired (written off).
Good: No goodwill (organic growth) Moderate: Goodwill <20% of equity Risk: Goodwill >50% of equity
⚠ Warning sign: Large goodwill + acquired company underperforming = future impairment write-off that will hit profits hard. This is what happened with Tata Steel’s Corus acquisition (2007), Hindalco’s Novelis, and dozens of telecom acquisitions worldwide. Always check: is goodwill growing? Is it larger than net worth? Has it been impaired before?
🆕
Type 3 — Can Be Real Moats
Intangible Assets
Intangible assets are non-physical assets with economic value — patents, trademarks, brand names, software, licences, customer relationships, and non-compete agreements. Unlike goodwill, intangibles can be amortised (gradually expensed over their useful life). Internally generated intangibles like a brand or internally developed software are generally NOT capitalised under Indian GAAP — only acquired or externally developed intangibles appear on the balance sheet. This creates an important asymmetry: companies like HUL with enormous brand value show zero brand asset on the balance sheet!
Amortisation = Intangible Cost ÷ Useful Life (years)
Patents, licences, and customer contracts are amortised. Spectrum licences (telecom companies) are the largest intangible in Indian markets. Software companies capitalise R&D costs differently.
Productive: Patents generating revenue Monitor: Large capitalised software Red flag: Overstated customer relationships
💡 Important nuance: A telecom company with ₹50,000 Cr of spectrum licences as intangibles is perfectly normal — those licences generate real revenue. A company that capitalises ₹500 Cr of “customer acquisition costs” as intangibles to avoid hitting P&L is engaged in earnings management. Context and sector matter enormously when analysing intangibles.
Key Ratios to Evaluate Capex Intensity & Asset Quality
Asset Turnover = Revenue ÷ Net Fixed Assets  •  Capex Intensity = Capex ÷ Revenue
These two ratios tell you how hard the company’s assets are working and how much new capital is being invested to grow. Both are visible on Screener.in’s Ratios tab. A company with high asset turnover and low capex intensity is capital-efficient — the ideal combination.

🏭 How to Evaluate Fixed Assets on Screener.in

Step 1: Open Balance Sheet → find Net Block row → read 10-year trend. Is it rising or flat? Rising Net Block = company is investing in growth. Step 2: Ratios tab → Asset Turnover Ratio → click chart. Rising asset turnover = company using assets more efficiently. Falling = new assets not yet generating revenue (okay during buildout) or old assets underperforming (bad). Step 3: Cash Flow tab → Capital Expenditure row → compare Capex vs Depreciation. If Capex > Depreciation every year = company is expanding. If Capex < Depreciation for 5+ years = assets ageing and no replacement (concerning).

⚠ The Goodwill Trap — How to Spot it Instantly

Step 1: Balance Sheet → find Goodwill row. Is it zero? Great (organic growth). Is it large? Step 2: Calculate Goodwill as % of total equity (Reserves + Share Capital). If Goodwill > 30% of equity, the company has made major acquisitions and may be carrying inflated assets. Step 3: Check the P&L for past impairment charges (look under “Exceptional Items”). Any impairment = the company previously overpaid for an acquisition. Step 4: Find the acquired company in the Annual Report ’s subsidiary list and check if it is profitable or loss-making. If loss-making, goodwill impairment may be coming.

💡 Why Great Brands Are Invisible on the Balance Sheet

One of the most important accounting facts: Internally created brands, customer loyalty, and internally developed technology are NOT shown as intangible assets under Indian accounting standards. This means Hindustan Unilever’s Surf Excel, Lux, and Dove brands — worth tens of thousands of crores — show as ₹0 on the balance sheet. This is why P/BV ratios of 50–80× make sense for FMCG companies: the real value is in invisible brand assets not on the books. When a company acquires a brand (like Tata buying Tetley), the brand then appears as an intangible asset at the acquisition cost.

⏱ The Capex vs Depreciation Trick

The single fastest asset quality check: Open the Cash Flow Statement on Screener.in → find “Capital Expenditure” (Cash Flow from Investing, negative number) and compare with Depreciation (P&L). Capex > Depreciation: Company is investing more than it replaces — growing asset base, building future capacity. Capex ≈ Depreciation: Company is maintaining existing assets, not growing. Capex < Depreciation for 3+ years: Company is shrinking its asset base, possibly milking assets without reinvestment — concerning if also growing revenue (unsustainable).

05 — Tricks to Find Asset Quality Instantly

5 Screener.in Shortcuts Every Investor Must Know

These are field-tested tricks to evaluate Fixed Assets, Goodwill, and Intangibles in under 5 minutes — no annual report reading required. All data is free on Screener.in.

1
Screener.in — Ratios Tab
The “Asset Turnover Trend” Trick
Asset Turnover (Revenue ÷ Net Fixed Assets) is the most important single ratio for evaluating fixed asset quality. Go to the Ratios tab and find “Asset Turnover Ratio” → click the chart icon to see the 10-year trend. Rising asset turnover = each rupee of assets earns more revenue every year (excellent). Falling asset turnover = new assets underperforming or existing assets under-utilised (warning). Compare with 2–3 sector peers to benchmark efficiently.
→ Screener.in → any company → Ratios tab
→ Find “Asset Turnover Ratio” → click the chart 📈 icon
→ Is the trend rising or falling over 5 years? Compare with 2 peers.
✔ Rising turnover = Assets earning more ⚠ Flat = Maintaining, not improving ✗ Falling = Capital being deployed poorly
2
Screener.in — Balance Sheet Tab
The “Goodwill as % of Equity” Trick
Open the Balance Sheet tab and find two numbers: Goodwill (Non-Current Assets section) and Total Equity (Share Capital + Reserves). Calculate Goodwill ÷ Total Equity. If this percentage is under 10% — goodwill is manageable. If it exceeds 30% — a meaningful portion of equity exists only because of past acquisition premiums, not real business assets. Also check if Goodwill has changed year-on-year: a sudden increase = new acquisition made; a decrease = impairment taken (write-off).
→ Balance Sheet → Non-Current Assets → find Goodwill row
→ Balance Sheet → find Total Equity (Reserves + Share Capital)
→ Calculate: Goodwill ÷ Equity × 100. Is it <10%? >30%?
✔ Goodwill = 0% of equity = organic growth ⚠ Goodwill 10–30% of equity = monitor ✗ Goodwill >30% of equity = acquisition risk, check subsidiaries
3
Screener.in — Cash Flow Tab
The “Capex vs Depreciation” Trick
Open the Cash Flow Statement → find Capital Expenditure (negative number in Cash Flow from Investing) and compare with Depreciation (add-back in Cash Flow from Operations). This tells you whether the company is growing its asset base or just maintaining it. Capex > Depreciation = investing in growth. Capex ≈ Depreciation = maintenance mode. Capex < Depreciation for 3+ years = assets ageing without replacement (the “melting ice cube” problem). Trend matters more than any single year.
→ Cash Flow tab → find “Capital Expenditure” (investing section)
→ Cash Flow tab → find “Depreciation” (operating section add-back)
→ Compare for 3–5 years. Which is consistently higher?
✔ Capex > Depreciation = Investing in growth ⚠ Capex ≈ Depreciation = Maintenance only ✗ Capex < Depreciation 3+ years = Assets shrinking
4
Screener.in — Balance Sheet CWIP Row
The “CWIP Trend” Trick
Capital Work in Progress (CWIP) is money spent on assets under construction — not yet operational, not yet earning revenue. Normal CWIP is fine (all factories take time to build). The trick: watch CWIP as % of Net Block across 3–5 years on Screener.in. If CWIP keeps rising year after year without converting to Net Block (commissioned assets), the project is delayed. Delayed projects mean interest is being capitalised (hidden in CWIP), cash is trapped, and revenue projections are not materialising. This is one of the earliest visible signals of project execution risk.
→ Balance Sheet → find “Capital Work in Progress” (CWIP) row
→ Read the 5-year trend: rising or flat or converting to Net Block?
→ Calculate CWIP ÷ Net Block × 100. Rising % = project delays
✔ CWIP falling = projects commissioned on time ⚠ CWIP stable = large ongoing project, expected ✗ CWIP rising 3+ years = delays, capital trapped, investigate

Screener.in — The Invisible Asset Trick
The “P/BV vs ROE” Trick — Find Hidden Intangible Assets
Great brands, customer loyalty, and internally developed technology never appear on the balance sheet — but they drive enormous profits. The trick to identify companies with massive off-balance-sheet intangibles is to compare P/BV (Price-to-Book Value) with ROE (Return on Equity). A company trading at 50× Book Value with 80% ROE has enormous invisible assets — the market is valuing what the balance sheet does not show. Use this to find quality businesses where the accounting understates real value.
→ Ratios tab → find “Price to Book Value (P/BV)” → note current value
→ Ratios tab → find “Return on Equity (ROE)” → note 5-year average
→ If P/BV >10× and ROE >25% — company has invisible off-balance-sheet assets
→ Examples: HUL (P/BV ~60×, ROE 200%+), Nestle (P/BV ~80×, ROE 100%+)
→ High P/BV + Low ROE = Overvalued, book value inflated (possible goodwill)
→ Low P/BV + Low ROE = Value trap, poor business quality (avoid)
→ High P/BV + High ROE = Invisible moat (brand, patents, relationships)
→ Low P/BV + High ROE = Hidden gem — market undervaluing the business
✔ High P/BV + High ROE = Invisible moat worth paying for ⚠ High P/BV + Moderate ROE = Premium pricing, justify carefully ✗ High P/BV + Low/falling ROE = Goodwill inflating book value, overpriced
