Chapter 01 — Ownership

Equity & Shareholders — Who Owns the Business?

🏠
Term 01
Equity
Your ownership stake in a business
🎂 Chaitali put ₹1 lakh of her own money to start the bakery. That ₹1 lakh IS her equity. She owns 100% of the business.
Equity simply means ownership. When you invest your own money into a business, that money becomes your equity — your ownership stake. If the business becomes worth ₹10 lakh tomorrow, your ₹1 lakh equity has grown to ₹10 lakh. If the business loses money and is worth only ₹50,000, your equity has shrunk. Equity holders are the last ones paid — after suppliers, employees, and lenders — but they get to keep all the upside when things go well. This is why equity is called risk capital: high risk, high reward.
🏠
Daily Life Analogy
Think of equity like owning a flat. If you bought a flat for ₹40 lakh and it's now worth ₹60 lakh, your equity in that flat grew. Same principle in business — the equity grows as the business becomes more valuable.
👥
Term 02
Shareholders & Share Capital
Partners who own pieces of the business
🎂 Chaitali's bakery is booming. She needs ₹3 lakh more to open a second branch. Her friend Anjali offers to invest ₹1 lakh for a 25% stake. Chaitali now has a shareholder.
When a business needs more money to grow, it can divide ownership into shares and sell some to investors. These investors become shareholders — they own a piece of the company. Share Capital is the total money raised by selling shares. If Chaitali divides the bakery into 100 shares at ₹1,000 each (total ₹1 lakh) and Anjali buys 25 shares for ₹25,000 — Anjali owns 25% and becomes a shareholder. Every share carries equal rights: equal dividend, equal vote, equal growth.
🍕
Pizza Slice Analogy
Think of the company like a pizza. Share capital is the whole pizza. Each share is one slice. The more slices you own, the bigger your ownership. If the pizza grows bigger (company grows), your slices become more valuable too.
Chapter 02 — The Money Flow

Sales, Costs & Profit — How Money Moves

Every rupee that enters and leaves a business has a name. Understanding the difference between revenue, operating profit, and net profit is the foundation of reading any financial statement — and understanding any business.

💰
Term 03
Sales / Revenue
Total money collected from customers
🎂 Chaitali sold 500 cakes in a month at ₹200 each = ₹1,00,000 revenue. This is her total Sales / Revenue for the month.
Revenue (also called Sales or Turnover) is the total money a business earns from selling its products or services — before subtracting any costs. It is always the first line of a Profit & Loss statement. Revenue is not profit — it is the starting point. A business can have ₹10 crore revenue and still make a loss if its costs are ₹12 crore. Revenue growth is the most visible metric investors track, but profitability is what matters.
🍿
Raju's Snacks Shop
Raju sells 200 plates of samosa at ₹20 each = ₹4,000 daily revenue. Revenue is just the cash coming in — not the money he keeps.
📊
Term 04
Operating Profit (EBITDA)
Profit from core business, before interest & tax
🎂 Chaitali's Revenue: ₹1,00,000 minus Raw Material ₹35,000, Electricity ₹5,000, Staff ₹20,000, Rent ₹10,000 = Operating Profit ₹30,000 (30% margin).
Operating Profit is what the business earns from its core operations — after paying for raw materials, electricity, staff, and rent — but before paying interest on loans and income tax. It shows how efficient the business is at what it does. EBITDA stands for Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation, and Amortisation — a common way analysts measure operational efficiency. A 30% EBITDA margin means for every ₹100 of revenue, ₹30 is left after running costs.
🎯
Term 05
Net Profit
The final bottom line — what the owner actually keeps
🎂 From ₹30,000 Operating Profit: subtract Interest on loan ₹5,000 + Depreciation ₹2,000 + Tax ₹5,000 = Net Profit ₹18,000. This is what Chaitali actually earned.
Net Profit (also called PAT — Profit After Tax) is the final "bottom line." It is what remains after paying everything: raw materials, salaries, rent, electricity, interest on loans, depreciation on equipment, and income tax. This is the actual profit the owner/shareholders can either reinvest in the business or distribute as dividends. Net Profit margin = Net Profit ÷ Revenue. A higher and rising net margin is the surest sign a business is compounding well.

Chapter 03 — More Business Terms

Debt, Inputs & Sharing Profits

🏦
Term 06
Debt / Loan
Borrowed money that must be repaid with interest
🎂 Chaitali took a ₹1 lakh bank loan at 12% per year to buy a second oven. She repays ₹1,000/month interest + principal EMI.
Debt is money borrowed from banks or lenders to fund the business. Unlike equity (your own money), debt must be repaid with interest regardless of whether the business is doing well or not. Interest is the cost of borrowing — if you borrow ₹1 lakh at 12% per year, you pay ₹12,000 in interest annually. High debt = high interest burden = less profit available for shareholders. The Debt-to-Equity ratio (Total Debt ÷ Total Equity) tells you how much a business is dependent on borrowed money. A ratio above 2× is considered high.
🏨
Suresh's Hotel
Suresh took a ₹50 lakh loan to build a hotel. He pays ₹5 lakh/year in interest. This interest eats into profit every single year until the loan is repaid.
🌾
Term 07
Raw Material Consumed
Ingredients and inputs used to make products
🎂 Chaitali uses flour, butter, sugar, eggs, and flavouring to bake cakes. These are her raw materials. Every cake she sells "consumes" some of each ingredient.
Raw Material Consumed means the cost of inputs used to make the product in a given period. It is the largest cost for most manufacturing businesses. For a bakery, it is flour and butter. For a car company, it is steel. For a paint company, it is crude oil derivatives. When raw material prices rise (e.g., wheat price jumps 30%), the bakery either absorbs the extra cost (margins fall) or raises prices (customers may buy less). Tracking raw material cost as a percentage of revenue tells you how well a business manages its input costs.
🍿
Raju's Snacks
Raju's biggest cost: potatoes, oil, maida for samosas. When potato prices double in summer, his profits shrink unless he raises samosa prices.
⚡
Term 08
Electricity Used
Power cost — significant for manufacturing businesses
🎂 Chaitali's oven runs 8 hours a day. Her monthly electricity bill is ₹5,000 — 5% of her revenue. This is "Electricity / Power Cost."
Electricity is a key operating cost for any business that runs equipment, machinery, refrigeration, lighting, or air conditioning. For a small bakery, it may be 4–6% of revenue. For industries like aluminium smelting or cement manufacturing, electricity can be 25–35% of total production cost — making power tariffs one of the most important profitability drivers. Businesses with captive solar power or cheap coal-based power plants have a structural cost advantage over competitors buying power from the grid. In Annual Reports, look under "Manufacturing Expenses" for power and fuel costs.
🎁
Term 09
Dividend — Sharing Profits
Company pays a portion of profits back to shareholders
🎂 At year end, Chaitali's bakery made ₹2,16,000 net profit. She decides to keep ₹1,50,000 to reinvest in a new oven and pays ₹66,000 as dividend — ₹660 per share to her and Anjali.
A dividend is a share of a company's profits paid out to its shareholders, usually quarterly or annually. It is a reward for being a shareholder — you get cash just for owning shares. Not all companies pay dividends — fast-growing companies prefer to reinvest profits back into the business for faster growth. Mature companies with steady profits (like ITC, Coal India) pay high dividends because they don't need as much reinvestment. Dividend Yield = Annual Dividend per Share ÷ Share Price × 100%. A 3–5% dividend yield is considered healthy for income investors.
🏨
Suresh's Hotel
Suresh's hotel earned ₹10 lakh profit. He paid ₹3 lakh as dividend to his 3 investor friends (₹1 lakh each) and kept ₹7 lakh to renovate rooms. That ₹1 lakh each friend received is a dividend.

Chapter 05 — Growing Profits

What Should a Company Do to Increase Profit?

Profit is not just about selling more. It's about selling smartly, managing costs tightly, and building advantages that are hard for competitors to copy. Here are 9 proven profit-growth strategies, explained through our three businesses.

📈
1. Raise Volume — Sell More
The simplest way: sell more units to more customers. Open more locations. Expand delivery radius. Run promotions during festivals. Chaitali opened 4 more bakery outlets — revenue grew 5× while fixed costs (management, systems) only grew 2×. Each new outlet is more profitable than the first because overhead is already absorbed.
📊 Revenue Growth
💎
2. Raise Prices — Improve Pricing Power
If customers value your product highly, they'll pay more. Chaitali launched a "Premium Artisan Cake" range at ₹500 per cake (vs ₹200 earlier). Same ingredients, better presentation — and 3× the margin. Pricing power comes from brand, quality, and uniqueness. A business that can raise prices without losing customers is a great business.
💰 Margin Expansion
🔧
3. Cut Raw Material Cost
Buy in bulk. Negotiate better rates. Switch to alternate suppliers. Chaitali joined a bulk-buying cooperative with 10 other bakeries — getting flour and butter at 15% lower rates. Same cakes, lower input cost = higher margin without charging more. Suresh's hotel negotiated 3-year contracts with vegetable suppliers at fixed rates.
⚙️ Cost Reduction
⚡
4. Reduce Electricity / Energy Cost
Install solar panels. Use energy-efficient equipment. Run heavy machines at night (cheaper power tariff). Chaitali installed a ₹2 lakh solar rooftop system — electricity bill dropped from ₹5,000 to ₹1,500/month. In 3 years the solar system paid for itself — permanent cost reduction. Suresh's hotel installed LED lighting and inverter ACs — saving ₹8 lakh/year.
🌱 Energy Efficiency
🔄
5. Reduce Waste — Improve Yield
Every unsold cake is wasted raw material. Chaitali started tracking daily demand patterns and pre-baking only what was likely to sell. Waste dropped from 15% of production to 5%. Same revenue, 10% less raw material consumed = directly higher profit. Raju's snacks shop started making fresh batches every 2 hours instead of morning-only, reducing wastage 20%.
♻️ Operational Efficiency
🏪
6. Add New Revenue Lines
Use the same assets to earn more. Chaitali added: (a) cake delivery orders online (no extra rent), (b) baking classes on weekends (using the same kitchen), (c) corporate gifting contracts. Three new revenue streams with almost zero additional fixed cost. Each new revenue line has nearly 100% contribution margin above direct costs.
🎯 Diversification
💻
7. Technology & Automation
Replace manual tasks with machines or software. Chaitali bought an industrial cake mixer (₹80,000) — one baker now does the work of three. POS billing software eliminated errors and tracking time. Suresh's hotel used a booking management system — front desk staff reduced from 5 to 2. Technology investment pays back through permanently lower staff costs.
🤖 Automation
🏷️
8. Build a Brand
A strong brand commands premium pricing permanently. Chaitali invested in packaging, social media, and a loyalty program. "Chaitali's" became the go-to birthday cake in town — customers pay ₹100 more for a Chaitali's cake vs a generic bakery. Brand = sustainable pricing power = higher margins for life. Raju's snacks became famous for a secret chutney recipe — the brand moat.
⭐ Brand Value
🤝
9. Repay Debt — Reduce Interest Burden
Every rupee of debt repaid saves permanent interest cost. Chaitali's ₹5 lakh loan at 12% costs ₹60,000/year in interest — money that could have been profit. She used 30% of each year's profit to repay loans faster. Once debt-free, that ₹60,000/year flows straight to net profit. Suresh's hotel was 70% debt-funded — after 8 years of repayment, interest went from ₹35 lakh to ₹8 lakh/year = ₹27 lakh extra profit annually.
💳 Deleveraging
Chapter 06 — Smart Growth

What to Consider While Growing — 8 Key Factors

Growth is exciting but dangerous when done without caution. Many businesses that grew fast also failed fast — because they didn't consider these critical factors before expanding. Chaitali almost made the mistake of expanding too quickly in Year 3 — here's what saved her.

💸
1. Cash Flow — Not Just Profit
A business can show profit on paper but run out of cash to pay salaries. This happens when customers delay payments or when you grow faster than cash comes in. Always check: do you have enough cash to fund the next 3–6 months even if revenue is delayed?
🎂 Chaitali took a ₹5 lakh corporate order but wasn't paid for 60 days. Meanwhile, she had to buy ₹3 lakh of ingredients. She nearly couldn't pay staff — profit on paper, crisis in cash.
🏦
2. Debt Level — Don't Over-Borrow
Borrowing to grow is fine — but borrowing too much creates a debt trap. If revenue falls even 20%, you may not be able to pay EMIs. Keep Debt-to-Equity below 1.5×. Growth funded by profits is always safer than growth funded by debt.
🏨 Suresh borrowed ₹2 crore to open 5 new hotels. COVID hit — revenue dropped 80%. He couldn't pay EMIs. Two hotels had to be closed and sold. Over-expansion killed profits for 5 years.
👩‍💼
3. Management & Team Quality
Chaitali can manage 5 outlets herself but not 50. Scaling requires hiring competent managers, building systems, and trusting people. Poor hires at senior levels are the #1 reason growing businesses lose quality. Hire slowly, fire quickly.
🍿 Raju hired an experienced operations manager to handle 10 new snack shops. The manager introduced standard recipes, hygiene checks, and supplier management — quality became consistent across all outlets.
🏪
4. Location & Market Size
Expanding into a city with no demand for your product is wasted capital. Research the market — population, income levels, competition density, and growth trends — before committing to a new location. Not every bakery that works in Mumbai will work in a Tier 4 town.
🎂 Chaitali's ₹600 premium cakes worked brilliantly in South Mumbai but flopped in a small town. She pivoted to a budget range (₹150) for smaller cities — matching product to market demand.
⚙️
5. Operational Systems & Processes
What works in one outlet by personal supervision breaks down at 20 outlets. You need written recipes, standard operating procedures, training programs, and technology systems before scaling. Process creates consistency; consistency creates brand trust.
🏨 Suresh created a 50-page "Hotel Operating Manual" before opening his 3rd hotel — check-in procedures, housekeeping standards, complaint resolution protocols. The 5th hotel opened smoothly because systems were already in place.
👀
6. Competition & Market Share
Who else is in your market? How easily can they copy you? Growing into a city where a better-funded competitor already dominates can be devastating. Build strong local brand loyalty, unique products, or cost advantages before entering competitive territory.
🍿 Raju expanded to a food court dominated by a large chain. He couldn't compete on price. He survived by specialising in regional snacks the chain didn't offer — the uniqueness moat protected him.
📋
7. Regulatory & Legal Compliance
FSSAI (food safety), GST registration, fire safety, health certificates, labour law compliance — growing businesses face increasing regulatory complexity. A single FSSAI notice can shut multiple outlets. Build a compliance team before you have 10 outlets, not after.
🎂 Chaitali hired a part-time compliance consultant at ₹15,000/month when she had 8 outlets. She avoided 3 FSSAI notices that would have cost ₹5–10 lakh each in fines and disruption.
🌱
8. Sustainability & Long-Term Thinking
Short-term profit maximisation (cutting quality, over-promising, exploiting suppliers) destroys reputation and creates long-term losses. Build supplier relationships, invest in staff development, maintain product quality even when growing. Sustainable businesses grow for decades; short-sighted ones peak and crash.
🏨 Suresh maintained premium service standards even when temporarily loss-making. His hotel got "Best Boutique Hotel" award — which attracted 40% more bookings organically. Quality investment pays compound returns.

💡 The Big Lesson from Arjun's Story
Every great business begins with a tiny amount of equity and a lot of courage. Arjun's journey teaches us: Revenue is the cups of chai sold. Raw material cost is the milk and tea leaves. Subtracted from Fixed Assets to show Net Book Value

🚗A car worth ₹5 lakh depreciates ₹1 lakh/year. After 2 years, its book value = ₹3 lakh. The ₹2 lakh "written off" is accumulated depreciation.

Also called: Current Asset
₹1.2 L
Raw materials and finished goods currently held. For Arjun's Chai: tea leaves, milk powder, sugar, disposable cups in stock. Inventory is a current asset — it will be converted to revenue within the next 12 months. Inventory Days = (Inventory ÷ COGS) × 365 — lower is more efficient.

Money owed TO the company by customers
₹0.8 L

Most liquid current asset
₹2.4 L

🏦Arjun has ₹2.4L in his current account. This is "free cash" — he can use it to pay suppliers, buy equipment, or repay a loan immediately.

🐷Think of reserves like a piggy bank. Every year, Arjun keeps some profit inside the business instead of withdrawing it. That savings grows year after year — building the company's net worth.

💡 The Golden Rule of Balance Sheets
A Balance Sheet always balances — Total Assets MUST equal Total Liabilities + Equity. This is not coincidence; it's the fundamental accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Equity. If Arjun's total assets are ₹10.6 lakh, then the sum of what he owes to others (liabilities) plus what the business owes to owners (equity) must also be ₹10.6 lakh. Every rupee the company has came from somewhere — either borrowed (liability) or from owners (equity). A growing net worth (equity) year-on-year is the clearest sign of a healthy, value-creating business.

💡 From Revenue to Net Profit — The Waterfall
Revenue is just the starting line. The P&L is a waterfall — costs are subtracted one level at a time to arrive at the bottom line (Net Profit / PAT). The key milestones: Gross Profit (after material cost), EBITDA (after all operating expenses — the truest operational metric), EBIT (after depreciation), PBT (after interest), PAT (after tax — the final "bottom line"). Statement 03 · Cash Flow Statement
Cash Flow Statement
The Reality Check — Arjun made ₹4.38L profit but how much actual cash did the business generate? · Three sections: Operating · Investing · Financing
⚠️ Why Profit ≠ Cash — The Most Important Concept in Finance
Arjun's P&L shows ₹4.38L net profit. But his bank balance grew by only ₹1.2L. Where did the rest go? This is the "profit ≠ cash" paradox — and understanding it separates good investors from great ones. Three things can make cash lower than profit: (1) Receivables: sold chai on credit — revenue recorded but cash not yet received. (2) Inventory: bought extra stock — cash went out but not shown in P&L as expense yet. (3) Capital expenditure: bought new equipment — cash out but only depreciation (small fraction) hits P&L. The Cash Flow Statement tracks these real cash movements.
💧 Three Sections of Cash Flow
🔄
Operating Cash Flow (OCF)
Cash generated from day-to-day business
+₹5.8 L
Cash generated (or used) by the core business operations. Starts with Net Profit and adjusts for: (+) non-cash items like depreciation, (−) increase in working capital (debtors, inventory), (+) increase in payables. OCF is the most important cash flow line. A business with consistently positive OCF is self-funding — it doesn't need external money to survive. If OCF is negative despite positive P&L profit, the business has a working capital problem.
☕Arjun's OCF of ₹5.8L means his chai business generates ₹5.8L real cash from operations — after paying all costs and accounting for credit sales and stock changes.
🏭
Investing Cash Flow (ICF)
Cash spent on or received from assets
−₹3.2 L
Cash used to buy or received from selling long-term assets — equipment, property, investments. Capital Expenditure (CapEx) = cash spent on new fixed assets = negative ICF. If a company sells a building or investment, that's positive ICF. Free Cash Flow (FCF) = OCF − CapEx — the cash left after maintaining and growing the asset base. FCF is what ultimately creates long-term shareholder value.
🛠️Arjun bought 2 new commercial gas setups (₹2.8L) and replaced 3 carts (₹0.4L) = ₹3.2L cash out. This is Capital Expenditure — it improves future capacity but hits cash flow now.
💳
Financing Cash Flow (FCF)
Cash from borrowing or returning money
−₹1.4 L
Cash flows related to raising capital or returning money to providers. Includes: loan repayments (cash out), new loans taken (cash in), dividend paid (cash out), new share capital raised (cash in). Arjun repaid ₹0.9L of his bank loan principal and paid ₹0.5L as dividend to Meena and friends = ₹1.4L outflow.
🏦Arjun pays ₹9,000/month loan principal (EMI minus interest) = ₹1.08L/year principal repayment + ₹50,000 dividend = ₹1.38L ≈ ₹1.4L financing outflow.
🌊
Working Capital Changes
The invisible cash consumer
−₹0.6 L
Working capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities = money "locked" in the daily operations cycle. When debtors increase (customers delay payment), or inventory increases (you stockpile more), cash is consumed. When creditors increase (you delay paying suppliers), cash is released. Working Capital Days = Debtor Days + Inventory Days − Creditor Days. Lower number = more efficient = less cash tied up. Businesses like DMart (negative working capital) actually generate cash from operations before paying suppliers.
⏰Arjun's corporate clients owe him ₹80K. He's bought ₹1.2L inventory. He owes suppliers ₹80K. Net working capital = ₹80K + ₹1.2L − ₹80K = ₹1.2L of cash locked in the cycle.
💎
Free Cash Flow (FCF)
OCF minus CapEx — The King of Metrics
+₹2.6 L
Free Cash Flow = Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditure. This is the single most important long-term metric for assessing business quality. FCF is the actual cash available to repay debt, pay dividends, buy back shares, or invest in growth — after keeping the existing business running. Companies with high FCF/Net Profit ratios are the most reliable long-term wealth creators. Warren Buffett famously values businesses primarily on their FCF-generating capacity.
💵Arjun's OCF: ₹5.8L minus CapEx ₹3.2L = FCF ₹2.6L. This is the "free" money — completely unrestricted cash he could theoretically hand to shareholders.

💡 The Three Questions a Cash Flow Answers
1. Is the business self-sustaining? OCF of ₹5.8L means YES — Arjun generates more cash than he needs to run the business. 2. Is the company investing in future growth? CapEx of ₹3.2L means YES — he's actively expanding capacity. 3. Is debt under control? Financing outflow includes loan repayment — he's steadily reducing debt. The ideal company: OCF > Net Profit (quality earnings), FCF > Zero (self-funding), Financing activities show debt repayment (financially responsible).
