Analyse WC Efficiency & Cash Conversion Cycle

By the end of this module you will open any company on Screener.in, read the working capital section, calculate the Cash Conversion Cycle, and know instantly whether the company manages cash brilliantly — or is stuck in a perpetual cash crunch.

⚙️
Your Core Takeaway — Cash Is King, Speed Is Everything
Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities. But the real insight is HOW FAST the company moves: from buying raw material → making goods → selling → collecting cash.
Imagine a samosa shop. The owner buys potatoes on Monday (Inventory). Makes samosas. Sells to a canteen on credit — they will pay in 15 days (Debtor). But the potato supplier gives 30 days to pay (Creditor). So the shop owner has 30 days of credit in, collects cash in 15 days, and keeps 15 days of cushion as free float. That is negative working capital — the best possible position. CDSL and Nestle India work exactly this way. On the other hand, a manufacturer who holds 90 days of inventory, waits 60 days to collect from customers, but only gets 30 days from suppliers — is always short of cash even if profitable. This is the working capital trap most retail investors miss completely.

02 — Three Working Capital Components

Inventory, Debtors & Creditors — Explained Simply

Every company’s daily financial health is determined by three numbers: how much stock it holds (Inventory), how much customers owe it (Debtors/Receivables), and how much it owes suppliers (Creditors/Payables). Together they determine whether cash flows freely or gets trapped.

📦
Component 1 — Current Asset
Inventory (Stock)
Inventory is the value of all goods a company holds at any point — raw materials waiting to be processed, work-in-progress on the factory floor, and finished goods ready to ship. Every rupee of inventory is cash that has left the bank but not yet returned as revenue. Holding too much inventory ties up capital; holding too little risks stockouts and lost sales.
Inventory Days = (Inventory ÷ COGS) × 365
How many days worth of sales are sitting in the warehouse. Lower = faster inventory turns = less cash trapped. COGS = Cost of Goods Sold (P&L statement).
Excellent: <30 days (FMCG) Moderate: 30–90 days Concern: >120 days
💡 Real analogy: You run a clothing store. Every shirt in your store is paid money that isn’t earning anything until it sells. Holding 6 months of unsold shirts means your cash is locked in fabric while your rent keeps running. The fastest fashion retailers (Zara) turn inventory in 15 days. Slow ones take 180+ days and constantly borrow to pay salaries.
📋
Component 2 — Current Asset
Debtors (Receivables)
Debtors are customers who bought goods or services but have not paid yet — they owe the company money. This appears as “Trade Receivables” on the balance sheet. Every rupee of debtors is a sale that has happened but cash has not arrived. High debtors relative to revenue means the company is financing its customers — sometimes by force, especially when dealing with large buyers like government entities.
Debtor Days = (Trade Receivables ÷ Revenue) × 365
How many days of sales are outstanding as unpaid invoices. Lower debtor days = faster collection = more cash available. Also called Days Sales Outstanding (DSO).
Excellent: <30 days Moderate: 30–60 days Concern: >90 days
💡 Real analogy: You sell 100 samosas to a hotel for ₹500 but they pay in 60 days. You have “earned” ₹500 on paper, but your bank account is empty for 60 days. Meanwhile you need flour, oil, and gas right now. Companies with 120-day debtor days are effectively providing free 4-month loans to every customer. This becomes critical in sectors like infrastructure and defence where government payments can take 6–12 months.
⛳
Component 3 — Current Liability
Creditors (Payables)
Creditors are suppliers who have delivered goods or services but have not been paid yet — the company owes them money. This appears as “Trade Payables” on the balance sheet. Unlike inventory and debtors (which use cash), creditors are effectively free financing from suppliers. The longer you can delay paying suppliers (without damaging relationships), the more cash you retain for operations. This is why strong brands like Nestle have negative working capital.
Creditor Days = (Trade Payables ÷ COGS) × 365
How many days the company takes to pay its suppliers. Higher creditor days = more free financing from suppliers. But too high can damage supplier relationships and signal financial stress.
Healthy: 30–60 days Monitor: 60–90 days Red flag: >120 days (stress or dominance?)
💡 Real analogy: The potato supplier gives you 30 days to pay. For those 30 days, you are using the supplier’s capital for free. Nestle India gets 60–90 days from suppliers because of its brand power — suppliers can’t afford to lose Nestle as a customer. Walmart, Amazon, and large FMCG companies use this supplier float to fund entire operations. Creditors > Inventory + Debtors = negative WC = money machine.
The Master Formula — Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC)
CCC = Inventory Days + Debtor Days − Creditor Days
The CCC tells you how many days cash is tied up in operations. Negative CCC = suppliers fund your business for free. Zero or very low = excellent. High positive CCC = cash always short, may need constant borrowing.

✔ Why CDSL Has Zero Inventory Forever

CDSL is a pure services business — it acts as India’s monopoly depository, electronically holding and transferring securities. There are no physical goods, no raw materials, no finished product sitting in a warehouse. The “product” is a digital record. 📊 Why CDSL Has Negative Working Capital

CDSL collects settlement fees, annual maintenance charges, and transaction fees from brokers and issuers — often in advance or very quickly (18–20 day debtor days). Meanwhile, CDSL pays vendors (technology, infrastructure, staff costs) on longer credit terms (30–45 days). The result: Trade Payables (₹475 Cr) > Trade Receivables (₹360 Cr) — meaning CDSL uses its suppliers’ money to fund operations. Net Working Capital is structurally negative. This is the most capital-efficient operating model possible: the more business you do, the more free financing you receive.

This two-second check on Screener.in’s Balance Sheet covers 80% of working capital analysis for non-financial companies. Payables exceeding receivables = suppliers fund operations (negative WC). Falling inventory days = company is turning stock faster. Both together = a capital-efficient business that compounds returns automatically. Find it in 90 seconds: Balance Sheet → compare Trade Receivables vs Trade Payables rows → then Ratios tab → Inventory Days chart. If both checks pass, focus your remaining analysis on other aspects of the business.
