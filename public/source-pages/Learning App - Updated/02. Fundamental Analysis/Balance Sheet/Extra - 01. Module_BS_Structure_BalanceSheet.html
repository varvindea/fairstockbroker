01 — Key Learning Outcome

The Balance Sheet is a Financial Photograph

📊
Your Core Takeaway
A Balance Sheet is a company's financial statement showing what it owns (Assets), what it owes (Liabilities), and what belongs to shareholders (Equity) — at a specific point in time. Assets always equal Liabilities plus Equity.
Imagine you just bought a house. You paid ₹50 lakh. You had ₹20 lakh savings (Equity) and took a ₹30 lakh home loan (Liability). The house is worth ₹50 lakh (Asset). Notice: ₹50 lakh Asset = ₹30 lakh Liability + ₹20 lakh Equity. This is the fundamental accounting equation — and it's the same for every company on earth, from a corner shop to Reliance Industries. A balance sheet is taken at a specific date (like a photograph). It doesn't show what happened over the year — that's the Income Statement's job. Instead, it shows the company's exact financial position at that moment: what resources it controls, what obligations it has, and how much belongs to the owners.

02 — The Three Pillars

Assets, Liabilities & Equity Explained

Every balance sheet is built on exactly three components. Understanding each one — what goes in it, why it matters, and how to read it — is the foundation of all fundamental analysis.

🏛️ EQUITY (Shareholders' Funds)
Share Capital
Face value of all shares issued. Fixed amount. Rarely changes unless new shares are issued.
Reserves & Surplus
Profits retained in the business over all years. Growing reserves = company is profitable and reinvesting.
Other Equity Items
Securities premium, general reserve, retained earnings. All profits not distributed as dividends accumulate here.
💳 LIABILITIES (What Company Owes)
Borrowings (Long-term)
Bank loans, bonds, debentures due after 12 months. High borrowings = higher financial risk.
Short-term Borrowings
Working capital loans, overdraft. Due within 12 months. Should not be excessive relative to current assets.
Trade Payables
Money owed to suppliers. Normal business liabilities. High payables = company has pricing power with suppliers.
Other Liabilities
Deferred tax, provisions, advances from customers, employee obligations.
🏗️ ASSETS (What Company Owns)
Fixed Assets (Net Block)
Land, buildings, machinery, equipment — physical assets that generate revenue. Listed at cost minus depreciation.
Investments
Equity stakes, mutual funds, bonds held by the company. Income from investments flows to P&L.
Current Assets
Cash, inventory, trade receivables, short-term investments. Converted to cash within 12 months.
Other Assets
Goodwill, intangible assets, deferred tax assets, long-term advances.
Total Assets = Total Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity
This equation always balances — that is why it's called a "balance" sheet. If it doesn't balance, there's an error.

Every company's balance sheet has the same three parts — Equity (owners' money), Liabilities (money owed), and Assets (what the company owns). They always balance: Assets = Liabilities + Equity.

E

Equity — The Owners' Permanent Investment

Equity = Share Capital + Reserves. This is money that belongs to shareholders permanently — it's not a loan, it doesn't need to be repaid. Share capital is the face value of all shares issued. Reserves are cumulative retained profits (all earnings not paid as dividends over all years). Growing equity = the company is profitably retaining earnings. For CDSL (from the screenshot), reserves grew from ₹265 Cr in 2015 to ₹1,751 Cr in 2026 — showing consistent profitability over a decade.

L

Liabilities — The Company's Obligations

Liabilities are divided into Non-Current (due after 12 months: long-term loans, bonds) and Current (due within 12 months: short-term borrowings, trade payables, advances). High debt relative to equity is dangerous — it means more of the company's earnings go to interest payments. CDSL's borrowings are near-zero across all years — a sign of exceptional financial strength for a market infrastructure company.

A

Assets — Everything the Company Controls

Assets split into Fixed (Non-Current: land, machinery, goodwill, investments held long-term) and Current (inventory, cash, receivables due within 12 months). The asset mix tells you the business model: asset-heavy companies (steel, power) have massive fixed assets. Asset-light companies (IT, financial services like CDSL) have mostly investments and current assets. Notice CDSL's investments = ₹1,487 Cr in 2026 — its main "asset" is its investment portfolio, not physical machinery.

⚖️

Why Both Sides Always Balance

This isn't a coincidence — it's double-entry bookkeeping. Every transaction affects at least two entries. Buy machinery for ₹100 with cash: Assets (machinery +₹100, cash −₹100) — net zero change. Buy machinery by taking a loan: Assets +₹100, Liability +₹100 — both sides increase by equal amounts. The balance sheet can never NOT balance. If it doesn't, there's a data error somewhere.

05 — What to Look for in Any Balance Sheet

3 Things Every Investor Should Check First

You don't need to read every line. These three checks take under 2 minutes and give you 80% of the insight you need from any balance sheet.

📈
Check 1: Is Equity Growing?
Open Screener.in, search any company, click Balance Sheet. Look at Reserves trend over 10 years. Growing reserves = company is retaining profits, compounding equity. Flat or declining reserves = losses or 100% dividend payout. For CDSL: ₹265 Cr → ₹1,751 Cr in 11 years = 19% CAGR in book value. This single data point tells you the company is consistently profitable and reinvesting for growth.
💳
Check 2: What Is the Debt Level?
Find Borrowings row. Calculate Debt/Equity (Borrowings ÷ Equity). Below 0.5× = conservative. 1-2× = moderate. Above 3× = high risk for most sectors (except banks and capital-intensive infrastructure). Also check Debt/EBITDA from the P&L. CDSL's near-zero borrowings is the ideal — no financial risk, full cash flow retained for shareholders. High debt is not always bad (banks, roads, power) — context matters.
🏗️
Check 3: Asset Quality and Composition
Look at what the company's assets actually are. Growing Fixed Assets = investment in capacity expansion (positive if revenue grows with it). Large Goodwill = past acquisitions (could be overvalued). High Trade Receivables vs Revenue = customers slow to pay (cash flow problem). Large CWIP = projects under construction (short-term drag, long-term capacity). Cash + Investments as main assets = asset-light, capital-efficient business (like CDSL, TCS).
