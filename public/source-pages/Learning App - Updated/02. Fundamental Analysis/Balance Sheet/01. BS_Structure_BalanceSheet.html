What Is a Balance Sheet? Start Here.

Before getting into numbers and line items, let's understand the core idea using something everyone relates to — buying a house. If you get this analogy, you've already understood 80% of the balance sheet.

🏠
The Big Idea
A Balance Sheet = A financial photograph taken on ONE specific day. It shows what a company owns, owes, and what remains for its owners.
You bought a house worth ₹60 lakh. You used ₹20 lakh of your own savings and took a ₹40 lakh bank loan. On that day, your finances look like this:

🏠 You OWN a house = ₹60 lakh  →  This is an Asset
🏦 You OWE the bank = ₹40 lakh  →  This is a Liability
💰 Your real share = ₹60L − ₹40L = ₹20 lakh  →  This is Equity

Every company's balance sheet works exactly the same way — just with bigger numbers!
🏗️ Asset = What the company OWNS 💳 Liability = What the company OWES 🏛️ Equity = What owners KEEP

Here's how the house example maps to the three sections of every balance sheet 👇

🏠
Section 1 — Asset
What You OWN
₹60 Lakh
The house itself. Something valuable that can be used or sold. Assets are resources that generate money in the future.
Shown on the LEFT side
🏦
Section 2 — Liability
What You OWE
₹40 Lakh
The bank loan you must repay. A legal obligation to someone outside — must be paid before you get anything.
Shown on the RIGHT (top)
💰
Section 3 — Equity
YOUR Real Share
₹20 Lakh
What's truly yours after paying all debts. Sell the house, repay the bank → ₹20L is yours. This is the owners' wealth.
Shown on the RIGHT (bottom)
The One Rule You Must Never Forget
Assets  =  Liabilities  +  Equity
This equation holds true for every balance sheet in the world — from a corner tea stall to Reliance. It ALWAYS balances. If it doesn't, there's an accounting error. This is exactly why it's called a "balance" sheet.

The 3 Pillars

Assets, Liabilities & Equity — Explained Simply

Every single number on a balance sheet belongs to one of these three sections. Learn what goes in each, why it matters, and how to spot it when reading any real company's annual report.

Picture This — A Company's Balance Sheet in One Image

Building, machines, and cash = Assets. Bank loan and unpaid salaries = Liabilities. What remains for the owner = Equity.

🏗️
Pillar 1 — Assets
What the Company Owns
Assets are everything the company controls that can make money now or in the future. Buildings, machines, cash, products in the warehouse, money customers owe — all assets.
🏭 Fixed Assets (Non-Current)
Things that stay for more than 1 year — land, factories, machines, vehicles. The backbone of any manufacturing business. Shown at cost minus depreciation.
📦 Current Assets
Things that convert to cash within 12 months — cash in bank, inventory (products ready to sell), trade receivables (money customers owe you).
🧠 Intangible Assets
No physical form but real value — brand name (Amul), patents, software licences. Worth thousands of crores despite being invisible.
💡 Quick test: "Can the company use this to make money?" → Yes = it's an Asset.
💳
Pillar 2 — Liabilities
What the Company Owes
All the money the company must pay to outsiders — banks, suppliers, employees, and the government. These must be settled before owners get a single rupee.
🏛️ Long-term Liabilities
Debts due after 1 year — big bank loans, bonds issued to investors, debentures. Example: A 15-year loan to build a factory. Repaid slowly over many years.
⚡ Short-term Liabilities
Debts due within 12 months — unpaid supplier bills (trade payables), employee salaries owed, short-term bank loans, advance received from customers.
📋 Provisions
Money set aside for expected future costs — warranty repairs, pending legal cases, employee pensions. Like keeping ₹50L ready for possible warranty claims.
💡 Quick test: "Does the company owe this to someone outside?" → Yes = it's a Liability.
🏛️
Pillar 3 — Equity
What Belongs to Shareholders
Equity is what's left for owners after paying ALL liabilities. It's not a debt — never repaid. When the company earns profit and keeps it inside, equity grows every single year.
📋 Share Capital
Face value of all shares issued (usually ₹1, ₹2, ₹5 or ₹10/share). Very small and rarely changes. Infosys has ~₹420 Cr share capital at ₹5/share.
📈 Reserves & Surplus
All profits kept inside the company over many years. The MOST important equity number — watch it grow year after year. TCS has over ₹1 lakh crore built by retaining profits.
💵 Retained Earnings
This year's profit not paid as dividend — kept for reinvestment. Gets added to Reserves at year end. Each year of profit compounds equity. This is how wealth is built.
💡 Quick test: "Is this what remains for owners after paying everyone else?" → Yes = it's Equity.

Here's exactly how these 3 sections appear inside a real balance sheet 👇

🏛️ EQUITY — Owners' Money
Share Capital
Face value of shares. TCS: ~₹370 Cr at ₹1/share. Small number, almost never changes.
Reserves & Surplus
Profits saved year after year inside the business. The real wealth engine — grows with every profitable year.
Securities Premium
Extra amount when shares issued above face value. Face ₹10 but issued at ₹500 → ₹490 premium stored here.
Retained Earnings (P&L)
This year's unspent profit. Gets added to Reserves at year end — compounding in action, year after year.
💳 LIABILITIES — What's Owed
Long-term Borrowings
Bank loans & bonds due after 12 months. High borrowings = higher interest burden. Check if profits comfortably cover interest costs.
Short-term Borrowings
Working capital loans, bank overdraft. Must be repaid within 1 year. Should be covered by current assets to maintain liquidity.
Trade Payables
Money owed to suppliers for goods already received. High payables vs receivables = strong supplier negotiating power (like Walmart).
Other Current Liabilities
Salaries payable, taxes due, advances from customers, provisions. Routine day-to-day obligations from running the business.
🏗️ ASSETS — What's Owned
Fixed Assets (Net Block)
Land, buildings, factory, machines. Shown at original cost MINUS depreciation. A ₹10 Cr machine 50% depreciated = ₹5 Cr in books.
Long-term Investments
Shares in subsidiaries, long-term bonds. Income flows to P&L as dividends. Important for holding companies and conglomerates.
Current Assets
Cash, inventory, trade receivables (money customers owe). All convert to cash within 12 months — the company's short-term fuel.
Intangibles & CWIP
Brand, patents, goodwill. CWIP = Capital Work in Progress = projects under construction, not yet generating revenue.
Total Assets  =  Total Liabilities  +  Shareholders' Equity
This adds up perfectly on every single balance sheet ever published. If it doesn't — there's a bookkeeping mistake somewhere.

How to Read Any Balance Sheet — 3 Things to Check First

You don't need to read every line. When you open a balance sheet on Screener.in, just check these three things in order. They take under 2 minutes and give you 80% of what you need to judge a company's financial health.

STEP 1
Read Equity Capital + Reserves
Top rows show Equity Capital (face value) and Reserves (retained profits). Growing reserves year-over-year = consistently profitable business. This is the "book value" foundation.
Where on Screener: Balance Sheet → Top 2 rows under Equity section
✅ Reserves growing every year = Great sign
🚨 Reserves shrinking or negative = Red flag
STEP 2
Check Borrowings Level & Trend
Borrowings row tells you debt. Rising debt with no revenue growth = red flag. Zero or minimal debt with growing equity = financial fortress. Check Debt/Equity ratio and trend.
Where on Screener: Balance Sheet → Borrowings row + Ratios tab → D/E
✅ Borrowings = 0 or falling = Excellent
🚨 Borrowings rising faster than equity = Danger
STEP 3
Analyse Asset Composition
Fixed Assets show physical asset base. Investments reveal financial holdings. Other Assets include receivables and cash. The asset mix reveals the business model type — capital-heavy vs asset-light.
Where on Screener: Balance Sheet → Assets section, click + to expand
✅ Cash + Investments dominating = Asset-light moat
🚨 CWIP stuck for years = Project delays / mismanagement
⚡
Bonus Check — Takes 10 Seconds
Do Total Assets = Total Liabilities + Equity?
Scroll to the bottom of the balance sheet on Screener. If the Total Assets row and Total Liabilities row show the same number — the books balance. This is always true by definition. If they don't match on Screener, it's a data display issue, not a company problem. CDSL example: both sides = ₹2,419 Cr in Mar 2026. ✅

What each section of the balance sheet tells you at a glance 👇

🏛️
Equity Section
Is the company building wealth?
Look at Reserves trend. If growing → YES. If shrinking → NO.
💳
Liabilities Section
Is the company over-leveraged?
Check Borrowings vs Equity. D/E below 0.5 = safe. Above 2 = watch carefully.
🏗️
Assets Section
What kind of business is this?
Mostly cash = asset-light (TCS). Mostly fixed assets = capital-heavy (Reliance).
⚖️
The Equation
Are the books correct?
Total Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Always. No exception. Ever.

What does each row actually mean? 👇

🏛️ Equity Capital = ₹104 Cr (stable for 9 years)

What it means: CDSL issued ~104 crore shares at ₹10 face value. This number stayed flat from 2015 to 2024 — meaning no new shares were issued. In 2025 it jumped to ₹209 Cr because CDSL did a 1:1 bonus share issue (gave existing shareholders one free share each). Investors love this — it means the company rewarded shareholders from its own profits.

📈 Reserves = ₹265 Cr → ₹1,751 Cr (11-year journey)

What it means: This is the most exciting line. In 2015, CDSL had saved up ₹265 crore of profits. By 2026, that number hit ₹1,751 crore — a 6.6x increase in 11 years! Every year, CDSL earned profit, paid some as dividend, and kept the rest here. This is compounding in action. Growing reserves = the company is profitable and reinvesting intelligently.

💳 Borrowings = ₹0 for 8 Straight Years!

What it means: CDSL took ZERO debt from 2015 to 2022. Not even ₹1 of bank loan. Then ₹2 Cr appeared in 2023 (tiny, possibly a lease obligation). This is the hallmark of an exceptional business — one that grows entirely on its own profits without needing to borrow from anyone. Zero debt = zero financial risk. Any business can have a bad year; CDSL doesn't need a banker to survive it.

📋 Other Liabilities = ₹122 Cr → ₹457 Cr (normal growth)

What it means: These are routine business obligations — advance deposits from brokers who pay CDSL to use its infrastructure, service fees collected in advance, employee dues, and taxes payable. As CDSL's business grew 5x, these liabilities grew proportionally. Nothing alarming — this is normal operational growth, not dangerous debt.

🏗️ Fixed Assets = ₹7 Cr → ₹502 Cr (infrastructure build-out)

What it means: CDSL owns servers, data centres, and offices. For years, fixed assets were tiny (₹7–75 Cr) — classic asset-light model. From 2024 onwards it jumped sharply as CDSL invested in new data centres to handle the explosion in demat accounts (from 2 crore to 13+ crore). Still modest compared to any manufacturing company — CDSL's real assets are its software systems and regulatory licence.

💰 Investments = ₹389 Cr → ₹1,487 Cr (where the real cash sits)

What it means: This is CDSL's accumulated cash deployed into safe instruments — government bonds, Fixed Deposits, mutual funds. Since CDSL has no debt and earns more than it spends, spare cash gets invested here. In Mar 2026, ₹1,487 Cr sits in investments earning steady interest. This "investment income" flows to the P&L as other income — making CDSL's total income even higher than its operating revenue alone.

Equity Health · Trick 2

Book Value per Share = Total Equity ÷ Number of Shares. It tells you how much each share is "worth" based on what the company actually owns (after paying all debts). A growing Book Value means the company is genuinely building wealth for shareholders — not just showing paper profits.

Book value growing 15%+ per year consistently

Book value flat or declining for 3+ years
Stock price much higher than book value with low ROE
Equity falling due to accumulated losses

Equity Health · Trick 3
Is Equity Capital Diluted? (New Share Issues)
When a company keeps issuing new shares to raise money (dilution), each existing shareholder owns a smaller piece. Watch the "Equity Capital" row. If it keeps rising, the company is constantly diluting shareholders — often a sign it can't fund growth from its own profits and keeps going back to investors with a begging bowl.
What to check on Screener
Equity Capital row → should be stable or reduce (buyback)
Exception: A one-time rise due to a rights issue for a major acquisition can be fine. Multiple dilutions over 5 years = red flag.
✅ Good Signs
Equity Capital stable for 5–10 years (no dilution)
Equity Capital falling = buybacks (company buying own shares — reward to shareholders)
CDSL: ₹104 Cr stable for 9 years — no dilution

Equity Capital doubling every 3–4 years (constant dilution)

Debt Quality · Trick 4
Debt/Equity Ratio — Is Debt in Control?
The most famous balance sheet ratio. Debt/Equity tells you how much the company borrowed vs how much belongs to owners. A high D/E means the company is highly leveraged — even a small business slowdown can push it into trouble. But remember: D/E benchmarks differ by sector — banks and infra are naturally high.
Formula
D/E = Total Borrowings ÷ Total Equity
Rule of thumb: Below 0.5x = very safe. 0.5–1x = comfortable. 1–2x = watch carefully. Above 3x = risky (for non-infra/bank). Find Borrowings and Total Equity rows on Screener.
✅ Good D/E Levels
CDSL: ~0.001x (essentially debt-free)
Bajaj Auto: 0.0x (zero long-term debt)
Pidilite: ~0.02x (near debt-free)
🚨 Bad D/E Levels
Vodafone Idea: deeply negative (more debt than assets)
IL&FS: 10x+ (debt far exceeds equity)
Suzlon (peak): 8x — almost collapsed from debt
Screener shortcut: Go to any company → Ratios section → Find "Debt to equity" — it's pre-calculated! No manual math needed. Also check the 10-year trend: is D/E falling? Rising? Stable?
5
Debt Quality · Trick 5
Interest Coverage — Can It Afford Its Debt?
Having debt is not automatically bad. The real question is: can the company comfortably pay the interest on that debt from its operating profits? Interest Coverage Ratio answers this. You'll need the P&L for this, but the debt number comes from the balance sheet. If profit barely covers interest — any dip in business spells disaster.
Formula
Interest Coverage = EBIT ÷ Interest Expense
EBIT = Earnings Before Interest & Tax (from P&L). Rule: Above 3x = safe. Above 5x = very safe. Below 1.5x = danger zone — profits barely cover interest. Below 1x = company losing money after interest.
✅ Good Signs
Interest coverage above 5x for 5+ consecutive years
CDSL: essentially infinite (zero interest expense)
Reliance: EBITDA ~8x its net interest cost
🚨 Bad Signs
Interest coverage below 2x — any dip = trouble
Coverage ratio falling every year (profits declining, debt rising)
Company paying interest from fresh borrowings = Ponzi
Screener shortcut: Search the company → Ratios tab → Find "Interest Coverage Ratio" — it's shown directly. If the ratio has been below 2 for multiple years, treat it as a red flag regardless of the company's reputation.
6
Debt Quality · Trick 6
Is Debt Growing Faster Than Equity? (The Danger Signal)
Even if D/E looks acceptable today, watch the trend. If borrowings are growing at 30% per year but equity is growing at only 10%, the gap will explode in a few years. This means the company is borrowing to survive — not to grow. Contrast this with a company where equity grows faster than debt every year.
What to check
Borrowings Growth Rate vs Equity Growth Rate (5-year CAGR)
Healthy: Equity growing faster than borrowings. Danger: Borrowings growing much faster than equity AND reserves are stagnant or falling.
✅ Good Signs
Equity growing 20%/year, debt growing 5%/year
Debt actually falling while equity rising (deleveraging)
Suzlon 2020–25: paid off ₹11,000 Cr debt — textbook good
🚨 Bad Signs
Debt doubling while equity stays flat for 3+ years
New borrowings taken to repay old borrowings
IL&FS: borrowings grew 3x while equity barely moved
How to check on Screener: Go to Balance Sheet → look at Borrowings row across 10 years → then look at Total Equity across 10 years → calculate which one grew faster. That one calculation tells you the story of the company's financial health over a decade.
7
Asset Quality · Trick 7
Trade Receivables — Are Customers Paying on Time?
Trade Receivables = money customers owe the company for goods/services already delivered. If receivables grow much faster than revenue, it means customers are taking longer to pay — or worse, some may never pay. High receivables tied up in slow-paying customers = company is quietly funding someone else's business with its own money.
Formula — Debtor Days
Debtor Days = (Trade Receivables ÷ Revenue) × 365
Rule of thumb: Below 45 days = good. 45–90 days = acceptable. Above 90 days = investigate why. Sudden jump in debtor days = collections getting harder = cash flow pressure ahead.
✅ Good Signs
Debtor days stable or falling over 5 years
Pidilite: receivable days ~35 — customers pay fast
FMCG companies: near-zero receivables (cash sales)
🚨 Bad Signs
Receivables growing 30% while revenue grows only 10%
Debtor days suddenly jumping from 40 to 90 in one year
Many PSU contractors: receivables from govt. stuck for years
Screener tip: Go to company → Balance Sheet → click the + next to "Other Assets" to expand → find Trade Receivables. Then open the P&L tab for revenue. Divide and multiply by 365. If debtor days are rising every year, the company's profits on paper may not be turning into real cash.
8
Asset Quality · Trick 8
CWIP Alert — Are Projects Getting Stuck?
CWIP (Capital Work in Progress) is the amount a company has spent building assets that are NOT yet complete — factories under construction, machinery being installed. Some CWIP is normal. But if CWIP stays very high for 3–5 years without converting to Fixed Assets, projects are delayed — wasting capital and generating no revenue. Called "CWIP trap."
What to track
CWIP → Fixed Assets conversion over 2–3 years
Healthy: CWIP appears, then drops as it converts to Fixed Assets the next year. Unhealthy: CWIP stays elevated for 3+ years — means projects are delayed, cost overruns, or management is parking money (fraud risk).
✅ Good Signs
CWIP converts to Fixed Assets within 1–2 years
CDSL: CWIP ₹174 Cr in Mar 23 → dropped to ₹4 Cr Mar 24 (completed!)
Fixed Assets rising as CWIP falls = healthy capex cycle
🚨 Bad Signs
CWIP stuck at same level for 4–5 years — projects not completing
CWIP growing but Fixed Assets not growing → money going where?
Some infra companies: CWIP elevated with repeated project delays
How to spot it: On Screener Balance Sheet, look at the CWIP row across multiple years. A healthy company shows CWIP fluctuating as projects start and complete. Alarm bells ring when CWIP is large AND growing every year without a matching growth in Fixed Assets.
9
Liquidity Check · Trick 9
Current Ratio — Can It Pay Its Bills This Year?
Current Ratio measures whether a company can pay all its bills due in the next 12 months. It compares Current Assets (cash, inventory, receivables) to Current Liabilities (supplier bills, short-term loans, salaries due). If a company can't pay its short-term bills, it doesn't matter how profitable it is on paper — it can collapse suddenly.
Formula
Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
Rule: Above 1.5 = comfortable. 1.0–1.5 = tight but manageable. Below 1.0 = danger — company cannot cover short-term obligations. Find both in Screener under the Balance Sheet expanded rows.
✅ Good Signs
Current Ratio above 1.5 for 5+ consecutive years
Bajaj Auto: current ratio comfortably above 2x
Pidilite: strong current ratio — no liquidity stress
🚨 Bad Signs
Current ratio below 1 — cannot pay short-term bills
Future Retail pre-collapse: current ratio below 0.5
Ratio falling every year signals growing liquidity stress
Screener shortcut: Find "Current ratio" in the Ratios section — already calculated for you. Better: look at the 10-year trend. A slowly declining current ratio is an early warning sign that's usually ignored until it's too late. Spot it early!
10
Liquidity Check · Trick 10
Cash & Investments — Is There a Safety Cushion?
Great companies build up a cash and investment cushion over time. On the balance sheet, look at "Cash & Equivalents" and "Investments" under Assets. Companies with large, growing cash piles have enormous advantages — they can grab opportunities during downturns, survive bad years without borrowing, and reward shareholders. Cash is the ultimate buffer.
What to look for
Cash + Current Investments (check both lines under Assets)
Compare total cash to annual interest expense. If cash > 5 years of interest expense = fortress balance sheet. If cash is declining every year = business is burning cash faster than it earns.
✅ Good Signs
Cash & investments growing every year without borrowing
CDSL: ₹1,487 Cr in investments — massive for its size
Bajaj Auto: ₹18,000+ Cr — earns more from investments than some companies do from operations
🚨 Bad Signs
Cash declining every year while debt is rising
Company paying dividends from borrowed money (not profits)
Zero cash buffer + high short-term debt = dangerous
Power move: On Screener, compare "Cash & Equivalents" + "Investments" across 10 years for any company. Then compare this to its "Borrowings" row. A company where cash > borrowings for multiple years is financially fortress-like. Infosys, TCS, Bajaj Auto all qualify.

Quick Reference — Green Flags vs Red Flags on Any Balance Sheet 🏁

🟢
Green Flags — Invest with Confidence
Reserves growing every year for 5+ years
Means profits are consistently earned and retained — real wealth being built inside the company.
Debt/Equity below 0.5x (or near zero)
Company is largely self-funded. No big interest burden. Profits are truly for shareholders — not for bankers.
Current Ratio consistently above 1.5
Liquid enough to handle short-term shocks. Won't scramble for cash during a bad quarter.
Trade Receivable Days below 45 and stable
Customers pay quickly. Cash flow is real — not just accounting profits that never arrive as cash.
Cash & Investments growing every year
Company building a financial fortress. Future opportunities can be captured without borrowing.
Equity Capital stable (no repeated dilutions)
Shareholders not being diluted. Company funds growth from its own profits, not by issuing new shares.
🔴
Red Flags — Investigate Before Investing
Reserves flat or declining for 3+ years
Company is either losing money or paying out all profits. Equity is not compounding. Wealth is not being built.
Borrowings growing much faster than equity
Dangerous leverage build-up. A bad quarter can spiral into a debt crisis — exactly what happened to IL&FS and Suzlon.
Current Ratio below 1.0
Company cannot pay its own short-term bills from its current assets. One bad month can trigger a supplier crisis.
Trade Receivables growing 2–3x faster than revenue
Sales being "booked" but money not arriving. Could signal channel stuffing (fake sales) or customers in trouble.
CWIP stuck for 3+ years without converting
Projects delayed or stalled. Capital stuck, earning nothing, while interest keeps ticking. Common in weak infra companies.
Negative equity (liabilities exceed assets)
Technically insolvent. Every rupee of "profit" goes to lenders first. Shareholders are last — and there's nothing left.

The Most Important Reminder
No Single Trick Works Alone — Use All 10 Together
A company may have zero debt (Trick 4 ✅) but exploding receivables (Trick 7 🚨). Another may have high debt (Trick 4 🚨) but exceptional interest coverage (Trick 5 ✅) and growing equity (Trick 1 ✅) — like Reliance in its early Jio years. Always use all 10 tricks together, weight them by sector context, and combine with P&L and Cash Flow analysis before deciding. The balance sheet is one piece of a three-part puzzle.
