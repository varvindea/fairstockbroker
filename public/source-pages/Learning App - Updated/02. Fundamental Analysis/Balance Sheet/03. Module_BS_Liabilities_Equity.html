01 — Key Learning Outcome

Liabilities & Equity Are the Funding Side of the Balance Sheet

💳
Your Core Takeaway
Every asset a company owns was funded from one of two sources: liabilities (borrowed money — must be repaid with interest) or equity (owners' money — no repayment obligation). The mix, quality, and trend of these funding sources determines financial resilience.
Imagine building a factory worth ₹100 crore. You could fund it with ₹40 Cr from shareholders (equity) and ₹60 Cr bank loan (liability). The bank loan is cheaper in good times but dangerous in a recession — interest must be paid even if the factory sits idle. Shareholder equity has no fixed payment obligation. Current liabilities are money owed within 12 months — trade payables to suppliers, short-term loans, advance deposits from customers. Long-term debt is money borrowed for 3–20 years — bonds, term loans from banks. Deferred tax is a timing adjustment. Retained earnings are the accumulation of all profits the company reinvested back rather than distributing as dividends — growing retained earnings is the single best sign of a self-funding, profitable business. A manufacturing company that funds 70%+ of assets from equity and retained earnings, keeps current liabilities manageable, and maintains Net Debt/EBITDA below 2× is in excellent financial health.
💳 Liabilities = Borrowed Funds 🏛️ Equity = Owners' Funds 📈 Retained Earnings = Self-Funding ⚖️ Net Debt/EBITDA <3× Target
02 — The Five Funding Components

What Each Line Item Actually Means

Five components fund every company's asset base. Each has distinct characteristics, risk profiles, and signals that matter differently for manufacturing companies versus financial services or technology firms.

All five funding sources (Current Liabilities, Long-term Debt, Deferred Tax, Equity, Retained Earnings) flow into the same factory. The mix determines financial safety. Equity and retained earnings are the safest; current liabilities must be carefully managed.

Working Capital — Liquidity Health
Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities
Positive = company funds day-ops from own assets. Negative = suppliers fund operations (powerful advantage). Crisis: WC declining fast + current ratio below 1.
Net Debt Ratio — Debt Safety Check
Net Debt/EBITDA = (Total Debt − Cash) ÷ EBITDA
Below 2× = comfortable. 2–3× = moderate. Above 3× = elevated risk. Above 5× = financial distress zone for most manufacturers.
Book Value Per Share — Equity Floor
BVPS = Total Shareholders' Equity ÷ Shares Outstanding
P/B ratio = Market Price ÷ BVPS. P/B below 1 = market values company below book (distressed or undervalued). P/B above 3 = market rewards quality/moat.
Retained Earnings Growth — Self-Funding Power
RE = Previous RE + Net Profit − Dividends Paid
Growing retained earnings = company is profitably compounding equity without new share issuance. Declining RE = losses eroding equity base — existential risk if multi-year.
💳
Current Liabilities
Obligations due within 12 months · Operational funding · WC management
Time Horizon: ≤12 months · Must be repaid or rolled over
Current liabilities are the short-term obligations that a company must settle within one year. They include four main items: Trade Payables — money the company owes to its raw material suppliers. Short-term borrowings — working capital loans from banks, overdraft facilities. Advance payments received — customer deposits paid before goods are delivered (e.g., real estate bookings, capital equipment orders). Accrued expenses — salaries payable, utilities billed but not yet paid.

The most important metric derived from current liabilities is Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities. A manufacturing company's working capital tells you how efficiently the business manages its cash cycle. Positive Working Capital means the company uses its own assets to fund operations — normal for most manufacturers. Negative Working Capital is a competitive superpower: companies like Nestle India and Asian Paints collect cash from retailers before paying their raw material suppliers — meaning suppliers effectively provide free financing to the business.

High trade payables relative to cost of goods sold = company has strong bargaining power over suppliers and is using supplier credit efficiently. Watch: if short-term borrowings are rising rapidly without revenue growth, the company may be using expensive working capital loans to fund losses — a serious early warning.

Long-Term Debt
Bonds · Debentures · Term loans · Due beyond 12 months
Fixed interest obligation · Independent of profitability
Long-term debt is money borrowed for periods exceeding 12 months — typically bank term loans (3–10 years), Non-Convertible Debentures (NCDs), bonds, and external commercial borrowings (ECBs). The critical difference from equity: interest on debt must be paid regardless of whether the company makes a profit. If EBITDA falls and the company cannot cover interest payments, it enters financial distress.

For manufacturing companies, long-term debt is often necessary and appropriate — a steel plant costs ₹10,000 Cr and generates returns over 30 years, so borrowing to build it makes financial sense. The question is always: is the debt level sustainable relative to earnings? Net Debt/EBITDA is the primary metric: below 2× is comfortable for most manufacturers, 2–3× is manageable, above 3× demands close monitoring, and above 5× is the danger zone where any EBITDA decline can trigger covenant breaches or credit downgrades.

The Interest Coverage Ratio (EBIT ÷ Interest Expense) is equally important: above 5× is excellent, 2–4× is moderate, below 1.5× means the company is barely covering its interest from operations — one bad year could push it into default. Capital-intensive sectors like steel, cement, and power have historically comfortable with higher debt due to their tangible asset collateral and stable long-term demand, but all investors should track debt trend over 3–5 years, not just the current year snapshot.

Deferred Tax Liability / Asset
Timing differences between accounting profit and taxable profit
Non-cash item · Timing adjustment · Complex but essential
Deferred tax arises because accounting rules (IndAS/GAAP) and income tax rules treat certain items differently — specifically the timing of when income is recognized and when expenses are deducted.

Deferred Tax Liability (DTL) arises when tax payment is deferred to a future period. The most common example: a company uses accelerated depreciation for tax purposes (claiming larger depreciation deductions early) but straight-line depreciation for its accounts. The result: lower taxable income today (lower tax now), higher taxable income later. The difference is a DTL — tax owed but not yet paid. DTL is a liability on the balance sheet.

Deferred Tax Asset (DTA) arises when the company has already paid taxes on income or expenses that haven't been recognized in accounting yet, OR when it has losses that can be carried forward to offset future taxes. Manufacturing companies with losses often build up large DTAs — but a DTA is only valuable if the company is expected to be profitable in the future to actually utilise those tax savings. An impaired DTA (written off because future profits are uncertain) is a bad sign.

For most analysts, deferred tax is a secondary concern — but when net profit margins are unusually high or low compared to EBITDA margins, check whether the difference includes large deferred tax movements. A company reporting ₹500 Cr PAT but paying only ₹20 Cr actual tax while carrying a growing DTL may have lower "true" PAT than the headline suggests.
⚠️ Key check: Operating Cash Flow vs PAT. If OCF consistently much lower than PAT, and a growing DTL exists, some reported profit may reverse as tax becomes payable in future periods. DTL doesn't mean fraud — but it does mean some "profit" is timing-based rather than permanently earned.
🏛️
Shareholders' Equity
Paid-up capital · Share premium · Retained earnings · Other reserves
No repayment obligation · Permanent capital · Real ownership stake
Shareholders' equity is the residual claim on a company's assets after all liabilities have been deducted: Equity = Total Assets − Total Liabilities. It consists of four components:

Paid-up Capital — face value (₹1 or ₹10) of all shares issued and subscribed. This changes only when new shares are issued (IPO, rights issue, ESOP) or shares are bought back.

Share Premium (Securities Premium) — the amount received above face value when shares were issued. If a company issued shares at ₹500 per share with face value ₹10, the ₹490 excess goes into share premium. This is a permanent reserve — not distributable as dividend.

Retained Earnings — cumulative net profits reinvested in the business over all years. The largest component of equity for mature profitable companies.

Other Reserves — general reserve, capital redemption reserve, foreign currency translation reserve.

Book Value Per Share = Total Equity ÷ Shares Outstanding. Book value is especially important for asset-heavy companies (steel, cement, power, banking). P/B ratio below 1 means the market values the company below what its net assets are worth on paper — either the company is genuinely cheap or the assets are overvalued on the books. P/B above 3–5× indicates the market recognises significant intangible value (brand, technology, franchise) beyond book value.

Retained Earnings
Cumulative net profits reinvested · The self-funding growth engine
= Cumulative (Net Profit − Dividends) since inception
Retained earnings (also called "Reserves and Surplus" on Indian balance sheets) represent the total of all net profits the company has earned since its founding, minus all dividends paid to shareholders. This single line item tells you more about the quality of a business than almost any other number.

Formula: RE = Previous Year RE + Current Year Net Profit − Dividends Paid

For a manufacturing company established in 1990 with ₹10 Cr initial capital and consistent 15% ROE over 30 years with 40% payout ratio: retained earnings could have compounded to ₹800+ Cr — creating a business that is now largely self-funding and has no need to issue new equity or borrow heavily for maintenance and moderate growth.

Growing Retained Earnings is the most powerful signal of a self-funding, profitable business. Asian Paints grew its reserves from ₹8,752 Cr (FY2020) to ₹15,180 Cr (FY2025) — entirely from retained profits, with zero new equity issuance and zero meaningful debt. That's the definition of compounding retained earnings.

Declining Retained Earnings is the opposite signal — and one of the most dangerous patterns on a balance sheet. When a company consistently reports net losses, those losses are charged against retained earnings: RE shrinks. If losses continue and RE turns negative (accumulated deficit), total equity shrinks. If equity approaches zero or turns negative, the company is technically insolvent — its total liabilities exceed total assets. Vodafone Idea, Jaiprakash Associates, and MTNL all show this pattern — massive negative reserves representing decades of cumulative losses.
✅ The single most important long-term signal: growing retained earnings + growing revenue + stable or declining debt = a company compounding its value organically. No analyst shortcut replaces checking this 10-year retained earnings trend on Screener.in.
03 — What to Look For

Healthy Signals vs Red Flags

Two minutes of liability and equity analysis separates safe manufacturing investments from financial landmines. These signals apply across all manufacturing sub-sectors.

✅ Healthy Funding Signals
📈

Retained Earnings Growing Faster Than Revenue

When reserves compound at 15%+ annually without new equity dilution, the business is self-funding its growth from profits. This is the most powerful positive signal available from the liability/equity section — it means no new debt and no dilution are needed to sustain expansion.

⚖️

Net Debt/EBITDA Stable or Declining

Even if a company carries debt, declining Net Debt/EBITDA over 3–5 years signals that cash generation is outpacing debt accumulation. The company is growing into its debt — exactly what should happen post a major capacity expansion. Best-in-class: reaching Net Debt/EBITDA below 1× signals near debt-free status.

💳

Trade Payables > Trade Receivables (Negative Working Capital)

When a manufacturer owes more to suppliers than customers owe to it, suppliers are effectively providing free financing. This negative working capital model — exemplified by Nestle India, Asian Paints, and Hindustan Lever — allows the company to operate with minimal capital and generate extraordinary returns on equity.

🏛️

ROE Consistently Above 15–20% Without Leverage

High ROE (return on equity) with low debt means the business genuinely earns superior returns on its capital base — not just financial engineering through leverage. This is the most reliable profitability signal from the equity section: quality businesses compound equity at high rates without excessive borrowing risk.

🚨 Liability / Equity Red Flags
📉

Retained Earnings Declining / Turning Negative

Negative reserves (accumulated deficit) means cumulative losses have exceeded all historical profits. This is the clearest sign of a deeply distressed company. When combined with still-high borrowings, the company's total liabilities can exceed total assets — technical insolvency. Vodafone Idea's -₹1.88 lakh Cr reserves is the most extreme current example.

📋

Net Debt/EBITDA Above 4–5× and Rising

Debt growing faster than EBITDA is the classic pre-distress signal. When interest payments consume 40%+ of EBITDA, even a minor revenue decline can push a company into covenant breach. Watch for EBITDA declining while debt stays constant — that's equivalent to debt rising on a relative basis. Multiple credit rating downgrades often follow.

🔄

Short-Term Borrowings Funding Long-Term Assets

Using short-term (1-year) loans to build infrastructure that earns returns over 15 years is an asset-liability mismatch — one of the most dangerous balance sheet patterns. If the short-term loans cannot be renewed at a critical moment (credit crunch, rate spike), the company faces a liquidity crisis even if it's technically solvent. IL&FS collapsed due to exactly this mismatch.

💸

Equity Capital Growing Without Revenue Growth

Repeated equity dilution (rights issues, preferential allotments) without proportional revenue or profit growth means existing shareholders are being diluted while the company continues to struggle. Each new share issued reduces earnings per share for existing investors. Vodafone Idea has issued massive equity multiple times, yet losses continue — dilution without recovery.

Final Key Insight
Retained Earnings Is the Single Most Honest Signal in the Entire Balance Sheet
Every accounting choice — depreciation method, revenue recognition timing, provisioning policy — affects the income statement. But retained earnings reflects the cumulative result of all these choices since the company was founded. A company can manage one year's profit; it cannot manage 20 years of cumulative retained earnings. Asian Paints' ₹15,180 Cr reserves versus MTNL's −₹26,204 Cr deficit tells you everything you need to know about the difference between these businesses — one has created wealth for decades, the other has destroyed it.
