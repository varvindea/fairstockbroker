Your Core Takeaway — Debt Maturity Changes Everything
The same amount of debt can be safe or dangerous depending on WHEN it must be repaid and HOW the company earns money to repay it.
Imagine two friends each borrowed ₹10 lakh. Friend A has a 20-year home loan repaid monthly from salary — comfortable and manageable. Friend B borrowed on a credit card due in 3 months — a crisis. Same amount, completely different risk. Companies work identically. A company with ₹10,000 Cr in 30-year infrastructure bonds is safe. A company with ₹10,000 Cr in commercial paper due next month is in serious trouble. 02 — Four Types of Debt You See on Screener.in

Understanding Every Debt Instrument

When you expand the Borrowings row (+) on any company’s Screener.in balance sheet, you see multiple sub-items. Each type has different risk, cost, and repayment characteristics. Here is exactly what each one means in plain English.

⚡
Type 1 — Highest Risk
Short-term Borrowings
⏰ Due Within 12 Months
Short-term borrowings must be repaid within 1 year. Companies use these to fund daily operations — buying raw materials, paying wages, bridging the gap between when they spend cash and when customers pay. The danger: if these cannot be renewed, the company faces an immediate cash crisis.
Cash Credit (CC): A revolving credit line from banks for day-to-day operations. Interest is charged only on the amount actually used, not the full limit.
Bank Overdraft: Drawing more than the bank account balance. Very short-term, high interest rates. Used for emergency liquidity only.
Commercial Paper (CP): Short-term bonds issued for 7–365 days. Only large creditworthy companies can issue these at low rates.
Working Capital Loans: Specific loans tied to the business cycle — typically renewed every year by the bank based on current-year performance.
🚨 Why risky: If the company cannot refinance at maturity, it faces an immediate liquidity crisis. IL&FS collapsed in 2018 when its short-term commercial paper could not be rolled over — assets existed but cash did not. Always ask: is short-term debt larger than current assets?
🏛️
Type 2 — Manageable Risk
Long-term Borrowings
📅 Due After 12 Months
Long-term borrowings have repayment schedules beyond 1 year — typically 3 to 30 years. Companies use these to fund capital expenditure: building factories, buying machinery, acquiring land. Long timelines give the company years to generate enough cash to repay from operations.
Term Loans: Standard bank loans for 3–15 years with structured EMI repayment. The most common type of long-term debt for Indian companies. Interest + principal repaid monthly.
External Commercial Borrowings (ECB): Loans from foreign banks or institutions, often at lower interest rates — but carry currency risk if rupee depreciates.
Deferred Payment Liabilities: Instalments owed to equipment suppliers, paid over time as the machinery earns revenue. Common in capital goods industries.
Lease Liabilities (Ind AS 116): Long-term leases treated as debt since 2019. This is often a pure accounting item — the actual cash payment is the monthly rent, not a loan repayment.
💡 Context matters: A manufacturer with ₹5,000 Cr long-term debt to build a new factory is healthy if operating profit covers interest 5× or more (ICR >5×). The same debt is dangerous if operating cash flow is negative or ICR is below 2×.
📄
Type 3 — Market Instrument
Non-Convertible Debentures (NCD)
📊 Listed on Stock Exchange
NCDs are debt instruments listed on NSE/BSE and traded like shares. Unlike convertible debentures, they cannot be converted to equity shares — they are pure, predictable debt. Companies issue NCDs when they want fixed-cost capital from investors without giving up any ownership or control.
Public Issue NCDs: Offered to retail investors via the stock exchange. Interest rate fixed upfront (e.g. 8.5% per annum for 3 years). Minimum investment typically ₹10,000.
Private Placement NCDs: Sold directly to institutional investors (mutual funds, insurance cos.) — faster and cheaper process, no public prospectus needed.
Secured NCDs: Backed by specific company assets. If company defaults, NCD holders get those assets first before other creditors.
Unsecured NCDs: No asset backing. Higher interest rate compensates investors for the higher risk. Riskier for investors but cheaper for the company.
💡 Good signal: Companies that successfully raise NCDs at low interest rates have strong credit ratings. Bajaj Finance and HDFC regularly issue NCDs at 6–8% — reflecting investor confidence in their repayment ability. A high NCD interest rate (12%+) signals credit risk.
🎍
Type 4 — Sovereign & Corporate
Bonds
📈 Fixed Income Security
Bonds are long-term debt securities with a fixed maturity date, coupon rate (interest), and defined principal repayment. Corporations issue bonds to raise large amounts from institutional investors globally. Unlike NCDs (India-focused), bonds are often international instruments reaching foreign investors.
Corporate Bonds: Issued by companies for 3–30 years. Interest paid semi-annually or annually as a coupon. Traded on exchanges and in over-the-counter markets.
Masala Bonds: Rupee-denominated bonds issued overseas to foreign investors. The company has no currency risk because repayment is in rupees, but the foreign investor takes the FX risk.
Green Bonds: Proceeds earmarked exclusively for environmental or sustainability projects. Growing rapidly in India — Greenko, Power Finance Corp, NTPC have issued these.
Perpetual Bonds (AT1): No maturity date — interest paid indefinitely. Used by banks for Tier-1 regulatory capital. Highest risk category for bond investors.
💡 For investors: A company that successfully issues bonds in international markets (e.g. Reliance’s USD bonds) signals strong global creditworthiness. The interest rate on the bond versus its credit rating tells you exactly what the market thinks of its risk. AAA bonds get the lowest rates; BBB or lower get penalised.

03 — Three Ratios That Reveal Debt Health

Assess Any Company’s Debt In Under 2 Minutes

You do not need to read every footnote of an annual report. These three ratios — all available free on Screener.in — tell you whether a company’s debt is safe, borderline, or dangerous before you invest a single rupee.

⚖️
Ratio 1
Debt-to-Equity (D/E)
D/E = Total Borrowings ÷ Total Equity
How much debt the company has relative to shareholder funds. D/E of 1× means £1 of debt for every £1 of equity. High D/E means lenders own more of the economics than shareholders, and interest costs squeeze profits heavily. For banks, D/E is not applicable — use Capital Adequacy Ratio instead.
Safe: <0.5×Caution: 0.5–2×Risky: >3×
🔥
Ratio 2 — Most Important
Interest Coverage Ratio (ICR)
ICR = EBIT ÷ Interest Expense
The single most important debt safety ratio. It tells you how many times the company can pay its annual interest bill from operating profit. ICR of 5× means profit is 5× the interest. Below 1.5× means the company barely covers interest — any revenue slowdown triggers default risk. This is the ratio that catches failing companies first.
Safe: >5×Caution: 2–5×Danger: <1.5×
💧
Ratio 3
Current Ratio
Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
Measures ability to pay short-term debt. Current assets = cash + receivables + inventory. Current liabilities = all debt and payables due within 1 year. Below 1× means the company cannot cover upcoming payments from liquid assets — serious immediate liquidity risk. Nestle India runs below 1 deliberately due to its negative working capital model — always understand the business model before judging.
Safe: >1.5×Caution: 1–1.5×Danger: <1×

5 Screener.in Shortcuts Every Investor Must Know

These are field-tested tricks used by experienced investors to spot debt problems or strength in under 5 minutes — without reading a single line of an annual report. All data is free on Screener.in.

1
Screener.in — Balance Sheet Tab
The “5-Year Borrowings Trend” Trick
The single fastest debt check: look at the Borrowings row across 10 years on Screener.in. Is it going up, flat, or down? A rising borrowings row year after year means the company is taking on more debt every year. If profit is also rising, that may be fine. But if borrowings rise while profits stay flat — the company is borrowing to survive, not to grow. A falling borrowings row with rising profits is the ideal signal.
→ Screener.in → search company → Balance Sheet tab
→ Find the “Borrowings” row — read it left to right (oldest to newest)
→ Compare 5-year Borrowings trend vs 5-year Net Profit trend (Profit & Loss tab)
✔ Borrowings falling + Profits rising = Excellent ⚠ Both rising = Investigate if productive ✗ Borrowings rising + Profits flat/falling = Red flag
2
Screener.in — Ratios Tab (ICR Chart)
The “ICR Trend Chart” Trick
Most investors only check the current ICR number. The real trick is the ICR trend over 5–10 years. A company with ICR of 8× today that was 20× five years ago is declining — debt is becoming more burdensome relative to profits. A company with ICR of 6× today that was 2× five years ago is improving — the turnaround is happening. The direction matters more than the absolute number at any single point.
→ Screener.in → Ratios tab → find “Interest Coverage Ratio”
→ Click the chart icon (📈) next to ICR to see the 10-year trend
→ Ask: is ICR rising or falling over the last 3–5 years?
✔ ICR rising year-on-year = Getting safer ⚠ ICR flat at 2–4× = Borderline, monitor ✗ ICR falling below 3× = Getting riskier, investigate

Screener.in — Balance Sheet Expand (+)
The “Expand Borrowings” Trick
Most investors see the total Borrowings number and stop. The real trick is clicking the + next to Borrowings to expand it into Short-term and Long-term sub-categories. This tells you the maturity profile instantly. If Short-term Borrowings > Long-term Borrowings, the company has more debt due within 12 months than later — this is a serious liquidity risk signal that most casual investors completely miss because they only see the total.
→ Balance Sheet tab → find Borrowings row → click the + on the left
→ Two rows appear: “Short-term Borrowings” and “Long-term Borrowings”
→ Compare: which is bigger? This is the maturity profile in one glance
✔ Long-term >> Short-term = Good maturity profile ⚠ Both roughly equal = Monitor refinancing schedule ✗ Short-term > Long-term = High rollover risk, investigate urgently
4
Screener.in — Compare D/E vs ROE
The “Debt Efficiency” Trick
High debt is not always bad — if the company earns high returns on that debt, it actually benefits shareholders. The trick is to compare Debt-to-Equity (D/E) with Return on Equity (ROE). If D/E is 2× but ROE is 30%+, the company is using debt productively. If D/E is 2× and ROE is 8%, debt is barely earning back its own cost. This is called “financial leverage” — and Screener.in shows both numbers on the Ratios tab side by side.
→ Ratios tab → note down D/E ratio (5-year trend)
→ Ratios tab → note down ROE (Return on Equity, 5-year trend)
→ Ask: Is ROE well above the cost of debt (~8–12%)? Is it rising?
✔ High D/E + Very high ROE (25%+) = Productive leverage ⚠ High D/E + Medium ROE (12–20%) = Moderate efficiency ✗ High D/E + Low ROE (<10%) = Debt destroying value
5
Screener.in — Cash Flow Statement Tab
The “Cash Flow vs Borrowing” Trick — The Most Powerful One
The most powerful and least-used trick: open the Cash Flow Statement on Screener.in and look at two numbers in the “Financing Activities” section: Proceeds from Borrowings (how much new debt was taken on) vs Repayment of Borrowings (how much debt was repaid). If the company consistently repays more than it borrows, it is getting lighter. If proceeds consistently exceed repayments, debt is growing. Also compare Operating Cash Flow (from Operations section) with Interest Paid — if Operating CF is 3×+ interest paid, the company can comfortably service its debt from actual business operations without needing to borrow more.
→ Cash Flow tab → Financing Activities section
→ Find “Proceeds from Borrowings” (positive = new debt taken)
→ Find “Repayment of Borrowings” (negative = debt repaid)
→ Net = Repayment − Proceeds. Positive = debt falling. Negative = debt growing
→ Operating Activities section → find “Interest Paid”
→ Find “Cash from Operations” (top of Operating section)
→ Calculate: Operating CF ÷ Interest Paid = Cash ICR (more reliable than reported ICR)
→ Cash ICR >3× = company comfortably services debt from real cash
✔ Net repayment + Operating CF >3× interest = Strong debt health ⚠ New debt > repayment but Operating CF covers interest = Investigate growth rationale ✗ New debt > repayment + Operating CF < interest = Company borrowing just to pay interest (death spiral)

⚠ Special Cases — When Standard Checks Don’t Apply
Banks, NBFCs, Infrastructure Companies
For Banks: Use Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR >11%), Gross NPA (<3%), Net NPA (<1%), CASA ratio (>35%) — NOT D/E or ICR.  |  For NBFCs: Use D/E carefully (1:7 gearing is normal), check NPA and ALM (Asset Liability Mismatch).  |  For Infra companies: Long-term project debt is normal — focus on project cash flows and concession agreements, not D/E alone.
🎯
The One-Line Debt Filter
If a Company Can Pay Its Interest 5× Over from Operations and Debt Is Falling — Stop Worrying About Its Debt
ICR >5× and borrowings trending down over 5 years covers 90% of debt risk for non-financial companies. This combination means the company earns far more than it needs to pay interest, and it is using that surplus to repay debt rather than piling on more. Find it in 60 seconds on Screener.in Ratios tab → Interest Coverage + Debt/Equity → click chart icon for both. If both lines show the right trend, proceed to the next aspect of your analysis. If either is wrong, dig deeper before investing.
