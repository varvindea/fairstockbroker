02 — Three Types of Hidden Obligations

Contingent Liabilities, OBS Items & Guarantees Explained

Hidden risks come in three flavours. Each sits in a different part of the annual report, carries different probability of becoming a real cash outflow, and requires a different evaluation approach. Understanding the difference is the first step to uncovering them.

⚖️
Type 1 — May Crystallise
Contingent Liabilities
Contingent liabilities are possible future obligations that depend on the outcome of an uncertain event — typically a lawsuit, government demand, or regulatory order. They are disclosed in the Notes to Accounts of every annual report but do NOT appear on the main balance sheet unless the liability becomes probable and quantifiable. The company must disclose them if: (a) a possible obligation exists, and (b) it cannot be reliably estimated to be remote.
Income tax disputes: Tax department claims additional taxes; company contests in court. Could be ₹500 Cr or ₹0 depending on verdict.
GST / Excise demands: Indirect tax notices under appeal. Very common in manufacturing companies.
Customer / vendor lawsuits: Breach of contract claims, product liability suits, labour disputes.
Environmental liabilities: Pollution clean-up orders, regulatory penalties under appeal.
⚠ Key rule: If total contingent liabilities exceed 20–30% of net worth, a single adverse verdict could materially hurt the balance sheet. Always check: has the company won or lost similar cases before? Is the quantum growing year-on-year?
📋
Type 2 — Real But Hidden
Off-Balance Sheet Items
Off-balance sheet items are real, committed obligations that accounting rules allow to be kept off the main balance sheet. Unlike contingent liabilities (which may never materialise), these WILL require cash payments — they are just structured to avoid the balance sheet. Post Ind AS 116 (2019), most operating leases are now on the balance sheet — but other items still remain off.
Take-or-pay contracts: Company committed to buy raw material / power whether or not it uses it. Steel plants, power generators sign these.
Capital commitments: Signed contracts to buy machinery / land not yet delivered. Shown in notes as “Capital commitments not provided for.”
Undrawn loan commitments: Banks have committed to lend (credit lines sanctioned) but not yet drawn down. An off-BS contingent obligation for banks.
Sale and leaseback: Company sells an asset then leases it back — generates cash today but creates long-term lease obligation.
💡 Where to find: Notes to Accounts → “Commitments and Contingencies” section. Also in the Cash Flow Statement footnotes. Screener.in links directly to the Annual Report where these notes live.
🏥
Type 3 — Backing Others’ Debt
Corporate Guarantees
A corporate guarantee is when Company A promises to repay Company B’s loan if Company B defaults. If Company B is a subsidiary, associate, or promoter-related entity, this is common and often disclosed. If Company B defaults, Company A must pay — converting a hidden obligation into a very real and sudden liability. Promoter share pledges work similarly: if the stock price falls below a threshold, lenders can sell pledged shares, causing the stock to crash further.
Subsidiary guarantees: Parent company guarantees loans taken by wholly-owned subsidiaries. Usually disclosed in notes.
JV guarantees: Company guarantees debt of a joint venture. Risk depends on JV’s financial health.
Promoter pledges: Promoter has pledged company shares to get personal loans. Visible in shareholding data; if stock falls, lenders dump shares.
Letters of comfort: Softer version of guarantee — parent signals intention to support subsidiary without formal legal obligation.
💡 Where to find: (a) Notes to Accounts → Related Party Transactions & Guarantees Given. (b) BSE/NSE shareholding disclosures → promoter pledge percentage. (c) Subsidiary financials in the Annual Report → check their loan-to-equity ratio.
The Hidden Risk Danger Ladder — Low to Critical
How Hidden Risks Escalate into Real Losses
Not all contingent liabilities are equal. This ladder shows how a disclosed risk can move from harmless to catastrophic, and what warning signs to watch for at each stage.
1
🟢 DISCLOSED — Remote Probability
Liability disclosed in notes. Company’s legal team believes outcome is unlikely. Small amount. Multiple similar cases won historically. Safe to ignore unless amount is very large.
2
🟡 WATCH — Possible but Uncertain
Medium-sized tax dispute or lawsuit under appeal. Outcome unclear. Company has provisioned some amount. Monitor for verdict dates and provision increases in future quarters.
3
🟠 ESCALATING — Amount Growing Year on Year
Contingent liability quantum is rising every year in the notes. New cases being added. This signals either growing legal risk or aggressive tax authority. Warrants deep investigation before investing.
4
🔴 DANGER — Exceeds 30% of Net Worth
Total contingent liabilities exceed 30% of shareholders’ equity. A single adverse outcome could materially impair the balance sheet. Large guarantees to weak subsidiaries. High promoter pledge (>50%). Must investigate immediately.
5
🔴 CRITICAL — Crystallising into Real Loss
Court ruling against company. Tax demand upheld. Subsidiary defaults, triggering guarantee. Lenders sell pledged shares. The hidden risk becomes a reported loss overnight. By this point, the stock has already crashed. Investors who read the notes ahead of time had a chance to exit.

🚨 The 3 Questions to Ask for Every Contingent Liability

Question 1: How large is it relative to net worth? Under 10% = manageable. Over 30% = investigate urgently. Question 2: Is the amount growing year-on-year? Rising contingent liabilities = new legal problems being added or existing ones escalating. Question 3: What has the company’s track record been? If past contingent liabilities were resolved in the company’s favour — current ones are likely manageable. If past liabilities resulted in actual outflows — treat current ones more seriously. All three answers come from comparing the last 5 years of annual report Notes to Accounts sections.

☀️ Promoter Pledge — The Fastest Hidden Risk Check

The single fastest hidden risk check for any stock: Go to BSE/NSE → search company → Shareholding Pattern → look at Promoter section → find “Shares Pledged or Encumbered.” If this number is above 20% of promoter holding, the promoter has taken personal loans against company shares. If the stock falls significantly, lenders dump those shares in the market, pushing the price down further — a dangerous spiral. Screener.in also shows this data in the Shareholding section. Zero pledge (like CDSL) = best. Over 50% pledge = serious warning. Over 70% = avoid entirely.

05 — Tricks to Find Hidden Risks Instantly

5 Shortcuts to Uncover Hidden Obligations

These are field-tested tricks to spot contingent liabilities and off-balance sheet risks in under 10 minutes — before they become tomorrow’s headline. All data is publicly available.

1
Annual Report — Notes to Accounts
The “Contingent Liab. as % of Net Worth” Trick
Every annual report must disclose contingent liabilities in a specific note. Open the Annual Report (linked from Screener.in) and search for “Contingent Liabilities.” Note the total amount. Then find Total Equity (Share Capital + Reserves) from the main balance sheet. Calculate: Contingent Liabilities ÷ Total Equity × 100. Under 10% = safe. 10–30% = monitor carefully. Over 30% = investigate every item individually before investing.
→ Screener.in → Annual Report tab → download PDF
→ Search “Contingent Liabilities” in the PDF → note total amount
→ Calculate: Total Contingent Liab. ÷ Total Equity × 100
✔ <10% of equity = safe ⚠ 10–30% = monitor ✗ >30% = investigate urgently
2
BSE / NSE — Shareholding Pattern Quarterly
The “Promoter Pledge %” Trick
BSE and NSE publish promoter shareholding data every quarter, including the exact number of shares pledged. Go to BSE.com → search company → Shareholding Pattern → Promoter section → “Shares pledged or otherwise encumbered.” Compare with total promoter holding to get pledge %. Screener.in also shows this under the Shareholding section. Any pledge over 20% of promoter holding warrants attention. Over 50% is serious. Over 70% is a major red flag. Check this every quarter — a rising pledge % is more dangerous than a high static number.
→ BSE.com or NSE.com → search company → Shareholding Pattern
→ Promoter section → find “Shares Pledged” column
→ Calculate: Pledged shares ÷ Total promoter shares × 100
✔ 0% pledge = ideal (CDSL, Bajaj) ⚠ 20–50% = watch quarterly ✗ >50% & rising = serious warning
3
Annual Report — Related Party Notes
The “Guarantees Given to Subsidiaries” Trick
Search the Annual Report for “Guarantees given on behalf of” or “Related Party Transactions.” This reveals all guarantees the parent has given to subsidiaries, JVs, and associates. Once you find the amount, go to the subsidiary’s standalone financials (usually in the same Annual Report under the subsidiary list) and check: is the subsidiary profitable? Is it growing? If the subsidiary is loss-making and the parent has given large guarantees, the risk of crystallisation is high. Loss-making subsidiary + large guarantee = active time bomb.
→ Annual Report → search “Guarantees given on behalf”
→ Note amount → find subsidiary in Annual Report subsidiaries list
→ Check: is the subsidiary profitable? PAT positive or negative?
✔ Guarantee to profitable subsidiary = low risk ⚠ Small guarantee, moderate subsidiary = watch ✗ Large guarantee + loss-making sub. = time bomb
4
Annual Report — Year-on-Year Comparison
The “Rising Contingent Liab. Trend” Trick
A single year’s contingent liability number means little without context. The real trick is comparing Notes to Accounts for 3 consecutive annual reports to see if the total is rising, stable, or falling. A rising quantum signals either new legal cases being added, existing disputes escalating in magnitude, or aggressive tax authorities. A falling quantum (cases being resolved in company’s favour) is actually a positive signal — it shows the company’s management is resolving legal disputes successfully. A sudden jump in one year is the most alarming signal — new major legal exposure has appeared.
→ Download 3 years of Annual Reports → find Contingent Liab. note in each
→ Write down total for FY22, FY23, FY24, FY25 → compare trend
→ Is it rising, flat, or falling? Sudden jump = new major legal risk
✔ Falling trend = cases being resolved ⚠ Stable = ongoing normal disputes ✗ Rising sharply = new major risk has emerged
5
Annual Report + Screener.in — The Complete Hidden Risk Scan
The “30-Minute Hidden Risk Scan” — The Most Complete Check
Combine all four tricks above into a systematic 30-minute scan before any investment. This covers 90% of hidden risks that cause sudden stock collapses. The checklist is designed to be done in sequence: if any step fails the test, investigate deeply before proceeding to the next step.
1. Promoter Pledge: BSE.com → Shareholding → Pledge % → Over 20%? Stop and investigate.
2. Contingent Liab. %: Annual Report notes → Contingent Liab. ÷ Equity → Over 30%? Investigate each item.
3. Guarantee scan: Notes → “Guarantees given” → Find subsidiaries → Are they profitable?
4. 3-Year trend: Compare contingent liab. for 3 years → Rising sharply? New risk exposure.
5. Capital commitments: Notes → “Capital Commitments” → Large unspent commitments funded how?
6. Related party loans: Notes → RPT section → Large loans to promoter entities? Red flag.
7. Auditor’s report: Check for “emphasis of matter” or qualified opinions → Auditor is flagging something.
8. Cash flow vs profit: Screener.in → Compare Operating CF vs PAT → Large divergence = earnings quality risk.
✔ All 8 clear = hidden risk is low, proceed with analysis ⚠ 1–2 issues = investigate before buying ✗ 3+ issues = very high hidden risk, likely avoid

The One-Line Hidden Risk Filter
Zero Promoter Pledge + Contingent Liabilities <10% of Net Worth + No Guarantees to Loss-Making Subsidiaries = Hidden Risk Is Probably Manageable
This three-condition filter takes 10 minutes to check and eliminates the majority of companies with serious hidden risk exposure. CDSL, Bajaj Auto, TCS, and Pidilite all pass it effortlessly.
