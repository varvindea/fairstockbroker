Related Party Transactions Identify Wealth Transfer Risks Before They Destroy Your Investment

When promoters use a listed company to channel money into their private pockets — through sweetheart deals, inflated royalties, and fake loans — minority shareholders pay the price. This chapter teaches you to spot these wealth transfers before the market does.

01 — Key Learning Outcome

Why Promoter Wealth Transfers Are the Silent Killer of Returns

🕳️
Your Core Takeaway
A listed company can look profitable on every visible metric — growing revenues, rising profits, impressive ROCE — while simultaneously having money quietly drained out through related party transactions. This hidden value extraction is called "tunnelling," and it can make a fundamentally good business a permanently bad investment.
Imagine you own 20% of a profitable bakery. The owner (promoter) also owns a separate flour supply company. He decides the bakery will buy flour exclusively from his private flour company — at twice the market price. The bakery's reported costs rise, profits fall, and your 20% stake becomes worth less every year. Meanwhile the owner is extracting the bakery's profits into his private company through the inflated flour price. This is tunnelling — and it happens in Indian listed companies through royalty payments, management fees, related-party loans at below-market interest, asset purchases at above-market prices, and sweetheart contracts. The terrifying part: it is all legal. It's disclosed in the annual report. It just requires careful reading to spot. The skill you develop here: identify which Related Party Transactions represent genuine business needs versus structured wealth extraction, and quantify how much of the company's profit is flowing out to promoter-controlled entities before it reaches you as a minority shareholder.

02 — Understanding RPTs

What Are Related Party Transactions — And Why Do They Exist?

Not all Related Party Transactions are bad. In fact, some are completely normal and necessary. The skill is distinguishing genuine business transactions from disguised wealth transfers. Here is the complete picture.

🏢 Who Is a "Related Party"?
Anyone connected to the company's promoters or key management — the web of relationships that creates RPT risk
A "related party" includes: promoter and promoter group companies, subsidiaries, associates, joint ventures, key management personnel and their relatives. Every rupee that flows between the listed company and any of these entities must be disclosed — but not all flows are suspicious.
📖
Where to Find RPT Disclosures — Free
Annual Report
Every listed company must disclose all RPTs in its Annual Report under the Notes to Financial Statements — typically Notes 34–40 depending on the company. The exact note is titled "Related Party Disclosures" or "Transactions with Related Parties." It lists every entity, every transaction type, and every rupee amount. SEBI also requires all "material" RPTs (above 10% of revenue) to be approved by shareholders at AGM. Pro tip: search the PDF for "related party" and jump directly to the section. The number of pages devoted to RPT disclosures is itself informative — complex corporate groups have much longer disclosures.
⚖️
The Key Question: Arm's Length or Sweetheart Deal?
Core Concept
"Arm's length" means the transaction is priced as if the two parties were strangers negotiating freely. A company paying its subsidiary ₹100/unit for a component that the market price is ₹95/unit — that's arm's length. A company paying its promoter's private firm ₹200/unit for the same component — that's a sweetheart deal. The challenge: companies are not required to disclose whether prices paid to related parties are at, above, or below market rates. You have to deduce this from the disclosed amounts versus publicly available market data.
🧮
The Simple Calculation That Reveals Everything
Investor Technique
Take total outflows to related parties (management fees + royalties + purchases from promoter entities + loans given) and divide by Net Profit. This gives you the RPT Drain Ratio. Below 5%: generally acceptable. 5–15%: monitor carefully. Above 15%: major red flag. Above 25%: significant portion of your investment returns may be flowing to promoter entities instead of compounding for you. Compare this ratio across 5 years — a rising RPT drain ratio while profits stagnate is one of the clearest tunnelling signals available.
03 — Three Types of RPTs

Legitimate, Suspicious & Dangerous — How to Classify Every Transaction

Not every related party transaction is a red flag. The same transaction structure — say, a service fee to a promoter company — can be completely legitimate or outright value extraction depending on the amount, pricing, and trend. Here is the classification framework every investor needs.

✅
Legitimate RPTs
Generally Safe
Transactions that exist for genuine business reasons, are priced at or near market rates, and benefit the listed company — not just the promoter. These are common in large corporate groups where shared services create real efficiencies.
✅ Examples: Shared IT infrastructure from parent company at reasonable cost · Trademark licence from promoter at industry-standard royalty rates (1–3% of revenue) · Purchase of raw materials from group company at publicly verifiable market prices · Salary paid to promoter-MD at reasonable levels relative to company size and profitability
⚠️
Suspicious RPTs
Monitor Carefully
Transactions that may be legitimate but whose pricing or scale raises questions. These don't prove tunnelling but require investigation — check whether the transaction terms are explained clearly and whether comparable market data supports the amounts paid.
⚠️ Examples: Royalty fees above 5% of revenue for a domestic brand (vs global FMCG standard of 1–3%) · Management fee paid to promoter holding company with vague "services" description · Advances given to related parties without clear repayment terms or interest · Purchase of assets from promoter entity without disclosed independent valuation
🚨
Dangerous RPTs (Tunnelling)
Exit Signal
Transactions that are structured primarily to extract value from the listed company to promoter entities, with no corresponding benefit to minority shareholders. These are the signatures of tunnelling — legal but deeply hostile to minority investor interests.
🚨 Examples: Interest-free loans to promoter group companies (listed co loses interest income) · Buying land/property from promoter at prices significantly above market · Providing guarantees for promoter's private company borrowings (listed co takes risk, promoter gets credit) · Selling assets to promoter entities at below-market prices · Persistent and growing "advances to related parties" that are never recovered
🕳️ How Tunnelling Actually Works — The Four-Step Wealth Transfer
How promoters legally extract value from listed companies into their private pockets — step by step
1
🏭
Listed Company Earns Profit
The listed company (which minority shareholders own) earns ₹100 crore in operating profit from its genuine business operations.
2
📄
Related Party Transaction Created
Promoter's private company invoices the listed company for "management consulting," "brand royalty," or "technology fee." Board approves. Disclosed in annual report.
3
💸
Cash Leaves Listed Company
Listed company pays ₹20 crore to the promoter's private company. Net profit of listed company falls from ₹100Cr to ₹80Cr. Minority shareholders' wealth drops proportionally.
4
🏠
Promoter Personally Benefits
Promoter's private company now holds ₹20 crore that originally belonged (proportionally) to all shareholders. Minority shareholders received nothing for this "service."
🔑 The Key Insight: Tunnelling is perfectly legal and fully disclosed — the promoter breaks no law. But it systematically transfers wealth from minority shareholders to the promoter's private entities. Over 5–10 years, a company tunnelling 20% of profits annually delivers dramatically lower returns to minority shareholders than its fundamental earnings growth would suggest.
04 — Your RPT Audit Checklist

Red Flags to Avoid vs Green Signals to Embrace

🚨 Red Flags — Investigate Before Investing
Any two of these together = pause and examine the annual report carefully before committing capital
Financial Red Flags
💸
RPT outflows exceed 15% of net profit
Total payments to related parties (royalties + fees + purchases above market) exceeding 15% of PAT means a significant slice of your returns flows to the promoter's private pocket.
📈
Royalty rate rising faster than brand investment
If royalty payments are growing 25% annually while the company's advertising spend and revenue grow only 8%, the promoter is increasing extraction without proportional brand value creation.
🏦
Loans/advances to related parties that aren't repaid
If the "advances to related parties" line on the balance sheet grows year after year without being recovered, it is effectively a permanent capital outflow disguised as a loan.
🏠
Asset purchases from promoter at inflated prices
When the listed company buys land, plant, or IP from the promoter's private entity without a disclosed independent valuation, the promoter can set the price at any level.
Governance Red Flags
🗳️
RPTs approved without independent director dissent
When every single RPT resolution passes unanimously — including promoter-enriching transactions — it suggests the Board is not independently scrutinising terms. Genuine oversight shows occasional dissent.
📋
Vague service descriptions ("management support," "consulting")
When the listed company pays crores to a promoter entity for vaguely described services with no quantifiable deliverable, it is almost always a disguised extraction rather than a genuine commercial arrangement.
🛡️
Corporate guarantees for promoter's private borrowings
The listed company puts its balance sheet at risk (by guaranteeing promoter's private company loans) while the promoter gets cheap credit. If the private company defaults, the listed company's shareholders bear the loss.
📉
Rising RPT/PAT ratio during profit growth
If RPT payments grow faster than profits during good years, it means the promoter extracts proportionally more when the company does well — structurally disadvantaging minority shareholders in boom years.
✅ Green Signals — Good Governance Indicators
Companies with most of these consistently are the cleanest governance stories in Indian markets
Financial Clean Signals
✅
RPT outflows below 5% of net profit
Total related party outflows under 5% of PAT means essentially all earnings stay within the listed entity and compound for all shareholders equally.
📊
Royalty at or below global industry standard (1–3% of revenue)
International FMCG and tech companies typically charge 1–3% royalties. Indian companies that stay within this band for established brands are pricing fairly.
💰
Zero or minimal loans to related parties
Companies that never extend loans to promoter entities — keeping the listed company's capital working within the business — treat minority shareholders as equal partners.
Governance Clean Signals
🏛️
Independent audit committee with active oversight
When independent directors on the audit committee visibly scrutinise and sometimes reject or renegotiate RPT terms, it signals genuine governance. Look for audit committee reports in the annual report.
📋
RPTs disclosed with specific pricing rationale
When the annual report explains exactly why the related party price was used, what the market comparator was, and which independent valuer confirmed it — transparency that protects minority shareholders.
📉
Declining RPT/PAT ratio over time
A falling RPT drain ratio over 5+ years means the promoter is progressively extracting less — or the business is growing faster than RPT payments — both shareholder-positive trends.
05 — Five Real Examples

RPT & Tunnelling in Practice — From Clean Governance to Organised Extraction

Five Indian companies across the full spectrum — from the cleanest RPT records in the country to some of the most egregious tunnelling cases that have destroyed minority shareholder wealth. Each example shows exactly what to look for in the annual report.

Example 01 · Royalty Under Scrutiny · NSE: MARUTI
Maruti Suzuki
Maruti Suzuki pays royalty to its Japanese parent Suzuki Motor Corporation for technology, brand, and engineering. The royalty rate — and whether it is fairly priced for the value received — has been a persistent question from minority investors, making it India's most-discussed legitimate RPT debate.
Royalty to Suzuki Japan ~5–6% of Net Revenue Technology transfer Disclosed, board-approved
The RPT Structure
💸
Annual royalty to Suzuki Motor Corporation (Japan)
Maruti pays royalty for every vehicle using Suzuki technology, platforms, and powertrains. The amount ranges from ₹3,500–5,500 Cr annually in recent years, representing roughly 5–6% of net revenue. Suzuki Japan owns 58% of Maruti — so 58% of these royalties effectively come back to the majority shareholder anyway. The 42% minority shareholders pay the full royalty but receive proportionally less.
⚖️
Is the royalty rate fair? The debate
Global auto royalty benchmarks vary widely: 2–3% for mature platforms, 5–8% for cutting-edge technology transfer to emerging markets. Maruti's rate has historically been at the higher end, though it has been gradually renegotiated downward over time. Key question: is the technology access worth 5–6% of revenue? Given Maruti's 42%+ market share and consistent R&D advantage, many analysts consider the royalty broadly justified — but the rate remains legitimately higher than pure-play Indian companies pay.
✅
What makes this acceptable (with monitoring)
The royalty is fully disclosed with specifics. Maruti's market leadership and engineering capabilities are directly enabled by the technology access. The Board includes independent directors from reputable institutions who actively scrutinise the terms. The rate has been renegotiated multiple times. This is a legitimate RPT — but one worth monitoring for any rate creep.

⚠️ Verdict — Legitimate But Needs Annual Monitoring
Maruti's royalty to Suzuki is the textbook example of a grey-area RPT. The technology is real, the benefit is real, the disclosure is full. But the rate is at the higher end of global benchmarks and 42% minority shareholders pay the full cost. The right approach: accept the royalty as a business reality but monitor the rate annually. Any increase without corresponding new technology access should be treated as a negative governance signal.
Example 02 · Clean Governance Benchmark · NSE: ASIANPAINT
Asian Paints
Asian Paints consistently exemplifies best-in-class RPT governance among Indian large-caps — minimal transactions with promoter entities, no loans to related parties, and full transparency about every inter-company transaction that does exist. A model to benchmark others against.
Minimal promoter RPTs Zero loans to promoters Full disclosure always Independent board oversight
RPT Track Record
✅
RPT outflows consistently below 3% of PAT
Asian Paints' total related party outflows — primarily transactions with its subsidiaries and associates in overseas markets — represent a very small fraction of net profit. The promoter family (Choksi family) does not charge management fees, royalties, or brand fees to the listed company. The promoter's wealth grows through the appreciation of their stake, not through extraction from the listed entity.
✅
Zero loans to promoter entities over 20+ year track record
There is no instance in the last 20+ years of Asian Paints extending loans or guarantees to promoter family companies. The balance sheet does not show any "advances to related parties" growing inexplicably. This clean record is a direct result of governance culture that treats all shareholders equally.
✅
Inter-company transactions fully explained with pricing basis
Where transactions do exist between Asian Paints and its overseas subsidiaries, the annual report discloses the transaction type, amount, and confirms pricing is on arm's length commercial terms. Independent directors on the audit committee review and certify these transactions annually.

Asian Paints is the benchmark for what RPT governance should look like. The promoter family has created extraordinary wealth over decades — not by extracting from the listed company, but by letting it compound efficiently for all shareholders. Every rupee of profit stays in the listed entity, working for all shareholders equally. When analysing any company's RPTs, the first question should be: how does this compare to Asian Paints' record?

Example 03 · India's Biggest Tunnelling Case · NSE: SATYAMCOMP (delisted)
Satyam Computers
The 2009 Satyam fraud was India's most egregious tunnelling case — Chairman Ramalinga Raju used the listed IT company as a fund source for his private real estate empire, routing money through fictitious transactions and fake cash balances totalling ₹7,136 crore over a decade.
₹7,136 Cr extracted Fake invoices to subsidiaries Real estate tunnelling Stock → ₹0 in 2 days
The Tunnelling Mechanisms Used
🏠
Fake transactions to promoter real estate companies
Satyam routed cash to Raju family real estate entities (Maytas Properties, Maytas Infrastructure — "Satyam" spelled backwards) through fictitious service contracts. The listed IT company paid for "technology services" that were never actually delivered. These payments were classified as normal business transactions but were actually structured diversions.
🏦
Fake bank balance concealed the extraction
To conceal the cash that had been siphoned out, Raju fabricated ₹7,136 Cr of "fixed deposits" on the balance sheet. The auditors (PwC) failed to independently verify these bank statements. The fake cash balance existed only on paper — the actual cash had already been tunnelled into the Raju family's real estate ventures.
📋
The 2009 Maytas acquisition — the moment of exposure
In December 2008, Satyam's board approved the acquisition of Maytas Properties and Maytas Infrastructure (both Raju family companies) for $1.6 billion. Institutional investors revolted — the stock fell 55% in a day. The board reversed the decision. Facing investigation, Raju confessed in January 2009. The Maytas acquisition was the planned final step: buy the real estate companies (funded by Satyam) at inflated prices, completing the tunnel.

🚨 Verdict — The Ultimate Tunnelling Case Study
Satyam teaches the hardest lesson: when promoters are determined to tunnel, even audited financial statements can be fabricated. The available signals — rising related-party transactions, large "advances" to group companies, low FCF/PAT ratio despite high reported profits, and cash balances earning suspiciously low interest — were all present in the filings. The lesson is to treat the Maytas acquisition attempt as a textbook example: when a listed company proposes buying promoter-owned assets at inflated prices, assume the worst until proven otherwise.
Example 04 · Complex Group RPT Structure · NSE: VEDL
Vedanta Limited
Vedanta demonstrates how complex multi-layer holding structures can create governance concerns — the UK-listed parent Vedanta Resources extracts dividends from India-listed Vedanta Limited to service its own debt, creating a systematic upward cash flow that directly affects minority shareholders' returns in the Indian entity.
Parent debt-funded dividends Vedanta Resources (UK) Royalty to promoter entity Complex holding structure
The RPT Concern Pattern
💸
Excessive dividends to fund parent company's debt
Vedanta Limited (India-listed) has periodically declared large special dividends that appear timed to help its UK parent Vedanta Resources service its own debt obligations. While dividends are legal and benefit all shareholders proportionally, the timing and scale — especially when declared during periods of the Indian company's own capital intensity — suggests the dividend policy is driven by parent needs rather than Indian minority shareholder interests.
🏢
Management and brand fees to promoter holding companies
Vedanta Limited has historically paid management and technical services fees to Vedanta Resources (parent) and other promoter entities. While some fee payments for genuine group services are normal, the amounts and terms require careful annual scrutiny — particularly whether the services rendered are demonstrably worth the fees charged and whether independent directors certify the arm's length pricing.
📋
What's different from Satyam — full disclosure
Unlike Satyam, Vedanta's RPTs are fully disclosed, board-approved, and reported in detail. The concern is not fraud — it is whether the scale and structure of cash outflows to the parent group (through dividends, fees, and guarantees) systematically disadvantages minority shareholders over time. This is the subtle, legal form of governance risk that requires ongoing monitoring rather than immediate exit.

⚠️ Verdict — Legal But Structurally Misaligned With Minority Interests
Vedanta's RPT structure illustrates the most common form of subtle tunnelling in India: using a listed subsidiary primarily as a cash cow for the parent's financial needs. No laws are broken, everything is disclosed, but the flow of value consistently runs upward to the promoter group rather than compounding within the listed entity for all shareholders. Investors should factor this structural misalignment into their valuation — Vedanta's earnings should trade at a discount to peers with cleaner governance structures precisely because of this ongoing outflow risk.
Example 05 · Clean with Brief Controversy · NSE: INFY
Infosys
Infosys maintains one of the cleanest RPT records among India's large-cap IT companies — essentially no material promoter-related RPTs, transparent governance, and a strong track record of returning capital to all shareholders equally. The brief 2017 controversy around founder allegations provides a useful lesson in distinguishing governance concerns from governance failures.
Near-zero promoter RPTs Full shareholder equality 2017 whistle-blower context Buybacks not extraction
RPT Record and 2017 Context
✅
Effectively zero promoter family RPTs
Unlike many Indian promoter-driven companies, Infosys has no material transactions with promoter family entities. The founding family (Murthy, Nilekani, etc.) hold shares and receive dividends like any other shareholder. They do not charge management fees, royalties, or technical service fees to the listed company. The promoter stake has declined over time through share sales, not extraction.
📋
The 2017 whistle-blower controversy — what actually happened
In 2017, Narayana Murthy (founder) publicly raised concerns about excessive compensation for the then-CEO Vishal Sikka and an acquisition (Panaya) done without adequate board scrutiny. This was NOT an RPT concern — it was a governance concern about CEO pay and acquisition process. The controversy ultimately led to Sikka's resignation. Importantly, no related-party tunnelling was alleged or found. The Panaya acquisition was later examined and a small write-down taken.
✅
Capital returned equally to all shareholders
Infosys' ₹27,000+ crore in buybacks were executed at fair prices, benefiting all shareholders proportionally. No differential treatment of promoters vs minority investors. Dividends paid uniformly. This equal treatment is the opposite of the Vedanta/Satyam patterns and represents the ideal alignment of promoter and minority shareholder interests.

💡 Verdict — The IT Sector Governance Benchmark
Infosys teaches a crucial distinction: governance concerns (executive compensation, acquisition discipline) are different from tunnelling risk (RPT extraction). The 2017 controversy was a governance debate — important but not an RPT red flag. On the RPT dimension specifically, Infosys remains a model: zero material promoter transactions, equal capital return to all shareholders, and strong independent board oversight. Investors should always separate "is there an RPT tunnelling risk?" from "is management making good strategic decisions?" — these are different questions requiring different analyses.
