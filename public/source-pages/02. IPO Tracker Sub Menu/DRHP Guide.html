Foundation

What Exactly is a DRHP?

Before diving into analysis, understand what you're reading and why SEBI mandates it.

Full Form
Draft Red Herring Prospectus

A DRHP is a preliminary registration document filed with SEBI before a company launches its IPO. It contains everything about the company — from its business model and financials to risk factors and how IPO proceeds will be used. It's called "Red Herring" because it's incomplete — the final price and issue size are added later.

📋
Why "Draft"?

SEBI reviews the DRHP and may ask the company to modify or add information before approving the final prospectus. The draft is public but not final.

🔴
Why "Red Herring"?

Historically, the cover page carried a red-ink warning: this is not a complete offer document. The price band, lot size, and dates are intentionally left blank.

⚖️
SEBI's Role

Every DRHP must be filed with SEBI at least 30 days before the IPO opens. SEBI doesn't "approve" the offering — it only ensures full disclosure.

📂
Where to Find It?

All DRHPs are publicly available on SEBI's EFILING portal (sebi.gov.in), NSE/BSE websites, and the company's own DRHP microsite. Free to download.

📏
Typical Size

Most DRHPs are 300–700 pages long. You don't need to read every page. Focus on 7–8 critical sections for a thorough investment thesis.

🕐
DRHP to IPO Timeline

SEBI takes 30–75 days to review. Once approved, the company has 12 months to open the IPO. Market conditions often drive the timing decision.

Deep Dive

10 Critical Sections You Must Analyse

Don't read 500 pages. Focus on these ten sections to build a complete investment thesis.

01
Objects of the Issue (Use of Proceeds)
▼

This is the most critical section. It tells you exactly why the company is raising money. Are they investing in growth, or are promoters cashing out via Offer for Sale (OFS)?

📌 Zomato Example: ₹4,197 Cr fresh issue for expansion, tech, marketing + ₹375 Cr OFS by promoters. Fresh issue is investor-friendly.
✔ Fresh issue for capex, R&D, or debt repayment = Positive
✘ Large OFS (promoters selling) = Proceed with caution
✘ Vague phrases like "general corporate purposes" = Red flag
02
Risk Factors Section
▼

Often ignored, risk factors are actually goldmines. The company legally must disclose ALL material risks — including ones competitors don't talk about. Read every word.

📌 Tip: Count risks related to "regulations," "key personnel dependency," and "litigation." More than 80 risk factors is a warning sign.
✘ Heavy reliance on one customer or geography
✘ Ongoing major litigations or CBI/SEBI investigations
✔ Risks clearly quantified (not just generic statements)
03
Promoter Background & Shareholding
▼

Who's behind the company matters as much as the business. Check promoter's track record, education, other business interests, criminal records, and post-IPO lock-in periods.

📌 Key metric: Promoter holding post-IPO. If below 40%, founders are diluting heavily — ask why.
✔ Promoter retaining 50%+ post-IPO = Skin in the game
✘ Promoter facing criminal charges or past SEBI violations
✘ Multiple pledged shares or cross-holding structures
04
Financial Statements (3 Years)
▼

SEBI mandates restated financials for the past 3 fiscal years. Look for revenue growth consistency, margin trends, debt levels, and working capital management.

📌 Zomato FY21: Revenue ₹1,994 Cr, Loss ₹816 Cr — loss-making but rapid revenue recovery post-COVID signalled strength.
✔ Consistent revenue growth (>20% CAGR for growth companies)
✘ Sudden revenue spike only in the IPO year
✘ High and rising debt-to-equity ratio
05
Management Discussion & Analysis (MD&A)
▼

MD&A is where management explains financials in their own words — what drove revenue, why margins changed, and their forward strategy. Compare with objective financial data.

📌 If management's explanation of margin expansion in MD&A doesn't match the numbers, something is wrong.
✔ Clear, detailed explanation of YoY changes
✘ Vague language for poor performance ("market conditions")
✘ MD&A contradicts audited financial figures
06
Industry Overview (IHR / CRISIL Report)
▼

Companies pay third-party research firms (CRISIL, Frost & Sullivan, RedSeer) to write the industry section. Understand the TAM, SAM, growth drivers, and competitive landscape.

📌 Zomato cited RedSeer data: India food delivery market to grow to $18.5B by 2025 — validating the long-term opportunity.
✔ Industry growing faster than the company's sector
✘ Very few independent data citations (too promotional)
✔ Company's market share clearly quantified and growing
07
Related Party Transactions
▼

This section reveals transactions between the company and its promoters, directors, or relatives. Excessive RPTs can indicate tunnelling of funds out of the listed entity.

📌 Watch for: loans given to promoter entities, purchases from promoter-owned vendors, or property rented from promoter at above-market rates.
✘ RPTs exceeding 10-15% of revenue = Major red flag
✘ Loans to sister companies without clear business rationale
✔ RPTs declining as company scales = Positive governance sign
08
Legal & Outstanding Litigations
▼

This section lists all pending cases against the company, promoters, or subsidiaries. Distinguish between routine business disputes and material ones that could impact operations.

📌 Always check total litigation exposure vs company's net worth. If exposure >25% of net worth, it's a material risk.
✘ SEBI enforcement orders or ED/CBI investigations
✘ Tax disputes pending for multiple years
✔ Routine labour or consumer cases = Low concern
09
Competitive Strengths & Strategy
▼

Every company says they have "strong brand," "experienced management," and "first mover advantage." Your job is to verify each claim against actual data in the financials.

📌 If a company claims "strong brand recognition" but their customer acquisition cost is rising, the claim is hollow.
✔ Each strength backed by a quantitative metric
✘ Strategy section doesn't explain how funds will be deployed
✔ Clear moat: patents, network effects, switching costs
10
Capital Structure & ESOP Pool
▼

Understand the fully diluted share count — including ESOPs and convertibles. IPO valuation metrics (PE, EV/EBITDA) should use fully diluted shares, not basic ones.

📌 Companies sometimes quote valuations on basic share count to appear cheaper. Always use fully diluted shares for calculations.
✔ ESOP grants tied to performance targets = Employee alignment
✘ Large convertible instruments not reflected in stated valuation
✘ >10% dilution from ESOPs can materially impact EPS

🔍 What Made LIC's DRHP Uniquely Complex
📊
Embedded Value (EV)

Unlike corporate P&L, insurance companies use Embedded Value — a metric combining net asset value + present value of future profits. LIC's EV was ₹5.4 lakh crore at filing.

🏛️
100% OFS Structure

Zero fresh capital raised — entire proceeds went to Government of India. This is the classic "promoter exit" scenario. No new funds entered the company from the IPO.

⚖️
Sovereign Guarantee Risk

DRHP disclosed that LIC policies carry an implicit government guarantee — but this may not automatically transfer to listed equity shareholders if solvency issues arise.

✅ Green Signals in LIC DRHP
▲ Absolute market dominance — 66% market share in new business premium, unmatched in India
▲ 13.5 lakh agent network = unbeatable distribution moat that private players can't replicate in decades
▲ Total Assets Under Management of ₹40.1 lakh crore — one of Asia's largest investment portfolios
▲ Policyholders' fund surplus: ₹5,54,266 crore — financial fortress with decades of premium collections
▲ Discounted pricing for policyholders & retail investors — ₹60 and ₹45 discount respectively
⚠️ Concerns in LIC DRHP
▼ 100% OFS — zero growth capital entered the company; IPO only monetized government stake
▼ Market timing was poor — IPO opened during Russia-Ukraine war selloff; Sensex fell 3,000+ points in IPO week
▼ Persistency ratios (policy renewal rates) below HDFC Life & SBI Life — signals higher lapses
▼ Heavy equity exposure (₹9.1 lakh crore in equities) creates NAV volatility tied to market cycles
▼ Government retains 96.5% post-IPO — low free float creates long-term liquidity concerns
💡
Key Learning from LIC vs Zomato DRHP

Zomato was a growth story — valued on future potential, loss-making but with a clear runway. LIC was a value story — profitable, dominant, but 100% OFS with no growth capital. Zomato listed at +53% premium; LIC listed at −8%. Both had strong DRHPs — but listing performance depends equally on valuation pricing and market timing, not just business quality. LIC eventually recovered and rewarded patient investors who read the DRHP correctly and understood the long-term thesis.

Balanced View

DRHP: Investor's Pros & Cons

Understanding the benefits and limitations of relying on a DRHP for investment decisions.

✅
Advantages
Why DRHP is an investor's best friend
📊
Complete Financial Transparency 3 years of audited financials — revenue, profits, debt, cash flow — all restated and auditor-certified. No better source of financial truth exists.
⚖️
Legally Mandated Disclosure Companies face criminal liability for material misstatements. The risk section must be comprehensive — companies can't hide major risks intentionally.
🆓
Completely Free & Public Available on SEBI EFILING, BSE/NSE sites, and company websites. No subscription or brokerage required to access primary research.
🔍
Proprietary Business Insights KPIs, unit economics, customer metrics — data competitors don't disclose publicly. Gives investors edge over those relying only on news.
🏛️
SEBI's 30-Day Review Regulators scrutinize and question anything unclear. Companies often revise their DRHP after SEBI observations — adding clarity and trust.
⚠️
Limitations
What a DRHP cannot tell you
🔮
No Price Band in DRHP The price is missing — arguably the most critical variable. Valuation analysis must wait for the RHP (Red Herring Prospectus) filed later.
📈
Only Historical Data Financial projections are not allowed in DRHP. Investors must extrapolate trends themselves — leaving room for error in fast-changing sectors.
📚
Highly Complex & Lengthy 300–700 pages of dense legal and financial text. Requires accounting knowledge to fully interpret. Not accessible to retail investors without guidance.
🎯
Promotional Bias in Key Sections Business description and competitive strengths are written by the company (via its investment bankers). Every company sounds like a world-beater.
⏱️
Can Be Stale by IPO Date Filed months before the IPO opens. Business conditions, market trends, or even financials may have changed significantly since filing.
Watch Out

Red Flags That Should Make You Pause

These warning signs in a DRHP have historically preceded poor listing performance or post-IPO value destruction.

🚨
Massive Offer for Sale

When 70%+ of the IPO is OFS, promoters are selling their stake. Fresh capital doesn't enter the company — investors fund an exit, not growth.

⚠️ Example: Paytm IPO — 97% OFS component. Post-listing crash of 70%+ followed.
📉
Declining Revenue in Base Year

If revenue in the most recent fiscal year (FY) is lower than the previous year, the company may have timed the IPO to coincide with peak valuations, not peak performance.

⚠️ Always compare CAGR over 3 years, not just the latest year's growth.
🔗
High Related Party Transactions

Recurring, large RPTs with promoter entities — especially loans, rent, or procurement — can indicate fund siphoning that dilutes shareholder value.

⚠️ Watch for transactions where "arm's length" pricing is claimed but never independently verified.
⚖️
Undisclosed Litigation Risk

Companies sometimes bury material legal cases as "routine" matters. Always calculate: total litigation exposure ÷ net worth. Above 25% is high.

⚠️ Check promoter personal legal cases too — not just company-level ones.
👔
Heavy Key-Person Risk

If one founder is responsible for 90% of business relationships and the company has no succession plan, investor capital is vulnerable to single-person decisions.

⚠️ Check management team depth. Is there a strong second-tier leadership?
✅
Green Signals to Look For

Not all DRHPs are red flags. Look for: institutional investors who invested at similar valuations, declining losses with improving cash burn rate, and strong debt-free balance sheets.

✔ Marquee PE/VC investors with recent funding rounds are validation signals.

⏱ 45 minutes · Financial Core

Financial Metrics

Key Ratios to Calculate from DRHP Data

These are the six most important ratios every retail investor should compute before applying to any IPO.

Price-to-Earnings (PE)
Important
PE = Issue Price ÷ EPS (Diluted)

Measures how many times earnings investors are paying per share. Must be compared vs sector average PE. Available only after price band is declared (RHP).

📊 Benchmark: IPO PE should be ≤ sector median for value; justify premium with higher growth
Return on Equity (ROE)
Critical
ROE = Net Profit ÷ Shareholders' Equity × 100

Shows how efficiently the company generates profit from shareholders' money. A rising ROE trend is more important than the absolute number at any single year.

📊 Benchmark: >15% ROE consistently for 3 years is healthy
Debt-to-Equity (D/E)
Critical
D/E = Total Debt ÷ Total Equity

Measures financial leverage. Capital-intensive sectors (infra, real estate) can sustain higher D/E. Tech and service companies with D/E >1 need scrutiny on debt servicing ability.

📊 Benchmark: <1 for tech/FMCG; <2 for manufacturing; <3 for infra
EV/EBITDA
Important
EV/EBITDA = Enterprise Value ÷ EBITDA

More comprehensive than PE — factors in debt. Crucial for capital-heavy businesses. For loss-making companies, EV/Revenue is used as a proxy until EBITDA turns positive.

📊 Benchmark: Compare with listed peers in same sector
Revenue CAGR (3Y)
Useful
CAGR = (Revenue_FY24 ÷ Revenue_FY21)^(1/3) - 1

Compound Annual Growth Rate of revenue over 3 years. A company growing revenue at 30%+ CAGR justifies a higher valuation multiple than sector peers growing at 10%.

📊 Benchmark: >20% for high-growth; >10% for stable businesses
Cash Flow from Operations (CFO)
Critical
CFO = Net Income + Non-cash Items ± Working Capital Changes

Profits can be manipulated; cash cannot (as easily). A company with positive and growing CFO despite accounting losses (as Zomato had) shows real operational strength.

📊 Benchmark: CFO should be positive and growing year-on-year
